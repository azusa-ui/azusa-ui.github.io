General Notes & Course Orientation

Course Orientation and Objectives

The course (GDT509, Public Health Policy and Ethics) is structured around two intertwined strands: health policy and public health ethics. By the end of the course, candidates are expected to be able to formulate issues in ethics and policy in public health, demonstrate the ability to formulate an ethically sound policy grounded in scientific evidence, and display ethical values in public health practice. The health policy strand covers the health policy framework (context, process, and actors), agenda setting in policy development, government and the policy process, policy implementation, research evidence and policy reform, and policy and health equity. The public health ethics strand covers the introduction to ethics in public health, ethics in healthcare services, ethics in epidemic management, ethics in health education and promotion, and ethics in public health research.

Assessment combines a group-based Ethical Policy Forum with an individually written short essay. For the Ethical Policy Forum, students are divided into subgroups (such as Ethics in Healthcare Service, Ethics in Epidemic Management, and Ethics in Health Education and Promotion), with members representing the policy proposer, relevant actors, implementers, advocates, and stakeholders for a selected policy. Each group must discuss the ethical justification of the selected policy (why it is ethical to implement) and the ethical considerations involved in implementing it (the concerns of stakeholders, advocates, and implementers). The companion short essay task asks students to discuss the main actors and their importance in developing a proposed policy, and to describe the stakeholders relevant to that policy.

  • Course learning outcomes: formulate ethics and policy issues; formulate an evidence-based, ethically sound policy; display ethical values in practice.
  • Two integrated strands: Health Policy and Public Health Ethics.
  • Assessment: group Ethical Policy Forum (policy actors, justification, implementation concerns) plus an individual short essay.

Key Definitions: Policy, Public Policy, Health Policy, and Ethics

Policy is commonly defined as decisions taken by those with responsibility for a particular policy area, whether in health, education, trade, or another domain. Those who make such decisions are termed policy makers, and a specific, highly placed subset with privileged access to other elites is sometimes called the policy elite. Public policy, in turn, refers specifically to policy made by the state or government. Thomas Dye's classic formulation holds that public policy is whatever governments choose to do or not to do, meaning that a deliberate failure to act on an issue is itself a form of policy. Policy need not arise from a single decision; it may consist of bundles of decisions, sometimes bundled together as a programme, that cumulatively produce a broad course of action over time.

Health policy embraces courses of action, and inaction, that affect the institutions, organisations, services, and funding arrangements of the health system, spanning both the public and private sectors. Because health is shaped by determinants that lie outside the formal health system (for example the food, tobacco, or pharmaceutical industries), health policy analysts must also attend to the actions of organisations external to health care that nonetheless influence health outcomes.

Ethics, meanwhile, refers to the set of moral principles that guide and govern individual behaviour and the conduct of activities, providing a framework for distinguishing right from wrong. As a formal branch of knowledge, ethics (or moral philosophy) systematically explores morality, values, and the principles underlying human conduct, developing theories that help resolve moral dilemmas.

  • Policy: decisions by those with responsibility for a policy area; may be a bundle of decisions rather than a single act (Dye, 2001).
  • Health policy: courses of action and inaction affecting institutions, services, and funding of the health system, public and private.
  • Ethics: moral principles governing behaviour, and the academic discipline (moral philosophy) that studies them.

The Health Policy Triangle and the Policy Cycle

The Health Policy Triangle is a widely used analytic framework comprising four interconnected components: actors, process, context, and content. It highlights that health policy is unusually consequential compared with other areas of social policy because it frequently involves matters of life and death, and it offers a systematic map for exploring the complexity of policy development and implementation rather than a rigid, linear model.

Actors are the individuals, groups, and organisations involved in formulating, implementing, and evaluating policy; these range from government bodies and bureaucrats to non-governmental organisations, international bodies such as the World Health Organization, and local community leaders. Actors' influence is shaped by power, which can derive from wealth, expertise, official position, or control of information, and by the interplay between individual agency and organisational structure.

Process refers to the stages of the policymaking cycle: problem identification and issue recognition, policy formulation, implementation, and evaluation, understood as an iterative rather than strictly linear sequence, often accompanied by advocacy and lobbying, and occurring at local, national, or international levels.

Context denotes the systemic political, economic, social, cultural, and international conditions within which policy is made. Context is commonly divided into situational factors (transient "focusing events" such as a disease outbreak), structural factors (relatively stable features such as the political system or demography), cultural factors (values and beliefs shaping acceptability of interventions), and international factors (global interdependence and cross-border health threats).

Content is the substance of the policy itself: its goals and objectives, the strategies and interventions it specifies, the regulations and guidelines it establishes, its resource allocation, timeframes and milestones, mechanisms for flexibility, communication strategies, and legal and ethical safeguards.

The related policy cycle model breaks the process into six recognisable stages: agenda setting (an issue is identified and elevated to political attention), policy formulation (proposals and options are developed), policy adoption (a proposal is formally approved by decision-making bodies), policy implementation (the adopted policy is translated into programmes and resource allocation), policy evaluation (effectiveness and outcomes are assessed against objectives), and support or maintenance (ongoing monitoring, adjustment, and sustaining of political and public backing).

  • Health Policy Triangle: actors, process, context, content (Walt and Gilson framework, as used in Making Health Policy).
  • Context sub-types: situational, structural, cultural, international.
  • Policy cycle stages: agenda setting, formulation, adoption, implementation, evaluation, support/maintenance.

Power, Agenda Setting, and Political Systems

Understanding who holds power in the policy process is central to health policy analysis. Power can be exercised in three dimensions. Power as decision making is the ability to shape which policies are adopted, modified, or rejected, and is closely tied to the distribution of resources such as wealth, status, and access to information (drawing on Robert Dahl's pluralist account). Power as non-decision making, associated with Bachrach and Baratz (1962), describes the ability of powerful actors to keep issues off the policy agenda altogether by manipulating dominant values and framing what counts as legitimate debate. Power as thought control, attributed to Steven Lukes (1974), is considered the most insidious dimension because it shapes people's underlying perceptions, preferences, and beliefs so that they do not object to arrangements against their own interests; this operates through control of information, media narratives, and socialisation, and is closely related to the idea of "soft power."

Theories of who holds power in society include pluralism (power is dispersed among competing interest groups and the state acts as a neutral arbiter, though critics question this neutrality and note unequal access for marginalised groups), public choice theory (politicians and bureaucrats are modelled as rational, self-interested utility maximisers, giving rise to concepts such as rent-seeking and regulatory "capture"), and elitism (power is concentrated in a small, privileged minority whose interests and values disproportionately shape policy, tending toward incremental rather than radical change).

Two influential agenda-setting frameworks explain how issues reach the formal government agenda. The Hall model holds that an issue's odds of reaching, and succeeding on, the policy agenda rise when it has high legitimacy (seen as appropriate for government action), high feasibility (technically and administratively achievable), and high support (from key interest groups). The Kingdon model describes policy emerging from the convergence of three independent streams: the problem stream (how issues come to be perceived as requiring government action, through indicators, feedback, or focusing events), the politics stream (the activity of policy entrepreneurs, shifts in national mood, and interest group campaigns), and the policy stream (the ongoing development and refinement of technical solutions). When the three streams align, a policy window opens, creating a favourable moment for an issue to move onto the agenda, as illustrated by the way the COVID-19 pandemic converged all three streams simultaneously.

Health systems also operate within broader political regime types, each with characteristic implications for health policy: liberal democratic regimes (participatory, with health policy ranging from market-oriented to welfare-state models); egalitarian-authoritarian regimes (strong state provision aimed at universal access, historically illustrated by countries such as the former Soviet Union); traditional-inegalitarian regimes (hierarchical, monarchical systems with private-sector-dominated, unequal access to care); populist regimes (dominant-party systems that often frame healthcare as a nationalist "for all" entitlement); and authoritarian-inegalitarian regimes (narrow elite-centric health provision, often linked to military governance).

  • Three dimensions of power: decision making, non-decision making, thought control (Dahl; Bachrach and Baratz; Lukes).
  • Theories of power distribution: pluralism, public choice theory, elitism.
  • Agenda-setting models: Hall (legitimacy, feasibility, support) and Kingdon (problem, politics, policy streams; policy windows).

Applied Case Examples in Malaysian Health Policy

Exam-style case discussions used in the course apply these frameworks to concrete Malaysian and comparative policy scenarios, illustrating how the abstract concepts translate into practical ethical and policy reasoning. A recurring example is the national smoking-ban policy (smoke-free enclosed public facilities from 2010, extended to all restaurant and food-premises areas by 2019), which is used to illustrate the least coercive means principle (starting with public awareness campaigns, signage, and cessation support before resorting to enforcement) alongside the reciprocity principle (emphasising the shared benefit of a smoke-free environment as a basis for voluntary compliance, and society's corresponding obligation to support smokers attempting to quit).

A second recurring scenario concerns the extension of communicable disease quarantine policy to contract workers, used to justify the application of justice, beneficence, utility, respect, liberty, reciprocity, and solidarity principles to outbreak management, and to weigh individual liberty restrictions against the collective benefit of disease containment.

A third scenario, on special budget allocation for treatment of rare diseases (using eligibility criteria such as a disease affecting fewer than 1 in 4,000 people, being a confirmed and severe condition, and requiring approval by a national advisory committee), is used to explore distributive justice and resource allocation dilemmas in funding low-prevalence, high-cost conditions, sometimes discussed in relation to the "rule of rescue" tension between aggregate welfare and the ethical pull to assist identifiable, severely affected individuals.

A further scenario, on new working-condition policies for housemen (restricting inter-departmental referrals to more experienced medical officers, mandating informed consent procedures, and capping working hours), is used as a worked example of applying the full policy cycle and the policy analysis triangle (content, context, process, actors) to a real institutional reform, situating the policy within its social, professional, legal, and ethical context.

  • Smoking-ban policy: illustrates least coercive means and reciprocity.
  • Quarantine extension to contract workers: illustrates justice, beneficence, utility, respect, liberty, reciprocity, and solidarity principles in outbreak management.
  • Rare-disease budget allocation: illustrates distributive justice and resource allocation trade-offs.
  • Houseman working-conditions policy: worked example of the policy cycle and policy analysis triangle.

Introduction to Public Health Ethics

Distinguishing Public Health Ethics from Clinical Ethics

Public health ethics and clinical (medical) ethics both descend from the broader fields of bioethics and applied ethics, but they diverge sharply in scope and orientation. Clinical ethics concerns the relationship between an individual healthcare provider and an individual patient, and is conventionally organised around four pillars: autonomy (the patient's right to make decisions about their own care, including the right to refuse treatment), non-maleficence (the duty to do no harm), beneficence (the duty to act for the patient's best interests), and justice (equal care for all patients). Public health ethics, by contrast, is concerned with the health and well-being of populations, and is organised around a different set of emphases: interdependence (the recognition that individual actions can affect the health of others, as in a decision to vaccinate during an outbreak), participation (the requirement that public health decisions involve input from the communities they affect), and reliance on scientific evidence rather than personal belief or conjecture as the basis for intervention.

Public health doctors and clinicians therefore approach the same underlying problem differently: a clinician looks after an individual patient, taking a history, performing an examination, forming a differential diagnosis, and treating the person in front of them; a public health doctor looks after a population, working with community-level data, forming a "community diagnosis," and directing preventive advice and healthy-lifestyle promotion even toward people who are not (yet) sick. An individual's health is understood as tied to their life within the community, in contrast to the individual-patient focus of clinical care.

  • Public health ethics field: interdependence, participation, reliance on scientific evidence.
  • Clinical ethics field (four pillars): autonomy, non-maleficence, beneficence, justice.
  • Public health ethics sits alongside bioethics, medical ethics, health research ethics, and health care ethics as related but distinct applied ethics domains.

Principles of the Ethical Practice of Public Health (Code of Ethics)

The Principles of the Ethical Practice of Public Health were promulgated by the Public Health Leadership Society in 2002 and adopted by the American Public Health Association the same year, as a living document intended to guide the profession. The code comprises twelve principles: public health should address the fundamental causes of disease and the requirements for health, aiming to prevent adverse outcomes; it should achieve community health in a way that respects individual rights; policies, programmes, and priorities should be developed and evaluated through processes that allow community input; public health should advocate for the empowerment of disenfranchised community members so that the basic resources for health are accessible to all; it should seek the information needed to implement effective policies and programmes; institutions should provide communities with the information needed for decisions and obtain community consent for implementation; institutions should act in a timely manner within their resources and mandate; programmes should incorporate approaches that respect diverse values, beliefs, and cultures; programmes should be implemented in ways that most enhance the physical and social environment; institutions should protect the confidentiality of information that could cause harm if disclosed, with exceptions justified only by a high likelihood of significant harm; institutions should ensure the professional competence of their employees; and institutions and employees should collaborate in ways that build public trust and institutional effectiveness.

  • Twelve principles, PHLS (2002), adopted by APHA (2002); available as a living document.
  • Recurrent themes: addressing root causes, respecting rights, community consent and participation, cultural sensitivity, confidentiality, professional competence, and trust-building.

Upshur's Four Principles for Justifying Public Health Intervention

Building on Mill's harm principle, R.E.G. Upshur (2002) proposed four ethical foundations that are traditionally implicit in public health practice and that together help build and maintain trust between the public health sector and the public it serves. The harm principle holds that the only purpose for which power can rightfully be exercised over an individual against their will is to prevent harm to others; a person's own good, physical or moral, is not sufficient warrant for coercion. In assessing an intervention against this principle, one asks whether the action prevents harm to, or improves the well-being of, individuals or groups other than those being restricted.

The transparency principle requires that all legitimate stakeholders participate in the decision-making process, have equal input into deliberations, and that the process be as clear and accountable as possible, free from domination by any one group or from undue political interference. The reciprocity principle, captured in the maxim "do unto others as you would have them do unto you," holds that when public health action imposes ethical duties or burdens on individuals or groups, society is obliged to help or compensate them, and to facilitate their ability to discharge those duties, rather than placing undue or unreasonable burdens on them. Finally, the principle of least restrictive or coercive means requires that public health goals be pursued through the mildest effective intervention: more coercive methods should be used only once less coercive methods (education, facilitation, and discussion) have failed, and any measures applied must be free of discrimination.

  • Harm principle: intervention justified only to prevent harm to others (Mill, 1859).
  • Transparency: inclusive, accountable, non-dominated decision making.
  • Reciprocity: burdens imposed on individuals should be compensated or facilitated by society.
  • Least restrictive or coercive means: escalate from education toward coercion only as needed, applied without discrimination.

Childress's Criteria and the Siracusa Principles

A complementary framework, Childress's public health ethics framework, sets out five criteria for judging whether a public health intervention is ethically sound. Effectiveness requires clear evidence that the intervention will actually improve health outcomes; without such evidence, the strategy cannot be ethically justified. Proportionality requires that the benefits of an intervention outweigh its potential harms, so that measures are not excessively burdensome relative to the health gains expected. Necessity requires that no less morally troubling alternative exists that could achieve the same result. Least infringement requires that, even once the first three conditions are satisfied, the degree of infringement on individual rights and freedoms be minimised. Public justification requires that public health authorities offer clear, transparent, and open communication explaining why an intervention is necessary and how it aligns with ethical principles.

The Siracusa Principles offer a related, internationally agreed legal framework for evaluating restrictions on civil liberties in emergency situations. Restrictions must be legal (carried out in accordance with law), legitimate (evidence-based and justified in the interest of others), and must use the least restrictive means reasonably available, with no discrimination in their application (not imposed arbitrarily, unreasonably, or in a discriminatory manner). Related to these frameworks is the distinction between procedural justice (fairness of the process by which decisions are made, including due process, transparency, inclusiveness, accountability, and oversight) and distributive justice (the fair allocation of health and healthcare outcomes in society), both of which are relevant when evaluating public health policy and health technology assessment decisions.

  • Childress's five criteria: effectiveness, proportionality, necessity, least infringement, public justification.
  • Siracusa Principles: legal, legitimate, least restrictive, non-discriminatory.
  • Procedural justice (fair process) versus distributive justice (fair allocation of outcomes).

An Ethical Framework for Analysing Public Health Programmes

A simplified ethical framework for appraising a proposed public health programme poses six sequential questions: does the proposed programme have clearly stated goals; is the programme actually effective in achieving those stated goals; are the potential burdens of the programme known; are alternative approaches available that could minimise those burdens; will the programme be implemented fairly; and how can the benefits and burdens of the programme be fairly balanced? This questioning sequence operationalises the more abstract principles above (harm, reciprocity, transparency, least restrictive means, proportionality, and necessity) into a practical checklist that can be applied when justifying a specific intervention, and mirrors the broader analytic move from goals, to relevant facts and uncertainties, to implications for the health and rights of affected individuals and communities, to fit with core public health values.

  • Does the programme have goals, and is it effective in achieving them?
  • Are burdens known, and are less burdensome alternatives available?
  • Is implementation fair, and are benefits and burdens fairly balanced?

Philosophy of Policy & Ethics in Public Health

Foundations: What Is Public Health, Policy, and Ethics

Public health ethics has evolved out of the broader fields of medical ethics and bioethics. Medical ethics traditionally addresses the relationship between healthcare professionals and individual patients, centred on issues such as autonomy, informed consent, and the duty to do no harm; bioethics extends this to ethical questions raised by advances in biotechnology and the life sciences, such as genetic engineering and organ transplantation. Public health ethics differs from both, and also from research ethics (which concerns the ethical conduct of research on individual participants), by broadening its perspective to the ethical implications of population-level interventions, policies, and programmes.

Public health itself is understood as both a science and an art: the science and art of preventing disease, prolonging life, and promoting health through the organised efforts and informed choices of society, organisations (public and private), communities, and individuals. This definition foregrounds several recurring tensions explored throughout the module, above all the tension between the rights and needs of the community and those of the individual.

  • Public health ethics sits downstream of medical ethics and bioethics, but is distinguished by its population-level (rather than individual-level) focus.
  • Public health is defined as the organised, evidence-informed effort of society to prevent disease, prolong life, and promote health.
  • The central recurring issue: balancing the rights and needs of the community against those of the individual.

Moral Philosophy Foundations: Consequentialism, Deontology, Principlism, and Virtue Ethics

Public health ethics draws on two broad branches of philosophy: moral philosophy and political philosophy. Within moral philosophy, consequentialist theories evaluate the morality of an action by its outcomes. Utilitarianism, the dominant consequentialist theory, holds that the right action is the one that maximises overall benefit; applied naively to public health, an intervention is considered fully justified simply because it produces benefit by protecting and promoting population health, though this "naive utilitarian" framing has been challenged for glossing over distributional and rights-based concerns.

Non-consequentialist theories evaluate an action by its inherent nature and the principles or rules governing it, independent of outcomes. Deontological theories, associated with Immanuel Kant, hold that some actions are inherently right or wrong regardless of consequences, and that people should never be treated merely as a means to an end; a deontological objection to a public health intervention rests on the nature of the intervention itself, not on whether it produces good outcomes. Principlism (the "four principles" approach of Beauchamp and Childress, 1979: respect for autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice) has come to dominate ethical theory and practice in clinical medicine and biomedical research, though it is not always the most suitable lens for public health, where population-level trade-offs often sit uneasily with an individually oriented principles framework. Virtue ethics, traced to Aristotle, holds that morality is a matter of character: right action is that taken by a moral agent who has acquired virtues such as compassion, truthfulness, prudence, integrity, and trustworthiness, and who exercises them appropriately; this is often considered the most naturally fitting philosophical foundation for public health practice, given the field's collective and civic orientation.

  • Consequentialism/utilitarianism: judges actions by outcomes; maximise aggregate benefit.
  • Deontology: judges actions by their inherent nature/rules, regardless of consequences (Kant).
  • Principlism: autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice (Beauchamp and Childress, 1979).
  • Virtue ethics: morality as character and the exercise of virtues (Aristotle).

Political Philosophy Foundations: Liberalism, Libertarian Paternalism, and Communitarianism

Political philosophy examines fundamental questions about the state, government, power, justice, and the relationship between individuals and the state, and provides a second lens for evaluating public health interventions. The liberal objection, rooted in Mill's harm principle, holds that public health interventions are problematic to the extent that they infringe on individual freedom; legitimate state power, on this view, may be exercised over an individual against their will only to prevent harm to others. Libertarian paternalism, associated with the concept of "nudging," attempts to steer people's choices in beneficial directions (for example through default options or environmental design) while preserving their formal freedom to choose otherwise, positioning itself as a middle path between coercive regulation and pure non-intervention. Communitarianism, by contrast, emphasises the value of community, shared goods, and social obligation as legitimate grounds for public health action, pushing back against a purely individual-rights-centred framing of policy questions.

These moral and political frameworks function as guides rather than rigid formulas: applying them to a real intervention means engaging with the underlying idea of a theory (for example, the reasoning behind the harm principle or behind virtue-based professional conduct) rather than citing the theory as a direct proof. Other frameworks, beyond moral and political philosophy, are also needed for particular domains such as healthcare resource allocation.

  • Liberalism/harm principle: interventions are suspect insofar as they infringe individual freedom without preventing harm to others.
  • Libertarian paternalism: nudges that preserve choice while steering behaviour.
  • Communitarianism: legitimises action grounded in shared community goods and obligations.

Historical Foundations: Codes, Declarations, and the Rights Basis of Public Health

Several historical documents ground the ethical and rights-based dimension of public health practice. The Nuremberg Code (1947), developed after the trials of Nazi war criminals whose medical scientists had used humans as experimental subjects, set out ten points for the ethical conduct of human experimentation, the first and most enduring of which is that the voluntary and fully informed consent of research participants is absolutely essential. The following year, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) was adopted by the United Nations General Assembly; Article 25 establishes that everyone has the right to a standard of living adequate for health and well-being, including food, clothing, housing, medical care, and necessary social services, providing an important reference point for the obligations of public health professionals in realising the right to health. The Declaration of Helsinki (1964 and subsequent revisions), issued by the World Medical Association, extended these protections specifically to the conduct of medical experiments on patients.

Together, these instruments, along with the Beauchamp and Childress principlist framework discussed above, form the backbone of contemporary research ethics and inform, while not always perfectly fitting, the population-level reasoning distinctive of public health ethics.

  • Nuremberg Code (1947): voluntary informed consent as an absolute prerequisite for human experimentation.
  • Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Article 25 (1948): health as a basic right.
  • Declaration of Helsinki (1964): protection of patients from harm in medical research.

Applied Dilemmas: Quarantine, Screening, Precaution, and Triage

The philosophical frameworks above are tested against recurring practical dilemmas. Quarantine and individual rights are illustrated by well-documented cases in which individuals with suspected drug-resistant tuberculosis were detained and had confidential medical information disclosed publicly in the interest of preventing transmission, raising unresolved tension between the right to privacy and the community's right to protection, particularly when decisions must be made under diagnostic uncertainty. Mass screening raises related dilemmas, illustrated by fever screening during the 2009 H1N1 influenza pandemic: because fever is a highly non-specific marker (affected by medication, environment, and individual physiology), mass screening produced large numbers of false positives and false negatives, leading to extensive but largely ineffective quarantine of travellers, and raising further questions about the fair prioritisation of scarce vaccine supply among health workers, high-risk patients, and the general public.

The precautionary principle, prominent in environmental and public health policy, holds that the absence of full scientific certainty should not be used to postpone cost-effective measures to prevent harm; the historical debate over water fluoridation illustrates how public opinion and political controversy, rather than scientific evidence alone, have sometimes driven precautionary or anti-fluoridation decisions. Triage in public health emergencies represents a further departure from standard biomedical ethics: rather than treating each patient according to individual need, disaster triage allocates scarce medical resources to maximise the number of lives saved, a population-level utilitarian logic that stands in tension with the individually oriented norms of clinical ethics while remaining anchored in the goals of protecting life, respecting rights, and promoting the common good.

Other applied areas discussed include newborn screening (where the case for mandatory versus voluntary consent turns on whether a detected condition is treatable, and on the value, or burden, of knowing about an untreatable condition), obesity and personal agency (where the causes of obesity are heterogeneous, involving genetics, environment, and constrained food choices, complicating simple appeals to individual responsibility and "dieting" as a policy solution), and vaccine trial design (where the ethical principle of clinical equipoise, requiring genuine uncertainty about relative benefit before a placebo-controlled trial is justified, interacts in complex ways with public health goals and fairness when testing reduced-dose vaccination schedules in resource-limited settings).

  • Quarantine cases: tension between individual privacy/liberty and community protection under diagnostic uncertainty.
  • Mass fever screening (H1N1): false positive/negative trade-offs and vaccine-prioritisation dilemmas.
  • Precautionary principle: acting despite scientific uncertainty (illustrated by water fluoridation debates).
  • Disaster triage: population-level utilitarian allocation of scarce resources, in tension with individual-patient ethics.
  • Newborn screening, obesity and agency, and vaccine trial equipoise as further applied case domains.

Law, Paternalism, and Social Capital as Ethical Instruments

Beyond moral theory, public health relies on legal and social mechanisms to give ethical principles practical effect. Law as a tool of social control operates through public health legislation (statutory powers such as notification of disease, quarantine, and inspection), through the criminal law (used more rarely, and controversially, for instance in prosecutions related to onward disease transmission), and indirectly by shaping social norms of behaviour over time, as seen in shifting public attitudes toward smoking following smoke-free legislation. Law also functions as a protector of individual rights, for example through the law of tort (enabling individuals whose rights have been infringed to seek redress) and through human rights legislation, which allows public health powers to be challenged where they are exercised inappropriately.

Paternalism, understood as interference with a person's choices justified by an appeal to that person's own good, is a recurring concern in product safety regulation and health promotion; its moral status depends on whether the "good" being invoked is genuinely the affected person's own good, on the degree of interference with liberty, and on whether less restrictive alternatives were available. Social capital, comprising the networks, norms, and generalised trust and reciprocity that enable people to act collectively for mutual benefit, has increasingly been treated as an ethical concept in its own right in public health scholarship: because social capital and the trust it generates are held to enable other morally valuable ends (such as cooperation with public health measures and collective action on shared threats), some scholars argue that fostering social capital constitutes an obligation for public health actors, subject to the usual caveat that "ought implies can."

  • Law's roles: statutory public health powers, criminal law, indirect shaping of social norms, and protection of individual rights (tort, human rights legislation).
  • Paternalism: interference justified by appeal to the affected person's own good; moral status depends on degree of interference and availability of less restrictive means.
  • Social capital: trust, networks, and generalised reciprocity, increasingly framed as an ethical concept that enables collective public health action.

Ethics in Health Care Services

Scope of Clinical and Health Care Ethics

Health care services encompass any medical or remedial care recognized under law, including the supplies delivered in connection with that care. They serve patients, families, communities and populations across the continuum of emergency, preventive, rehabilitative, long-term, hospital, diagnostic, primary, palliative and home care. Within this continuum, the health system delivers four broad categories of service: health promotion, disease prevention, diagnosis and treatment, and rehabilitation. Clinical ethics, however, is not confined to definitional questions about the beginning or ending of life, or to abstract notions of quality of life. Day-to-day ethical practice is instead grounded in respecting people, treating them with dignity, treating them fairly, and supporting their choices.

This framing is reinforced by Sustainable Development Goal 3 (Good Health and Wellbeing), which emphasizes the attainment of Universal Health Coverage. The underlying aim is to ensure that all people and communities can use the promotive, preventive, curative, rehabilitative and palliative health services they need, of sufficient quality to be effective, without exposing the user to financial hardship. In Malaysia, this ethical orientation is reflected in the National Policy for Quality in Healthcare (NPQHC), which can be examined through the lens of agenda setting (what the policy's agenda is), the actors involved, the evidence and data driving it, and how it is implemented.

  • Health care ethics is expressed through everyday interactions, not only extraordinary end-of-life dilemmas.
  • Four service domains: health promotion, disease prevention, diagnosis and treatment, rehabilitation.
  • UHC underpins the ethical aim of accessible, quality care without financial hardship.

Key readings: National Policy for Quality in Healthcare (NPQHC) full version; A Code of Ethics for Public Health.

Duty of Care

The Declaration of Geneva states that the health and wellbeing of the patient will be the physician's first consideration, but professional guidance (such as that of the Medical Council of New Zealand) recognizes that this duty must be balanced against competing duties: the need to use resources efficiently, the requirement for care to be consistent with good evidence-based practice, and the obligation to weigh the duty owed to an individual patient against the duty owed to the community and wider populations. Having a duty of care means being responsible for the health, safety and wellbeing of others; this is both a moral and a legal obligation. The responsibility can be visualized as layered commitments: welfare (protecting people from harm, abuse and injury), good practice (an effective and proven approach to care), compliance (following regulations and guidelines, clinical practice guidelines, manuals and legislation), wellbeing (promoted wherever possible), and health education and promotion (improving health literacy). The NPQHC in Malaysia was itself developed partly as an expression of duty-of-care considerations, translating this ethical obligation into ensuring competent providers and accessible care.

Duty of care becomes ethically contested at the bedside when health care professionals and patients or families disagree, particularly around the shift from curative to palliative intent. Decisions about when to move from active, curative treatment to palliative and comfort-focused care require balancing respect for patient autonomy against the principles of beneficence and non-maleficence (the harm principle). A related and less frequently articulated tension is the quality of life of the caregiver measured against the quality of life of the patient: caregiver burden is well documented, raising the question of whether health care should prioritize prolonging survival in vegetative or terminally ill patients, or give weight to the wellbeing of the family or caregiver providing that care.

  • Duty of care is a moral and legal obligation, not merely a professional courtesy.
  • Balancing individual patient duty against duty to the community and wider population is central to public health-oriented clinical ethics.
  • Curative-to-palliative transitions and caregiver burden are recurring sites of ethical tension.

Key readings: The Duty of Care of Healthcare Professionals; Ethical Criteria for Allocating Health-care Resources.

Informed Consent and Decision-Making Capacity

Informed consent in practice is complicated by poor health literacy, questions of decision-making capacity, the use of proxy decision-makers, and the pressures of decision-making in emergency situations. A patient's medical decision-making capacity is generally assessed against four elements: the patient must be able to demonstrate understanding of the situation, including the benefits, risks and alternatives of a proposed treatment or intervention (including the option of no treatment); demonstrate appreciation of the consequences of the decision; show reasoning in the thought process used to reach the decision; and communicate a choice, preference or wish.

A complementary structured approach to handling difficult conversations and decisions in clinical ethics uses the mnemonic M-E-R-C-I: Medical (understanding the clinical condition of the patient), Empathy (how the patient and family members feel, and what their worries are), Right and Respect (the patient's right to the best available care and to be treated with respect), Communication (who should be informed, what and how much should be disclosed, how and when), and Insight (a holistic appreciation of how illness affects the patient and significant others, including financial, job-related, caregiving and logistical burdens such as follow-up visits and living arrangements). The Jonsen four-quadrants approach (medical indications, patient preferences, quality of life, and contextual features) offers a further structured method for ethical decision-making in individual clinical cases, alongside procedural safeguards such as the registration of medico-legal cases referred for expert management or advice.

  • Four elements of decision-making capacity: understanding, appreciation, reasoning, communication of choice.
  • The MERCI framework structures difficult clinical conversations around medical facts, empathy, rights and respect, communication, and insight.
  • Jonsen's four quadrants provide a structured framework for case-based ethical decision-making.

Key readings: Ethics in Health Care Services (lecture note); Top 10 health care ethics challenges facing the public, views of Toronto bioethicists.

Resource Allocation and Waiting Lists

Resource allocation in health care raises questions of scarcity: the funding distribution to marginalized populations, chronic care, elderly and mentally ill patients and rehabilitation services; the geographic distribution of health care providers and specialties; and the balancing of harm against benefit at both the individual and population level. Increasing demand for health care services generates waiting lists whose consequences include compromised health status and outcomes, impeded ability to return to normal functioning at work and home, and psychological distress. Waiting lists can also reflect inappropriate use of scarce resources, such as acute care beds being used for long-term care patients, or intensive care unit beds being used for chronic care patients, and can expose geographical inequities in accessibility across regions.

The ethical criteria used to justify allocation decisions extend beyond ability to pay, which remains the dominant real-world determinant of access to health care globally despite being ethically contested. Formal frameworks for allocating scarce resources, such as organs or vaccines during a shortage, weigh criteria including expected benefit, priority to the worst off, and equal treatment of equal cases, always situated against the background reality that many health systems, including in low-resource settings, face critical shortages of doctors, nurses and midwives.

  • Resource allocation decisions must balance individual patient benefit against population-level equity.
  • Waiting lists illustrate how scarcity translates into ethical harm through delay, misallocation and geographic inequity.
  • Ability to pay remains the dominant global allocation mechanism, despite competing ethical frameworks proposing fairer criteria.

Key readings: Ethical Criteria for Allocating Health-care Resources; Ethics in Health Care Services Part 1 and Part 2 (slides).

The Upshur Principles and Public Health Justification

Where the traditional four principles of biomedical ethics (autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice) prove less useful for justifying actions that affect populations rather than individual patients, the Upshur principles offer an alternative framework developed specifically to justify public health interventions. The harm principle establishes that a government or authority may restrict individual liberty when necessary to prevent harm to others, drawing on the classical liberal formulation that the only legitimate purpose for exercising power over an individual against their will is to prevent harm to others. The least restrictive or coercive means principle recognizes that while a range of means exist to achieve public health ends, the full force of state authority should be reserved for exceptional circumstances, with more coercive methods used only once less coercive methods have failed. The reciprocity principle holds that once public health action is warranted, the responsible social entity has an obligation to assist individuals or communities in discharging the resulting duties. The transparency principle requires that legitimate stakeholders be involved in decision-making, have equal input into deliberations, and that the decision-making process be as clear and accountable as possible.

These four principles were illustrated in the context of assessing medical fitness to drive: the harm principle justifies restricting an individual's driving license because of the risk posed to others, independent of whether the affected individual has insight into that risk; the least restrictive means principle guides how the discussion with the patient should be built up gradually before escalating to a licensing authority; reciprocity requires attention to how the loss of a privilege such as driving affects a person's life and obliges the profession or system to help identify alternatives; and transparency requires honesty about the reasons a medical condition affects safety and the seeking of consent before disclosing concerns to others.

  • The Upshur principles were developed because straightforward application of autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice does not map neatly onto population-level concerns.
  • Four principles: harm, least restrictive or coercive means, reciprocity, and transparency.
  • Applicable to public health interventions ranging from infectious disease control to individual case decisions with population safety implications, such as fitness to drive.

Key readings: Using the Upshur Principles to Discuss Medical Fitness to Drive.

Codes of Ethics and the Shift Toward Value-Based Care

Public health institutions carry an inherently moral mandate to protect the health of the public, which in turn implies the possession of a degree of power that must be exercised responsibly. Formal codes of ethics for public health, such as the twelve principles adopted by public health institutions, articulate obligations including addressing the fundamental causes of disease, respecting the rights of individuals while pursuing community health, ensuring opportunities for community input into policies and programs, advocating for disenfranchised community members, seeking and acting on needed information in a timely manner, respecting diverse values and cultures, protecting confidentiality except where disclosure is justified by risk of significant harm, ensuring the professional competence of employees, and engaging in collaborations that build public trust.

This ethical architecture underpins the broader shift in health care delivery from a paternalistic model, through patient-centred care, toward value-based care, in which duty of care to the individual is explicitly balanced against duty of care to the community and wider population, resource efficiency, and evidence-based practice. Related and emerging dimensions of this shift include technology-enhanced health care delivery, including the use of artificial intelligence in health care, which introduces new ethical questions about safety, accountability and equitable access that sit alongside the traditional pillars of duty of care, patient safety and resource allocation. In public health specifically, the ethical orientation reframes the object of duty of care: the "patient" becomes the community, so that the traditional first-person clinical ethic extends outward to a collective, population-level ethic of wellness rather than illness.

  • Codes of ethics translate the moral mandate of public health institutions into concrete, actionable principles.
  • Value-based care represents an ethical evolution from paternalism, through patient-centred care, to a model balancing individual and population interests.
  • Technology-enhanced care and AI introduce new ethical considerations layered onto duty of care, patient safety and resource allocation.

Key readings: A Code of Ethics for Public Health; National Policy for Quality in Healthcare (NPQHC) full version.

Health Policy Framework: Context, Process and Actors

Defining Policy and Its Relevance to Public Health

Policy can be defined broadly as a set of instructions or rules about what may and may not be done in order to achieve a desired outcome. For public health, policy matters because it is the mechanism through which population-level goals are translated into authoritative courses of action: it guides behaviour, activities and practice, and provides a framework for present and future decisions. A policy is not a single decision but a purposive course of action followed by an actor or set of actors, usually connected to government, in response to a problem or matter of public concern. Because policy is chosen from among several competing alternatives, understanding how and why a particular option was selected, and by whom, is central to public health practice and to the discipline of health policy analysis.

  • Policy is a purposive course of action, not a single decision, chosen from among competing alternatives.
  • Public health engages with policy because it is the principal lever for achieving population health outcomes.
  • Policy provides a framework guiding both present decisions and future action.

Key readings: Who Makes Public Policy in Malaysia; Making Health Policy (Buse, Mays and Walt).

The Health Policy Triangle: Context, Content, Actors and Process

A widely used framework for making sense of how health policy is developed is the health policy triangle (Walt and Gilson, 1994, as presented in Buse, Mays and Walt's Making Health Policy). The triangle organizes analysis around four interacting elements. Context refers to the situational, social, economic, structural, cultural and international environment within which policy is made. Content refers to the substance of the policy itself. Actors are the individuals and groups, both inside and outside government, who are involved in developing, contesting or implementing the policy. Process describes how policy is initiated, developed, negotiated, communicated and implemented, and is often understood through the concept of the policy cycle. Because these four elements interact rather than operate independently, an adequate analysis of any health policy needs to examine not only what a policy says (content) but also who shaped it, when and how it emerged, and the surrounding conditions that made a particular option feasible or politically acceptable.

  • Context: situational, social-economic, structural, cultural and international environment.
  • Content: the substantive provisions of the policy.
  • Actors: individuals and groups shaping or contesting the policy.
  • Process: how the policy moves through the policy cycle, from initiation to implementation.

Key readings: Making Health Policy (Buse, Mays and Walt); Reforming the Health Sector in Thailand.

Power and the Role of Actors in Policy Making

Actors matter in policy analysis because policy making is inherently an exercise of power, understood as the ability to achieve a desired outcome. Power operates along at least three dimensions: decision-making power, which pushes a particular agenda forward; non-decision-making power, which controls or dampens competing agendas from reaching the table at all; and thought control, the ability to shape or influence preferences themselves rather than merely the choices made from among them. Three theoretical perspectives explain who holds this power in a policy system. Pluralism holds that power is dispersed throughout society and that the state functions merely as a neutral referee facilitating decision making, supported by features such as open electoral competition, the ability of individuals and pressure groups to organize and lobby, and the absence of any single dominant elite group. The public choice perspective shares the pluralist premise that power is widely held, but rejects the idea that the state is neutral, treating the state itself, and civil servants acting as self-interested bureaucrats, as an interest group in its own right. Elitism, by contrast, holds that policy content is dominated by a privileged minority whose position rests on wealth, family connections, technical expertise, or formal office, including technical and professional elite groups.

The political system within which actors operate further shapes how power translates into policy: liberal democratic regimes, egalitarian-authoritarian systems with closed ruling elites and state-managed popular participation, traditional-inegalitarian systems built around traditional monarchs, populist systems dominated by a single nationalist and personalized leadership, and authoritarian-inegalitarian systems typically involving military government and repression, each create different conditions for how actors gain access to, and exercise, policy-making power.

  • Power has three dimensions: decision-making, non-decision-making (agenda suppression), and thought control (preference shaping).
  • Three theories of power: pluralism (dispersed, neutral state), public choice (state as self-interested actor), elitism (dominance by a privileged minority).
  • The type of political regime conditions how actors can access and exercise policy-making power.

Key readings: Actors in Decision Making and Policy Process; Policy and Ethics Framework (lecture slides).

Official and Unofficial Policymakers

Actors in the policy process can be divided into official and unofficial policymakers. Official policymakers possess legal authority to formulate public policy and include the legislature, the executive, administrators and the judiciary, each contributing in a distinct way. Among official actors, primary policymakers, such as members of a national legislature and the executive, are constitutionally empowered to formulate policy directly, while supplementary policymakers, such as ministries, departments and administrative agencies, derive their authority to act from the primary policymakers and are expected to be responsive to them. The executive typically plays a dominant role in initiating, formulating and implementing policy, reflecting what has been described as an "executive-centred era," driven by the executive's access to technical expertise, funding, and the increasing complexity of policy problems that require specialized knowledge. Legislators, though constitutionally the primary policymakers, in practice often defer to the executive because of fragmentation along party, ethnic or religious lines and gaps in technical expertise. The judiciary, while not empowered to initiate or implement policy directly, shapes policy indirectly through judicial review, statutory interpretation, rulings on economic matters, and judicial activism, ensuring propriety, fairness, constitutionality and moderation in the policy process. Administrators, though formally supplementary policymakers, wield substantial influence in practice because of the discretionary authority delegated to them and their technical competence and continuity in office.

Unofficial policymakers do not hold formal public positions but derive their relevance from their capacity to influence official policymakers. They include interest groups (such as professional associations, human rights groups, business associations and community organizations), which perform an interest-articulation function by expressing demands, supplying technical information, and mobilizing public support; political parties, which pursue control of government and use that position to advance their programs and manifestos; and individual citizens, who influence policy both through the electoral process and, particularly for those with relevant expertise or prior government experience, through direct engagement, activism and public commentary.

  • Official policymakers: legislature, executive, administrators, judiciary; divided into primary (constitutionally empowered) and supplementary (derived authority) policymakers.
  • The executive dominates the modern policy process due to resources, expertise and legislative fragmentation elsewhere.
  • Unofficial policymakers: interest groups, political parties, and individual citizens, who shape policy without holding formal office.

Key readings: Actors in Decision Making and Policy Process.

Applied Context: Sectoral Reform and Policy Content Examples

The policy triangle framework can be applied to concrete cases of health-related policy content to illustrate how context, actors and process interact in practice. Sectoral health reform efforts, such as reform of the health sector in Thailand, demonstrate how structural and economic context shapes the feasible content of reform, while national case studies such as men's health policy in Ireland and tobacco control policy illustrate how content is negotiated among competing actors, including government ministries, industry, civil society and international bodies, within a particular national and international context. These applied examples reinforce that health policy analysis is not simply a description of what a policy document says, but requires tracing how context enabled or constrained particular actors, and how the resulting content was shaped through a process of negotiation, agenda setting and implementation.

  • Sectoral reform examples show how economic and structural context shapes what policy content is achievable.
  • National case studies (men's health policy, tobacco policy) illustrate actor negotiation within a defined process.
  • Policy analysis requires tracing the interaction of context, content, actors and process together, not any single element in isolation.

Key readings: Reforming the Health Sector in Thailand; Men's Health Policy Ireland; Tobacco Policy.

Ethics in Epidemic Management

The Reality of Outbreak Decision-Making and Competing Values

Outbreak response is characterized by uncertainty, limited resources and capacities, the need for quick decisions even with scanty evidence, and the potential for social and institutional disruption, fear and distrust. According to the World Health Organization's 2016 guidance for managing ethical issues in infectious disease outbreaks, this environment forces decision-makers to balance two competing sets of consequences. On one side sits the catastrophic consequence of the epidemic itself, framed in utilitarian terms as the imperative to promote the greater good of public health, since an infected individual represents a threat to others in society with broader socio-economic implications. On the other side sit the consequences of the prevention and control measures themselves, framed in libertarian terms as the need to protect the health of individuals in the population while safeguarding privacy, individual rights and liberties such as freedom of movement. Public health measures taken to control an outbreak can therefore infringe upon widely accepted basic human rights and liberties, and outbreak ethics exists precisely to guide how these two sets of values should be balanced when they come into conflict.

  • Outbreaks combine scientific uncertainty, resource scarcity and time pressure, forcing rapid decisions on incomplete evidence.
  • Utilitarian logic (protecting population health) is in tension with libertarian concerns (individual rights and liberties).
  • Control measures can themselves generate socio-economic harm distinct from the harm of the disease.

Key readings: Guidance for Managing Ethical Issues in Infectious Disease Outbreaks, WHO, 2016; An Overview of Ethics, Public Health, and Communicable Disease.

Core Ethical Principles Guiding Epidemic Response

WHO guidance for managing ethical issues in infectious disease outbreaks identifies a set of core principles for epidemic management. Beneficence requires efforts to relieve suffering and reflects society's obligation to meet the basic needs of individuals and communities. Respect for persons requires treating and recognizing humanity, dignity and rights, including individual liberty. Justice requires attention to discrimination, vulnerability and procedural justice: equity means treating like cases alike, avoiding discrimination and exploitation, and being sensitive to persons especially vulnerable to harm or injustice, while procedural justice requires a fair process for making important decisions, comprising due process (notice and opportunity to be heard), transparency (clear and accurate information about decisions and how they are made), inclusiveness or community engagement (ensuring all relevant stakeholders can participate), accountability (allocating and enforcing responsibility for decisions), and oversight (mechanisms for monitoring and review). Utility requires that actions be judged right insofar as they promote the wellbeing of individuals or communities, balancing potential benefits against risks of harm, and pursuing efficiency in achieving the greatest benefit at the lowest possible cost. Reciprocity requires a fitting and proportional return for the contributions people make to the response. Solidarity requires that a group, community, nation or the global community stand together in collective action against a common threat, supporting efforts to overcome inequalities, particularly for minorities and those suffering discrimination. As a general operating rule, the more intrusive a proposed action, the greater the evidence needed to justify it, and when evidence is unavailable, decisions must be grounded in reasoning, substantive argument, and analogous situations as far as possible.

  • Core principles: beneficence, respect for persons and liberty, justice (equity and procedural justice), utility, reciprocity, and solidarity.
  • Procedural justice has five elements: due process, transparency, inclusiveness, accountability, oversight.
  • Rule of proportionality: intrusiveness of action should scale with the strength of available evidence.

Key readings: Guidance for Managing Ethical Issues in Infectious Disease Outbreaks, WHO, 2016; Ethics in Epidemic Management (lecture note).

The Siracusa Principles and Restriction of Rights

The Siracusa Principles address the limitation and derogation provisions of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR), providing a framework for evaluating when it is appropriate to limit fundamental rights in an emergency situation. Under this framework, any restriction on human rights must be carried out in accordance with the law and in pursuit of a legitimate objective of general interest. Restrictions must be strictly necessary, meaning no less restrictive alternative exists to reach the same goal, and must be based on scientific evidence rather than imposed in an unreasonable or discriminatory manner. This framework operationalizes the least restrictive or coercive means principle in the specific context of epidemic-related restrictions on movement, assembly and other civil liberties, and provides the legal counterpart to the ethical principle of proportionality discussed elsewhere in epidemic management.

  • The Siracusa Principles interpret when ICCPR rights may be legitimately limited or derogated from during emergencies.
  • Restrictions must be lawful, pursue a legitimate objective, be strictly necessary, and rest on scientific evidence.
  • The framework provides a legal complement to the ethical least-restrictive-means principle in outbreak control.

Key readings: Siracusa Principles on the Limitation and Derogation Provisions in the ICCPR, 1985.

Restrictive Measures, Public Health Law and Vulnerable Groups

Movement restriction measures such as quarantine and isolation must rest on a justifiable basis, subject to continuous re-evaluation, and should apply the least restrictive means available: quarantine may be home-based or institutional, and isolation requires well-equipped health care facilities with humane conditions and logistic support for symptomatic individuals. Restrictions must be applied equitably to all who pose a similar risk, must address the financial and social consequences borne by affected individuals, families and communities, must include measures to reduce stigmatization, discrimination and violence, and must provide due process protection, meaning a mechanism to challenge the appropriateness of the restriction and the process by which it was imposed. Sufficient public health law is needed to give authorities the power to respond effectively while protecting human rights, requiring attention to duty of care, the balance between community beneficence and individual harm, the principle of least coercion, principles of justice, and the relationship between law and human rights.

Vulnerable groups, those with heightened susceptibility to harm or injustice during an outbreak, face particular ethical concern. They often have greater difficulty accessing services and resources, may require alternative communication strategies to build trust and motivate participation, and are disproportionately exposed to the burdens of outbreak response measures, including the impact of quarantine on basic life needs, the effects of social distancing and premise closures on schooling and childcare, heightened risk of violence and mental health harm, and the additional resources needed to reach them. Public trust in the epidemic response depends on being open to review in light of new information, acting on genuine input from affected communities, and adopting a synchronous approach in which authorities act in solidarity, recognizing that all share a common vulnerability and common threat from the infectious disease. Community involvement is built through early and ongoing engagement, inclusiveness, openness to debating alternatives and revising decisions, transparency about the basis for decisions and acknowledgment of uncertainty, and accountability for who makes decisions, supported by media access to accurate and timely information without raising unnecessary alarm.

  • Movement restrictions must be justified, least restrictive, equitably applied, and subject to due process and continuous re-evaluation.
  • Vulnerable groups bear disproportionate burdens of outbreak measures and require tailored engagement strategies.
  • Public trust rests on transparency, accountability, community engagement and a synchronous, solidarity-based response.

Key readings: Guidance for Managing Ethical Issues in Infectious Disease Outbreaks, WHO, 2016; The Ethics of Infectious Disease Control: Ebola Outbreak Lessons.

Research, Data Sharing and Frontline Workers During Outbreaks

Research conducted during an epidemic must be designed to respond to local realities and needs, implemented alongside the public health response without compromising it or draining critical health-related resources, and must adhere to all principles of research ethics, though accelerated review processes may be needed, and a clear distinction must be maintained between research and the provision of standard care, with equitable access to study findings. Where no proven effective intervention exists and a clinical trial cannot be initiated immediately, the Monitored Emergency Use of Unregistered and Experimental Interventions (MEURI) framework permits use of an unproven intervention where supportive safety and efficacy data exist (for example from animal studies), a qualified advisory committee recommends use based on risk-benefit analysis, adequate resources are available to minimize risk, informed consent is obtained, and results are monitored and shared in a timely manner with the wider medical and scientific community, balancing respect for autonomy against harm and benefit. Rapid data sharing, drawn from public health surveillance, clinical research, individual patient encounters, MEURI and epidemiological studies, supports identifying disease etiology, predicting spread, evaluating treatments, and guiding resource allocation, and should not wait for official publication. Public health surveillance itself requires protecting confidentiality, operating on a mandatory basis without possibility of individual refusal, and disclosing information transparently, including notification of events as part of global surveillance under the International Health Regulations, communicated in a way that minimizes the risk of stigmatization and discrimination.

Frontline health care workers, within and outside the health sector, including volunteers, are owed obligations in return for the heightened risk they accept: minimizing infection risk through training, tools and personal protective equipment; priority access to health care for workers and their families; appropriate remuneration; support against stigmatization and discrimination; and assistance to family members. This reflects a reciprocal relationship in which society must fulfil its obligation to workers whose risk must be proportionate to the public health benefit and distributed equitably and transparently across occupational categories, while health care workers in turn are expected to participate in surveillance and reporting while protecting patient confidentiality, provide accurate information to the public and counter misinformation, and avoid exploiting desperate patients by offering unproven treatments outside the MEURI framework. Deployment of humanitarian foreign aid workers requires proper coordination with local officials, clear responsibilities, registration as part of a recognized medical emergency team, cultural and linguistic competency, and adequate training, preparation, security and safety, so as to avoid the excessive withdrawal of local resources or the phenomenon of the unhelpful "disaster tourist."

  • Research during an epidemic must not compromise the response and must follow accelerated but rigorous ethical review.
  • MEURI permits ethically bounded use of unproven interventions absent a feasible clinical trial.
  • Reciprocal obligations bind health systems and frontline workers: protection and support in exchange for accepted risk.

Key readings: Guidance for Managing Ethical Issues in Infectious Disease Outbreaks, WHO, 2016; The Ethics of Infectious Disease Control: Ebola Outbreak Lessons.

Agenda Setting in Policy Development

The Concept of Agenda-Setting in the Policy Process

Agenda-setting is the pre-decision stage of the policy process in which certain issues, out of the much larger universe of problems competing for attention, come to be recognised by policy makers as warranting government action. It is the phase in which a public problem is converted into a political issue and thereby becomes eligible for further formulation, decision, implementation and evaluation. Cobb and Elder's foundational work distinguished between a systemic (or public) agenda, comprising all issues that society perceives as deserving attention, and an institutional or governmental agenda, defined as the concrete set of items scheduled for active and serious consideration by a specific decision-making body. Kingdon later added a further refinement, the decisional agenda, denoting the smaller subset of governmental-agenda items that are not only recognised as problems but are also ripe for actionable policy responses.

Central to this process is the idea that attention is a scarce resource: policy makers and institutions cannot attend to every problem simultaneously, and the process of prioritisation is shaped by cultural, temporal and institutional context. What is treated as urgent in one society, or at one point in time, may be disregarded elsewhere or in another era. Understanding which themes capture the attention of decision makers, and why, is therefore essential to understanding the wider policy process.

  • Systemic agenda: the broad set of issues perceived by society as legitimate concerns.
  • Institutional/governmental agenda: the narrower set of items actively considered by a decision-making body.
  • Decisional agenda (Kingdon): governmental-agenda items that are ripe for concrete policy action.
  • Attention is finite; prioritisation is a social and political construction, not a neutral technical exercise.

Key readings: Agenda setting: Policy change and policy dynamics (Brasil & Jones); Agenda-setting Policy: Strategies and Agenda Denial Mechanism; agenda setting in general; sutton policy process overview.

Kingdon's Multiple Streams and Hall's Model of Agenda Formation

John Kingdon's multiple streams framework remains one of the most widely applied models for explaining why some issues rise onto the decision agenda while others do not. It conceives of three largely independent streams flowing through the political system: the problem stream (conditions that come to be defined as problems requiring attention), the policy stream (the pool of ideas and technical proposals developed by specialists), and the political stream (shifts in national mood, election outcomes, and the balance of organised interests). Agenda change is most likely at a "policy window," a moment when the three streams are coupled, often by a policy entrepreneur who links a recognised problem to an available solution at a politically opportune moment.

A complementary, more applied model used in class discussion is Hall's model, which evaluates whether an issue is likely to gain a place on the agenda according to three criteria: legitimacy, feasibility and support. Legitimacy concerns whether the issue is seen as a proper matter for government concern; feasibility concerns whether the relevant authority has the technical and administrative capacity to act; and support concerns whether key constituencies, including the public, government, international bodies and professional groups, back the proposed action. Applying Hall's model to the case of COVID-19 vaccination illustrates the framework in practice: legitimacy was established through the rising case numbers and the scale of the pandemic's economic burden; feasibility rested on the Ministry of Health's operational capacity and the availability of developed vaccines; and support was drawn from prior vaccination coverage among citizens together with backing from government, the World Health Organization and medical practitioners.

  • Kingdon's three streams: problem, policy and political, converging at a policy window.
  • Policy entrepreneurs couple streams together to move an issue onto the decision agenda.
  • Hall's model criteria: legitimacy, feasibility and support.
  • Applied example: COVID-19 vaccination agenda-setting assessed against Hall's three criteria.

Punctuated Equilibrium and the Politics of Attention

Where Kingdon's model foregrounds the coupling of streams at discrete moments, Baumgartner and Jones's punctuated equilibrium theory addresses the longer-run pattern of policy change: long periods of incremental stability within policy subsystems, interrupted occasionally by large-scale, rapid change. Under normal conditions, policy subsystems, such as forest management or defence procurement, are governed quietly by affected interests working out arrangements with officials, largely outside public view. Punctuation occurs when a subsystem issue is mobilised into the arena of macropolitics, drawing in political parties, elected executives and broader social movements, and prompting large-scale revision of existing policy.

The general punctuation thesis extends this logic by describing government as facing an array of competing problems under conditions of bounded attention: information is inherently uncertain and ambiguous, and institutions, like individuals, cannot attend to everything at once. This produces a systematic delay in acting on problems, which are often allowed to fester until they cross a threshold of criticality. Resistance to action also comes from veto players, actors whose institutional authority allows them to block change, and from ideological rigidities that impede recognition of a problem in the first place. The resulting dynamic is described as "stick-slip": friction and resistance accumulate until a threshold is crossed, at which point change occurs rapidly, producing a distribution of policy outcomes with many small incremental adjustments, a few very large changes and comparatively few moderate ones. A related phenomenon is the policy bubble, in which alarmed discovery of a problem leads not to under-reaction but to an oversupply of government resources directed at the issue, creating a new policy subsystem of interested actors who then resist reversing the gains they have secured.

  • Subsystem politics (stable, incremental) versus macropolitics (large-scale, punctuated change).
  • General punctuation thesis: bounded attention leads to delay, then abrupt correction once a threshold of criticality is crossed.
  • Veto players and ideological rigidity as sources of resistance to agenda change.
  • Stick-slip dynamics and policy bubbles as patterns of policy change over time.

Power and Agenda-Setting: The Tobacco Control Case Study

Agenda-setting cannot be understood independently of power, since the ability of an issue to reach the agenda often depends on which actors are able to exert influence over the process. A scoping review of tobacco control policy in developing countries organised the roles of power into three categories: power exercised through authority (control over resources and formal decision-making capacity), power exercised through coercion (compulsion via obligation, such as accession requirements to regional blocs), and the power of non-decision-making actors, who influence the agenda indirectly through lobbying rather than through formal authority. Three sources of power more broadly identified in the literature are government, technical expertise (including evidence and technology), and funding resources.

Country case studies illustrate how these forms of power interact. In Turkey, strong political leadership, a national prevalence study revealing high smoking rates, and a coalition of physicians and academics combined to build political priority for tobacco control, culminating in ratification of the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC) in 2004. In India, competing lobbying by cigarette and bidi (local tobacco) industries, together with public interest litigation and use of the Right to Information Act, eventually forced implementation of graphical health warning labels, illustrating a protracted struggle over both authority and non-decision-making power. In Vietnam and China, one-party political systems in which the state also owned or profited from the tobacco industry generated internal contradictions that slowed agenda progress, with coercive and non-decision-making power dominating over open contestation. Across cases, transnational tobacco companies employed common tactics, including appeals to small business protection, warnings about illicit trade, and claims of policy ineffectiveness, to keep tobacco control off the agenda or dilute its priority.

  • Three categories of power in agenda-setting: authority, coercion, and non-decision-making (lobbying/influence).
  • Turkey: leadership, evidence and coalition-building produced rapid agenda success.
  • India: prolonged conflict between industry lobbying and advocacy litigation over health warning labels.
  • China and Vietnam: state ownership of the tobacco industry created conflicting government interests that slowed the agenda.
  • Common industry tactics: framing control measures as threats to small business, drivers of illicit trade, or ineffective.

Key readings: Agenda Setting and Power in Policy Making: A Case Study of Tobacco Policy in Developing Countries.

From Agenda-Setting to Policy Formulation: Actors and Lessons

Agenda-setting is not an endpoint but a gateway to policy formulation, the stage at which recommendations are drafted and submitted for validation by higher authorities. Multiple issues may be raised by initiators ranging from government officials and politicians to individuals, interest groups, pressure groups and non-governmental organisations; these compete for attention through lobbying, negotiation and advocacy before an issue is recognised as a policy agenda item. Once formulation begins, constituency-building, strengthening the involvement, knowledge and agency of those most affected by an issue, and resource mobilisation become essential to sustaining momentum toward implementation and eventual evaluation.

Mapping the actors involved in a policy domain across levels, international bodies such as the World Health Organization, national government ministries and legislatures, and non-governmental organisations acting as lobbyists, pressure groups or interest groups, clarifies where power is concentrated and where contestation is likely. Several lessons emerge from comparative case analysis: first, government leadership is decisive in determining whether an issue moves from the agenda into formulation; second, cooperation and network-building among advocates strengthens the prospects of sustained agenda priority; and third, opposing non-state actors, such as multinational industry interests, can substantially delay or distort the agenda-setting process even where public health evidence is strong. These lessons generalise beyond tobacco control to other domains of health policy where competing economic and political interests intersect with public health goals.

  • Policy formulation follows agenda-setting: recommendations move from senior officials to higher authorities for validation.
  • Constituency-building and resource mobilisation sustain an issue's position on the agenda.
  • Actors operate at international, national and non-governmental levels, each exerting distinct forms of influence.
  • Lessons: government leadership, coalition cooperation among advocates, and awareness of opposing industry interests are decisive to agenda success.

Key readings: Doing Health Policy Analysis; Policy Levers in Malaysia Graham; National Men's Health Policy 2008-2013.

Ethics in Health Education & Promotion

Conceptual Foundations: Health Education, Health Promotion and the Ottawa Charter

Health promotion and health education are related but conceptually distinct activities, and the distinction carries ethical weight because each implies a different relationship between the professional and the public. Under the Malaysian Health Promotion Board Act (Act 651, 2006), health promotion is defined as any action or activity that strengthens the skills and capabilities of individuals, groups and communities, or that enhances social and environmental conditions, in order to improve health status. The Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion (Geneva, 1986) frames health promotion as the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health, and situates it as involving social, economic and political change to create environments conducive to health. Health education, by contrast, refers to a combination of learning experiences designed to help individuals and communities improve their health by increasing knowledge or influencing attitudes, essentially providing information for rational decision-making about health behaviours, and motivating behavioural change by directly engaging values, beliefs and attitudes.

This distinction matters ethically because health promotion's ambition to change social, economic and political conditions extends the practitioner's reach beyond the individual and into structural determinants of health, raising questions about legitimacy and scope that are less pressing for health education's narrower, information-based mandate. Both activities nonetheless share a common ethical tension: motivating people to change health behaviour by influencing their values, beliefs and attitudes can shade into inducing fear or otherwise overriding autonomous decision-making, which raises the recurring question of whether a given intervention remains educational or has become coercive.

  • Health promotion (Act 651, 2006): strengthening capability and improving social/environmental conditions for health.
  • Ottawa Charter (1986): health promotion as enabling people to increase control over and improve their health.
  • Health education: learning experiences that increase knowledge or influence attitude to support rational health decisions.
  • Shared ethical tension: motivating behaviour change without inducing fear or overriding autonomy.

Key readings: Ethics in Health Education & Promotion; Ethical Issues in Health Promotion and Communication Interventions; Evidence, Ethics, and Values: A Framework for Health Promotion.

Autonomy versus Paternalism: The Persuasion-Coercion Continuum

A central ethical fault line in health education and promotion runs between respect for individual autonomy and the justification of paternalistic intervention. Individual rights encompass the freedom to select personal behaviour or lifestyle, to decide the appropriateness of one's own health behaviour, and to make decisions for oneself without external compulsion, a position associated with natural law theory. Against this stands the theory of paternalism, which holds that intervention on behalf of another person can be justified when that person is unable to decide for themselves, faces significant harm without intervention, and would later agree that the intervening decision was correct, provided the intervention is conducted by a credible professional acting in line with advances in health care. Paternalism faces the challenge that people hold their own preferences and values that arguably should not be subject to external criticism, and it risks an individualistic bias that locates the burden of change in the person rather than in the social, cultural and environmental conditions that shape their behaviour.

Fear operates as a double-edged instrument within this continuum: it can inhibit rational decision-making and cloud objective thinking, yet it can also function as a legitimate motivator when promoting prevention is understood as a matter of social justice, that is, the reduction of serious harm while avoiding greater danger. The underlying justification offered for paternalistic health promotion is that health professionals are better informed than the individual to advise on appropriate protective measures. This raises further fairness questions that recur throughout the module: is it consistent to impose higher taxation on smokers for a self-regarding behaviour while not similarly penalising, for example, heart disease patients who have chosen an unhealthy lifestyle? Does depriving people of an enjoyable but self-destructive activity violate their rights? These questions sit on what is described as a thin line between persuasion and coercion, requiring the practitioner to balance individual risk against environmental influence while maintaining a justifiable claim to be acting authoritatively rather than merely authoritarian.

  • Natural law / individual rights: freedom to select and decide one's own health behaviour.
  • Paternalism justified when a person cannot decide for themselves, faces significant harm, and would later agree with the decision.
  • Fear can inhibit rational choice but can also serve social-justice ends by reducing serious harm.
  • Recurrent fairness question: consistency of penalising some self-regarding health behaviours (e.g. smoking) but not others.
  • The thin line between persuasion and coercion, balancing individual risk against environmental influence.

Key readings: How to Think about Health Promotion Ethics; HEALTH PROMOTION ethical and social dilemmas of government policy; The Ethics of Health Promotion (Solberg).

Ethical Issues Frameworks: Gardner and McLeroy

Gardner's (2014) framework organises the ethical issues confronting public health practice around three questions: what are the ultimate goals of public health practice, that is, what "good" ought to be achieved; how should this good be distributed across the population, which requires separating medical facts from moral judgement, as in debates over free sexual behaviour and HIV transmission; and what means may legitimately be used to achieve and distribute this good, weighing cost-effectiveness against ethical acceptability of the method chosen. This framework foregrounds distributive justice and proportionality as criteria for evaluating any proposed health promotion intervention, not merely its technical effectiveness.

McLeroy and colleagues (1993) extend this analysis with a set of practical dilemmas facing the profession. Individuals bear some responsibility for staying healthy, yet illness often results from disruptive behaviour that is itself shaped by uncertain and multifactorial influences, raising a principle-of-justice question as to whether individuals can fairly be penalised for outcomes only partly within their control. This connects to the individualistic bias critique: should intervention target the individual or the environment that influences them, and can practitioners ethically ignore social, cultural and other social-determinant influences on behaviour? McLeroy also raises questions about the evidentiary threshold for action (to what extent should health promotion intervention proceed on the basis of limited evidence of effectiveness), about the risk of health promotion fostering an unwarranted "obsession" with health when underlying risks remain uncertain, and about safeguarding public confidence that interventions serve public rather than commercial interest.

  • Gardner (2014): ultimate goals ("good"), distribution of that good, and means used to achieve and distribute it.
  • Separating medical fact from moral judgement (e.g. free sex and HIV) as a distinct ethical task.
  • McLeroy et al. (1993): fairness of penalising individuals for multifactorial health outcomes.
  • Individualistic bias: whether intervention should target the person or the environment/social determinants.
  • Evidentiary threshold for acting, and the risk of promoting a public "obsession" with uncertain health risks.
  • Guarding against the perception of commercial rather than public interest in promotion activities.

Key readings: Ethical Issues in PH Promotion (Gardner, 2014); Ethical Issues in Health Education (McLeroy).

Professional Codes of Ethics and Best Practice in Health Education

Anderson and Fox (1987) locate the ethical concerns of health education within four interacting domains: the scope and mission of health education and promotion, the methodology used in education and promotion, the health issues, content and curriculum selected for teaching, and the selection and training of prospective educators. Each domain carries its own ethical exposure, from overreach in defining the profession's mandate to bias in curriculum choice or inadequate preparation of those delivering interventions. Building on this, professional codes of ethics for health educators, such as that maintained by the Society for Public Health Education (SOPHE), articulate core obligations: respect for autonomy, meaning respect for individual dignity regardless of the educator's personal attitudes or preferences, and respect for confidentiality; promotion of good and avoidance of harm; promotion of social justice, including practising within the scope of one's competence and maintaining that competence; and accountability, including serving as a role model. Additional responsibilities are owed to the profession, to the community, to professional competence, to the responsible and ethical use of technology, and to cross-cultural adaptability and respect.

These principles translate into concrete best-practice guidance for ethical health education. Practitioners should consider who they are educating and the situation in which education occurs, communicate rather than alienate, and avoid shocking or traumatising the client. They should teach and educate rather than preach or indoctrinate, and should not force or impose their own values. The orientation should be to motivate rather than dominate, remaining objective by presenting choices and alternatives along a behavioural continuum, and to be honest, open and well prepared while avoiding bias and moralising. Content should focus on relevant issues rather than trivial concerns or the mere imparting of facts, and the educator's role is to encourage thinking and facilitate decision-making, ensuring voluntary participation, rather than to make decisions on the client's behalf, ultimately encouraging positive behavioural choices. The rapid uptake of generative artificial intelligence tools within health education and promotion practice has also emerged as a live and unresolved ethical consideration for the profession, given its implications for accuracy, accountability and the educator-client relationship.

  • Anderson and Fox (1987): four domains of ethical concern, scope/mission, methodology, content/curriculum, and educator selection/training.
  • SOPHE code of ethics: autonomy, confidentiality, promoting good/avoiding harm, social justice, competence, accountability.
  • Additional responsibilities: to the profession, to the community, to technology use, and to cross-cultural respect.
  • Best practice: communicate not alienate; educate not indoctrinate; motivate not dominate; be objective and honest; encourage voluntary, informed choice.
  • Emerging consideration: ethical implications of AI use in health education and promotion practice.

Key readings: Ethical Issues in Health Promotion and Communication Interventions; The Ethics of Health Promotion (Solberg); anderson-fox-1987.

Government & the Policy Process

Typology of Policy Actors: Official and Unofficial Policymakers

The policy process is populated by a range of actors whose participation shapes each of its sub-processes: initiation, choice, formulation, implementation and evaluation. A widely used typology divides these participants into official and unofficial policymakers. Official policymakers possess legal authority to engage in the formulation of public policy and occupy formal public positions or political offices; this category includes legislators, the executive, administrators and the judiciary. Official policymakers are further divided into primary policymakers, who are constitutionally empowered to formulate policy and need not depend on other bodies to exercise this role (for example, members of a national legislature and the chief executive), and supplementary policymakers, who derive their authority to act from the primary policymakers and are expected to be responsive to their interests, such as ministries, departments and other government agencies that initiate and push policy proposals.

Unofficial policymakers, by contrast, do not occupy formal public positions and possess no legal authority to make binding policy decisions. They derive their relevance from government and the official policymakers, and their principal function is to harness, harmonise and channel interests and demands so as to influence official policymakers to factor them into the policy process. This category comprises interest groups, political parties and individual citizens. The distinction between official and unofficial status is not a distinction in influence: unofficial actors, particularly organised interest groups, frequently exert substantial influence over agenda content and policy design despite lacking formal decision-making authority.

  • Official policymakers: legislators, the executive, administrators, and the judiciary; possess legal authority.
  • Primary policymakers: constitutionally empowered, act independently (e.g. legislature, chief executive).
  • Supplementary policymakers: derive authority from primary policymakers (e.g. ministries, agencies).
  • Unofficial policymakers: interest groups, political parties, individual citizens; influence without formal authority.

Key readings: Actors in Decision Making and Policy Process; Govt & policy process LM note.

The Role of the Executive in the Policy-Making Process

The executive, comprising the head of government or state, ministers, special advisers and administrators, is formally responsible for implementing public policies and for supervising, coordinating and managing the ministries, departments and agencies engaged in implementation. In practice, however, the executive frequently dominates the entire policy process rather than confining itself to implementation, a pattern described in the literature as an "executive-centred era." Several factors explain this dominance: the executive commands an array of ministers, advisers, technocrats and experienced administrators across the full range of policy domains; executive power tends to grow as policy problems become more technically complex and require specialised knowledge; legislatures often lack the cohesion, time and information to originate policy and effectively cede that responsibility to the executive; and the executive controls substantial funds, facilities and patronage that can be mobilised to secure the passage of its proposals.

This concentration of policy leadership in the executive is especially pronounced in developing-country contexts, where power tends to be more highly centralised, giving the executive disproportionate influence over both the initiation and the implementation stages of the policy process, including in areas of foreign and security policy where executive discretion is typically at its widest.

  • Formal executive role: implementation, supervision and coordination of ministries, departments and agencies.
  • "Executive-centred era": the executive increasingly originates as well as implements policy.
  • Drivers: technical expertise, growing complexity of policy problems, legislative fragmentation, and control of resources/patronage.
  • Executive dominance is often most pronounced in developing-country political systems.

Key readings: Actors in Decision Making and Policy Process; Who makes public policy in Malaysia? (New Mandala).

The Roles of the Legislature and the Judiciary

Legislators constitute the primary policymakers in most constitutional systems, possessing direct authority to initiate and formulate policy on behalf of the constituencies they represent. Their formal role is to collate constituents' views, interests and demands, harmonise them into policy proposals, and subject these to the legislative processes of reading, debate and committee scrutiny before forwarding them for executive assent. In practice, however, legislators in many systems have ceded much of this initiating role to the executive, largely formalising and legitimising policies that originate elsewhere. Contributing factors include fragmentation along party, ethnic or other lines, uneven technical expertise relative to executive technocrats, and the structural resource advantage enjoyed by the executive. Legislatures nonetheless remain active and consequential policy actors in developed democratic systems operating under presidential or parliamentary government, even where their influence is diminished in more centralised or authoritarian settings.

The judiciary, comprising judges and courts, is not constitutionally empowered to initiate, formulate or implement policy directly, but it contributes significantly to the policy process through interpretation of the constitution and the law. Four instruments through which the judiciary shapes policy have been identified: judicial review, the power to determine the constitutionality of legislative and executive action and to void actions found unconstitutional; statutory interpretation, clarifying ambiguous constitutional or legislative provisions in ways that become binding; adjudication of cases with economic policy implications, such as those concerning contracts, property and employment relationships; and judicial activism, through which courts regulate social and political activity in line with changing circumstances, including adjudicating on matters such as rights to social welfare services or the operation of public institutions. Through these instruments, the judiciary can modify, redirect or moderate policy implementation, functioning as an actor that safeguards propriety, fairness, constitutionality and justice within the policy process.

  • Legislators: constitutionally primary policymakers, but often cede initiation to the executive in practice.
  • Judiciary: not a formal policy initiator, but shapes policy via judicial review, statutory interpretation, economic-policy adjudication and judicial activism.
  • Judicial role functions as a check that moderates and can redirect policy implementation.

Key readings: Actors in Decision Making and Policy Process; Thailand policy actors.

Administrators and Bureaucratic Discretion in Implementation

Administrators are classified as supplementary policymakers who derive their authority from primary policymakers and who work within the executive arm of government as the direct implementers of public policy. Although their formal mandate is implementation, the technicality and complexity of modern policy problems, combined with legislators' limited time and information, has led to substantial delegation of discretionary, often formally recognised rule-making, authority to administrative agencies. Administrators consequently make many decisions with far-reaching political and policy consequences, extending their practical role well beyond mere execution to include the generation, initiation and shaping of policy proposals. Because administrators can engage in foot-dragging or non-enforcement during implementation, they can, in effect, make or unmake a policy regardless of its formal content.

The professionalism, technical skill and institutional continuity that career administrators bring to government positions them to wield considerable influence, particularly given that political office holders depend on administrators for the quality time and specialised information needed to manage complex policy areas. This administrative discretion is described in the literature as a structural necessity of modern governance rather than a deviation from it, since effective government in a complex and rapidly changing environment would be impracticable without some devolution of judgement to the bureaucracy.

  • Administrators are supplementary policymakers who implement, but also generate and shape, policy.
  • Delegated discretionary and rule-making authority arises from policy complexity and legislative capacity limits.
  • Implementation behaviour (including foot-dragging or non-enforcement) can determine a policy's real-world effect.
  • Bureaucratic discretion is treated as structurally necessary to modern governance, not merely incidental.

Key readings: Actors in Decision Making and Policy Process.

Unofficial Policy Actors: Interest Groups, Political Parties and Citizens

Unofficial policymakers exert influence over the policy process without holding formal legal authority to make binding decisions. Interest groups are associations of individuals sharing common interests, beliefs and aspirations that organise as civil society bodies to advocate their demands; they can broadly be categorised into professional, labour and student associations; human rights groups; identity- or region-based groups contesting for state power and resources; business and commercial associations; and voluntary, community-based associations. These groups perform an interest-articulation function, expressing demands, proposing policy alternatives, and supplying officials with technical information about the likely consequences of policy proposals, thereby contributing to the overall rationality of policymaking. They exert influence through submitting memoranda and draft proposals to the legislature, mobilising public support, and working through elected representatives.

Political parties, groups of like-minded individuals seeking control of the machinery of government, play a related but distinct role: a party controlling power tends to steer its members in government toward policies that protect its programme and manifesto, while minority parties advocate and build coalitions to have their positions factored into policy, and all parties contribute alternatives to existing policy. Individual citizens, finally, remain consequential to the policy process both as voters, whose electoral choices can produce basic changes in policy direction and can discontinue policy programmes they reject, and, in the case of citizens with particular intellectual depth, prior government experience or public standing, as direct contributors of policy proposals through political activism, memoranda and media engagement. Sound implementation ultimately depends on all these actors, official and unofficial, discharging their responsibilities faithfully, with corruption in implementation identified as a persistent threat to otherwise well-formulated policy.

  • Interest groups: professional/labour, human rights, identity-based, business, and voluntary/community categories.
  • Interest groups perform interest articulation and supply technical information, aiding policy rationality.
  • Political parties shape policy through governing programmes, coalition-building, and offering policy alternatives.
  • Citizens influence policy through voting, activism, memoranda and media engagement.
  • Corruption during implementation is identified as a major threat to otherwise sound policy.

Key readings: Actors in Decision Making and Policy Process; tobacco policy; Supplementary Reading for Government.

Policy Implementation

The Policy Process and the Implementation Gap

Policy implementation is best understood as one stage within a broader policy cycle. Using the stages heuristic proposed by Sabatier and Jenkins-Smith (1993), the policy process moves through problem identification and agenda setting, policy formulation, policy implementation, and policy evaluation. Implementation itself is defined as what happens between policy expectations (the ideal, planned results) and the results that are actually perceived on the ground. This space between intention and outcome is often described as the "implementation gap" - the distance between the political act of formulating a policy and the technical, administrative work of putting it into effect.

Policy analysis more broadly is shaped by the interaction of process, content, context, and actors. Context includes situational, structural, cultural, and international or exogenous factors, while actors span government, government agencies, non-governmental organisations, and individuals. Implementation failure is commonly attributed to two broad causes: the wrong strategy (a technically or politically flawed policy design) or the wrong machinery (an implementation apparatus that is poorly suited to carry the policy out).

  • Policy process stages: agenda setting, formulation, implementation, evaluation (Sabatier & Jenkins-Smith, 1993)
  • Implementation is the gap between planning and ideal results
  • Failure arises from wrong strategy (design) or wrong machinery (delivery apparatus)

Key readings: Policy Implementation lecture slides; The Implementation Gap and Policy Failure Theories (supplementary note)

Top-Down Approaches to Implementation

The top-down approach treats implementation as a rational, linear process that proceeds from policy formulation (a political act) to policy implementation (a technical act), with a gap sitting between the two. In this model, goals must be clear and robust, and direction flows from the centre to the periphery; failure is explained as a product of poor strategy design or inadequate implementing machinery. Two classic frameworks operationalise this perspective. Sabatier and Mazmanian (1979) specify conditions for successful implementation: clear and logically consistent objectives, an adequate causal theory linking action to outcome, an implementation process structured to secure compliance from implementers, committed and skilful officials, support from interest groups and the legislature, and the absence of changes in socioeconomic conditions that would undermine political and causal support.

Hogwood and Gunn (1984) offer a parallel but more exhaustive list of "perfect implementation" conditions, including that circumstances external to the implementing agency do not impose crippling constraints, that adequate time and resources are available, that the required combination of resources is genuinely available, that there is a valid theory of cause and effect with a direct relationship between the two, that dependency relationships are minimal, that there is agreed understanding of objectives, that tasks are specified in correct sequence, that communication and coordination are near-perfect, and that those in authority can demand and obtain perfect compliance. Because these conditions are rarely all met simultaneously, the top-down model is frequently critiqued as an idealised benchmark rather than a realistic description of implementation.

  • Rational, linear model: formulation (political) then implementation (technical)
  • Sabatier & Mazmanian (1979): six conditions centred on clear objectives, causal theory, and compliance
  • Hogwood & Gunn (1984): ten conditions for "perfect implementation," rarely fully satisfied in practice

Key readings: Policy Implementation lecture slides

Bottom-Up Approaches and Street-Level Bureaucracy

Bottom-up approaches shift attention away from central designers and toward those who deliver policy on the ground. In this view, policy implementers have a stake and a voice in how policy takes shape, rather than being passive conduits for centrally determined objectives. Lipsky's (1980) concept of "street-level bureaucrats" captures this idea: front-line workers such as teachers, health workers, and social workers exercise discretion in how they apply rules and, in doing so, effectively make policy through their daily practice. The degree to which the centre can control the periphery in this model depends on control of funds, legislation, and operating rules together with the practical ability to enforce them. This perspective corrects for the top-down model's tendency to treat implementers as neutral executors and instead recognises that implementation outcomes are actively shaped, and sometimes reshaped, at the point of delivery.

  • Implementers are active participants in shaping policy outcomes, not passive executors
  • Street-level bureaucrats (Lipsky, 1980): front-line discretion shapes real-world policy effects
  • Centre-periphery control depends on funds, legislation, and enforceable operating rules

Key readings: Policy Implementation lecture slides

Principal-Agent Theory and Discretion

Principal-agent theory offers a third lens that sits alongside, rather than simply between, the top-down and bottom-up traditions, drawing on perspectives from political science, sociology, and management science. It frames the structure of modern government as one in which decision-makers (principals) must delegate implementation to other actors (agents) whom they cannot fully monitor or control. Because agents may not share the same vision, incentives, or priorities as principals, the resulting "discretion" can produce ineffective or inefficient translation of policy intent into practice. Politicians must therefore secure the compliance of their officials, and the degree of discretion exercised depends on the nature of the policy problem itself, including its inherent features, the context or circumstances surrounding it, and the organisation of the machinery required to implement it.

A related set of factors, identified by Linder and Peters (1989), further shapes implementation choices: features of the policy instrument itself (resource intensiveness, targeting, political risk, and degree of coerciveness), policy style and political culture, organisational culture, the context of the problem, the subjective preferences of administrative decision-makers, government capacity, and the overall complexity of the policy field.

  • Principals delegate to agents who are difficult to monitor and may not share the principal's vision
  • Discretion depends on problem features, context, and implementing machinery
  • Linder & Peters (1989): instrument features, political/organisational culture, and administrative capacity all shape implementation choices

Sabatier's Advocacy Coalition Framework

Sabatier's Advocacy Coalition Framework repositions implementation as an integral part of the overall policy process rather than a discrete, terminal stage. Policy is understood as a continuous process operating within a policy subsystem that is bounded by relatively stable parameters and periodically reshaped by major external events. Within this subsystem, various actors continue to engage in the ongoing policy cycle; notably, the general public is not considered part of the subsystem, since it typically lacks the time or inclination to participate directly in policy making. The framework is distinguished by its focus on competing advocacy coalitions, made up of actors who share core beliefs, who deploy various policy instruments over time, and who compete with one another across an extended timeframe. Rather than analysing implementation through the lens of individual "actors" or "institutions," the Advocacy Coalition Framework treats the policy subsystem itself as the unit of analysis, offering a more dynamic and less linear account of how implementation unfolds.

  • Implementation is continuous, embedded within a bounded policy subsystem
  • Subsystems are shaped by major external events and populated by competing advocacy coalitions
  • Unit of analysis shifts from actors/institutions to the policy subsystem itself

Key readings: Policy Implementation lecture slides

Research Evidence, Evaluation, and Policy Reform

Evidence-Based Public Health

Evidence-based public health is defined as the development, implementation, and evaluation of effective programs and policies in public health through the application of principles of scientific reasoning, including the systematic use of data and information systems and the appropriate use of behavioural science theory and program planning models. This framing emphasises that evidence-based practice in public health extends beyond simply citing research; it requires a disciplined process of generating, appraising, and applying evidence within program and policy cycles, in keeping with the guidance set out by the Secretary's Advisory Committee on National Health Promotion and Disease Prevention Objectives.

  • Combines scientific reasoning, systematic data use, and behavioural theory in program planning
  • Extends the evidence-based paradigm from clinical medicine into population-level policy and program design

Key readings: Research, Evidence and Policy lecture note; Evidence-Based Clinical and Public Health: Generating and Applying the Evidence (Secretary's Advisory Committee on National Health Promotion and Disease Prevention Objectives)

Models of How Research Enters the Policy Process

Several conceptual models describe how research evidence is thought to translate into policy action, each carrying different assumptions about the relationship between researchers and policy makers. The Engineering Model assumes a rational, linear approach in which research evidence precedes and directly informs policy making. The Enlightenment Model instead holds that research evidence is produced without clarity about when or how it will eventually be used; researchers pursue lines of inquiry driven by their own ideas, and that evidence is only later picked up and applied by others, often without the originating problem having been explicitly recognised as requiring policy attention, and without a transparent process for how or why particular evidence was ultimately selected.

The Strategic Model treats research evidence as a political instrument, used by governments and interest groups to promote pre-existing causes, sometimes reflected in a defensive posture of "we shall do more research" or "gather more evidence" rather than genuine open inquiry. The Elective Affinity Model proposes that evidence is given more weight when the policy maker was personally involved in the research process, or when the findings align with the values and beliefs already held by the policy audience, an ideological compatibility between researcher and policy maker that depends on the timing and duration of their contact. Under this model, research that challenges prevailing ideological thinking tends to be rejected in the short term, even though it may prove useful later.

  • Engineering Model: research evidence precedes and drives policy in a linear fashion
  • Enlightenment Model: evidence generated independently, used unpredictably by others later
  • Strategic Model: evidence deployed as a political instrument to support existing positions
  • Elective Affinity Model: uptake depends on ideological compatibility between researcher and policy maker

Key readings: Research, Evidence and Policy lecture note

The Two Communities Model and Barriers to Research Use

The Two Communities Model characterises researchers and policy makers as inhabiting different worlds, with correspondingly different views of what matters. The two groups are described as differing in their work, their attitudes toward research, their accountability structures, their priorities, their career incentives and rewards, their training and knowledge base, their organisational constraints, and their underlying values and orientation. This divergence helps explain why technically sound research evidence does not automatically or smoothly translate into policy decisions.

Beyond this cultural gap, a distinct set of barriers further impedes the use of research in policy making: political and ideological factors; broader social, economic, and cultural trends; policy and scientific uncertainty, given that the broad nature of many policy objectives leaves room for value-laden interpretation; differing conceptions of risk between researchers and policy audiences; the perceived utility of the research; the timing of its availability relative to the policy window; and issues of communication and researcher reputation.

  • Researchers and policy makers differ in work culture, accountability, priorities, and values (Two Communities Model)
  • Barriers to research use include political/ideological factors, uncertainty, differing risk conceptions, timing, and communication

Key readings: Research, Evidence and Policy lecture note

Hierarchies and Typologies of Evidence

Public health decision making has traditionally relied on a "hierarchy of evidence" that ranks study designs by decreasing internal validity, typically placing systematic reviews and meta-analyses at the top, followed by randomised controlled trials with definitive results, randomised controlled trials with non-definitive results, cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional surveys, and case reports at the bottom. This hierarchy was developed initially to help decide priorities when searching for clinical evidence and was later adapted for public health decision making, asking not only "does it work?" but also "is it worth it?"

However, the appropriateness of applying a single fixed hierarchy to public health and social interventions has been increasingly questioned. Critics such as Petticrew and Roberts argue that evidence from observational studies does not always produce inflated effect estimates relative to randomised trials, and that in some circumstances the hierarchy may even be inverted, for example placing qualitative research methods above experimental designs when the research question concerns process, acceptability, or salience rather than effectiveness alone. This has led to proposals for a typology of evidence rather than a strict hierarchy: a matrix approach that matches specific research questions (effectiveness, process of service delivery, salience, safety, acceptability, cost-effectiveness, appropriateness, and satisfaction) to the study designs best suited to answering them, drawing on qualitative research, surveys, case-control and cohort studies, randomised controlled trials, quasi-experimental and non-experimental evaluations, and systematic reviews as appropriate to the question at hand. This methodological-appropriateness perspective recognises that randomised trials and qualitative research are often synergistic rather than competing, particularly in social and public health interventions where robust evidence of outcomes and understanding of mechanism are both needed.

  • Classic hierarchy: systematic reviews/meta-analyses at top, case reports at bottom
  • Hierarchy is contested as rigid and not universally applicable to public health/social interventions
  • Typology (matrix) approach matches research question type to the most appropriate study design

Key readings: Evidence, Hierarchies, and Typologies (Petticrew & Roberts, 2003); Study Designs in Epidemiology and Levels of Evidence; Evaluating the Evidence: Is There a Rigid Hierarchy

Bradford Hill's Guidelines for Causation

Sir Austin Bradford Hill proposed a widely cited pre-evidence-based-medicine framework of factors to consider before concluding that an observed association is causal rather than spurious. Bradford Hill was explicit that none of these guidelines is individually necessary or sufficient to establish causation; rather, they are factors to be weighed together. A modern re-examination of the guidelines by Howick, Glasziou, and Aronson reorganises them into three broader categories: direct evidence from studies (randomised or non-randomised) that an association is causal and not spurious, which includes considerations such as dose-responsiveness and reversibility, and temporality (the cause must precede the effect); mechanistic evidence for the biological or causal process linking intervention and outcome, including biological plausibility; and parallel evidence supporting the causal hypothesis from related studies, including coherence, replicability (consistency), and similarity (analogy) with other established relationships.

This framework is particularly relevant to public health because high-quality randomised controlled trials are not always feasible, ethical, or available, yet causal judgements are still required, for example for interventions such as smoke alarms, seatbelts, or the Heimlich manoeuvre that have never been tested in randomised trials but are nonetheless strongly supported as effective. Evidence-grading systems that place randomised trials automatically at the top of a hierarchy risk misleading conclusions in such cases, reinforcing the argument that causal inference should draw on the totality of directness, mechanism, and coherence with the wider evidence base rather than study design alone.

  • Bradford Hill's guidelines are considered together, not treated as a checklist of necessary/sufficient criteria
  • Temporality is treated as essential; other guidelines include biological plausibility, coherence, consistency/replicability, and analogy/similarity
  • Useful where randomised controlled trials are infeasible, unethical, or unavailable

Key readings: What Can Bradford Hill's Guidelines for Causation Contribute (Howick, Glasziou & Aronson)

Evidence-Informed Health Policy

Evidence-informed health policy describes processes that, in contrast with traditional approaches relying heavily on expert opinion, demand systematic and transparent approaches to accessing, synthesising, and interpreting research evidence, and to integrating that evidence with other information, values, and judgements in order to formulate recommendations or make decisions. International attention to this agenda intensified following the World Health Organization's World Report on Knowledge for Better Health and the subsequent World Health Assembly resolution calling on member states to establish or strengthen mechanisms to transfer knowledge in support of evidence-based public health, health-care delivery systems, and health-related policies.

Organisations that support the use of research evidence in policy, such as those producing clinical practice guidelines or health technology assessments, are found to succeed when they collaborate with other organisations, establish strong links with policy makers while involving stakeholders, remain independent and manage conflicts of interest, build internal capacity, use good methods transparently, start with a clear audience and scope focused on important questions, and remain attentive to implementation considerations even where implementation is not formally their remit.

  • Evidence-informed policy requires systematic, transparent synthesis of evidence combined with values and judgement
  • WHO resolutions have called for member states to strengthen knowledge-transfer mechanisms into policy
  • Success factors for evidence-support organisations include independence, stakeholder engagement, and methodological transparency

Key readings: Evidence-Informed Health Policy (Lavis, Oxman, Moynihan & Paulsen); Evidence for Health Decision Making; What Constitutes Good Evidence for Public Health and Social Policy Making: From Hierarchies to Appropriateness

Policy and Health Equity

Disease Causation, Lifecourse, and Developmental Origins of Health and Disease

Understanding health inequity begins with an understanding of disease causation that extends beyond proximate biomedical factors to social determinants of health and a lifecourse perspective. David Barker's hypothesis, also known as the foetal origins of adult disease or biological programming hypothesis, proposed that low birth weight is associated with the early development of ischaemic heart disease, and this association has since been extended to diabetes mellitus and metabolic syndrome. The proposed mechanism is that environmental exposures, such as nutrition during the foetal critical growth period, may have a long-term effect on adult chronic disease by "programming" the structure and function of organs, tissues, and systems in utero.

This has developed into the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) framework, which points to epigenetic change, molecular pathways controlling gene expression and inducing specific phenotypes without altering the underlying DNA sequence, through mechanisms including DNA methylation, histone covalent modifications, and noncoding RNA expression. These changes can be tissue-specific, can vary in their sensitive period and expression time, and can in some cases be passed on transgenerationally if germ cells are involved. Current evidence draws on maternal nutrition in historical cohorts affected by famine and on environmental agents, including endocrine-disrupting chemicals and exposures such as thalidomide and rubella during pregnancy. A key critique of the DOHaD/Barker approach is that it can remain narrowly biomedical and lack social context: low birth weight is more prevalent among groups of low socioeconomic status, and is shaped by ethnicity, maternal health status, access to quality nutrition, and smoking or alcohol use during pregnancy.

Ben-Shlomo and Kuh's lifecourse perspective offers four causal models linking early exposures to later health outcomes: the critical period model, in which an insult at a critical period causes permanent effects; the critical period model with later effect modifiers, in which exposures later in life either enhance (synergism) or diminish (antagonism) the outcome; the accumulation of risk model, in which risks accumulate gradually across the lifecourse; and the chain of risk (pathway) model, a variant of accumulation in which a sequence of linked exposures leads to the outcome. These models are not mutually exclusive and may operate simultaneously, which makes them difficult to distinguish empirically and complicates the design of standardised interventions.

  • Barker's hypothesis: foetal/early-life exposures "program" long-term chronic disease risk
  • DOHaD: epigenetic mechanisms (DNA methylation, histone modification, noncoding RNA) link early exposure to later disease
  • Critique: DOHaD can be overly biomedical, underplaying social determinants such as SES, ethnicity, and maternal health behaviours
  • Ben-Shlomo & Kuh's four lifecourse models: critical period, critical period with effect modifiers, accumulation of risk, and chain of risk

Key readings: Health Equity and Policy lecture note; Wadhwa et al. (2009), Developmental Origins of Health and Disease: brief history and current focus on epigenetic mechanisms

Inequality versus Inequity

A foundational distinction in this field is between health inequality and health inequity. Inequality is a generic, descriptive term for differences, variations, or disparities in health achievements between individuals and groups; it is a dimensional concept, expressed in measurable quantities. Inequity, by contrast, is defined by Whitehead (2007) as "systematic differences in health status between different socioeconomic groups" that are "systematic, socially produced and unfair." Inequity is thus a political concept, expressing a moral commitment to social justice rather than a purely descriptive one. This distinction matters for policy because not every measurable health difference constitutes an injustice requiring remedy; the question is whether the difference is systematic, socially produced, and unfair given the wider social context.

Three dimensions define an inequity as such: it must be systematic, meaning it follows a consistent pattern across the population rather than being randomly distributed; it must be socially produced, meaning it arises from social processes rather than being purely biologically determined; and it must be unfair, meaning it is generated and maintained by "unjust social arrangements" that offend common notions of fairness, with the cause examined and judged as unfair in the context of what is happening in the rest of society.

  • Inequality: descriptive, measurable difference in health outcomes between groups
  • Inequity: systematic, socially produced, and unfair difference (Whitehead, 2007), a normative/political concept
  • Three markers of inequity: systematic pattern, social production, and unfairness

Key readings: Health Equity and Policy lecture note; the concepts and principles of equity and health

Social Justice Theories and Principles of Equity

Social justice is presented not as a fixed "thing" but as an ongoing series of relationships permeating everyday life, concerned with the systematic treatment of people as members of a definable group. It demands the equitable distribution of collective goods, institutional resources, and life opportunities, drawing on Amartya Sen's (1992) notion of a just society as one that ensures the development of the capacities of all its members, and it calls for democracy, that is, the empowerment of all social members alongside democratic and transparent structures to promote social goals and political equality.

Four competing theories of social justice inform how equity can be conceived: utilitarianism, which strives for "the greatest happiness for the greatest number" in the distribution of benefits; egalitarianism, in which everyone should have an equal opportunity to obtain benefits; libertarianism, in which individuals have rights, particularly to freedom, that the state must not violate, and the state should not interfere; and Rawlsianism, which accepts a trade-off between efficiency and equity such that an unequal distribution of benefits can be acceptable provided society maximises the benefits available to the poorest person (the maximin principle). Equity in public health can be operationalised in several distinct ways, including equal health outcomes, equal access to care for equal need, equal use of health care for equal need, equal expenditure or resources for equal need, and equal costs to the payer for equal need. Two further principles apply in health care specifically: vertical equity, whereby individuals with different or unequal health needs are treated differently in proportion to morally relevant factors such as need, ability to benefit, or autonomy; and horizontal equity, the equal treatment of individuals or groups who share similar circumstances.

  • Four theories of social justice: utilitarianism, egalitarianism, libertarianism, Rawlsianism (maximin)
  • Equity can be applied via equal outcomes, equal access, equal use, or equal expenditure for equal need
  • Vertical equity: proportionally different treatment for different needs; horizontal equity: equal treatment for similar circumstances

Key readings: Health Equity and Policy lecture note; Policy Approaches to Advancing Health Equity

Evidence Base and Measurement of Health Inequalities

The evidence base documenting health inequalities draws on landmark reports such as the Black Report and the Health Divide, the Acheson Report, the Marmot Review, and reports from the European Union and World Health Organization, which collectively describe a social gradient in health status running along lines of socioeconomic group, gender, and ethnicity, observable both within countries and between countries. The WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health, and the work of Whitehead and Dahlgren, are central reference points for this evidence base. Measurement of inequity, however, is not straightforward: it relies on vital statistics and other data sources, raises questions about the measurability of illness, disease, disability, or death (as discussed by Sen), and depends on the choice of reference group against which comparisons are made. There is no single agreed measure of health equity.

  • Key evidence sources: Black Report/Health Divide, Acheson Report, Marmot Review, WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health
  • Social gradient in health observed by socioeconomic group, gender, and ethnicity, within and between countries
  • Measurement challenges: choice of reference group, data limitations, no single standard measure

Key readings: Health Equity and Policy lecture note; WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health report

Health Equity Impact Assessment and Whitehead's Equity Action Spectrum

Health Equity Impact Assessment (HEIA) is a structured tool used to examine the differential impact of a proposed policy, program, or project on population subgroups, so that unintended negative effects on equity can be identified and mitigated before implementation. Health equity itself is described by the World Health Organization as the absence of avoidable or remediable differences among groups of people, whether those groups are defined socially, economically, demographically, or geographically. Applying an appropriate HEIA tool, addressing structural barriers to health equity, and embedding equity principles into routine operations are presented as complementary strategies for translating the equity commitment into practice.

Whitehead's equity action spectrum sets out principles for organising action on health equity: securing political commitment and cooperation from multiple sectors; assessing the extent of the problem, the gaps in information, potential intervention strategies, and the barriers to action; weighing the best organisational arrangements and financial requirements and designating responsibility and resources accordingly; and drawing up action plans at all levels specifying what actions will be taken, by whom, what budget will be allocated, and what output is expected. The WHO's "Closing the Gap in a Generation" report translates these principles into concrete priority areas, including equity from the start (early childhood), healthy places and healthy people, fair employment and decent work, social protection across the lifecourse, universal health care, health equity in all policies, systems and programmes, fair financing, market responsibility, gender equity, political empowerment, and good global governance.

  • HEIA: structured assessment of a policy's differential impact on population subgroups
  • WHO definition of health equity: absence of avoidable or remediable differences among groups
  • Whitehead's spectrum: political commitment, problem assessment, resourcing, and detailed action plans
  • Closing the Gap in a Generation: eleven priority action areas spanning early childhood to global governance

Key readings: Health Equity Impact Assessment (Canadian Public Health Association); Whitehead, The Equity Action Spectrum: Taking a Comprehensive Approach; Closing the Gap in a Generation (WHO); WHO Health Equity Policy Tool

Social Context and Health in All Policies

Health inequity is shaped by a broader social context encompassing political, cultural, social, and economic factors, which links directly to the WHO's Health in All Policies approach and the Rio Political Declaration on Social Determinants of Health. This perspective recognises health equity as a global issue requiring action from actors well beyond the health sector, and situates health outcomes within intersectoral governance structures rather than treating health policy as a self-contained domain. At the European level, priority areas for equitable health and social protection systems are organised around three interlinked themes: the lifecourse, living and working conditions, and equitable health and social protection systems themselves, reflecting the same lifecourse and social-determinants logic that runs throughout this module.

  • Health in All Policies: health equity requires intersectoral action beyond the health sector
  • Rio Political Declaration on Social Determinants of Health as a global reference point
  • European priorities: lifecourse, living/working conditions, and equitable health and social protection systems

Key readings: Health in All Policies Guide; Global Public Health Leaders of Tomorrow: What is at Stake; Equity-Oriented National Strategy for Public Health in Sweden

Ethics in Public Health Research

Defining Public Health Research

Public health research focuses on investigating and addressing health-related issues at the population level, with the primary goal of improving the health and well-being of communities and societies by understanding the factors that influence health, preventing diseases and injuries, and promoting healthy behaviours and policies. Its findings are used to inform public health policies, shape health-care practices, and guide interventions aimed at improving population health and reducing health inequalities. Public health research studies population health, well-being, disability, and loss of independence, together with the biological, behavioural, social, or environmental factors that determine health status, and it develops and assesses interventions to improve population health, prevent disease, compensate for disabilities, and drive organisational innovation in health, social, and medical/social services. Unlike much clinical research, it typically occurs outside a controlled environment, often in real-world social, political, and economic contexts using pragmatic designs, and may involve interventions with whole communities or populations rather than individual patients.

Epidemiology, the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations and the application of that study to the prevention and control of health problems, underpins much of this work through both descriptive studies (documenting disease occurrence and risk factors) and analytical studies (examining etiology, causality, or association, evaluating the effectiveness of public health interventions, and assessing public health systems).

  • Population-level focus distinguishes public health research from individual-level clinical research
  • Real-world, pragmatic designs, often involving whole communities rather than individual patients
  • Descriptive epidemiology (occurrence, risk factors) and analytical epidemiology (etiology, intervention effectiveness) both fall within its scope

Key readings: Ethics in Public Health Research course notes; Public Health Ethics: Cases Spanning the Globe (Barrett, Ortmann, Brown, DeCausey, Saenz & Dawson, 2016)

Distinguishing Public Health Research from Public Health Practice

A recurring ethical and regulatory question is how to distinguish public health research from non-research public health practice, since this distinction determines whether formal research ethics review and informed consent requirements apply. Public health practice activities are characterised by benefits that focus on the activity's own participants, collection of data limited to what is needed to improve the activity or the health of those participants, and generation of knowledge that does not extend beyond the scope of the activity itself. Public health research, by contrast, is characterised by benefits that extend beyond the needs of the study participants, collection of data that exceeds what is needed to care for those participants, and generation of knowledge with relevance outside the specific population from which the data were collected. Data originally generated through public health practice can subsequently be used as research data, which raises its own set of ethical questions.

Three commonly used comparisons help operationalise this distinction: public health surveillance versus research that uses surveillance data; emergency responses, including outbreak response, versus research conducted during outbreaks or disasters; and evaluation of a public health program versus a public health interventional study. Historic guidance for making this determination includes the CDC's Guidelines for Defining Public Health Research and Public Health Non-Research.

  • Practice: benefits and data collection limited to participants themselves; knowledge stays within the scope of the activity
  • Research: benefits and knowledge extend beyond study participants; data collection exceeds immediate care needs
  • Key comparisons: surveillance vs. research using surveillance data; emergency response vs. outbreak research; program evaluation vs. interventional study

Key readings: Ethics in Public Health Research course notes; CDC, Guidelines for Defining Public Health Research and Public Health Non-Research (1999)

Ethical Justification of Public Health Research

Public health research is ethically justified on the grounds that it provides the scientific basis for the practice of public health, provides information needed to carry out effective policies and programs that protect and promote health, and leads to the insights and innovations that solve health problems. Because this research often takes place in real-world settings shaped by social, political, and economic context, and may involve interventions with selected or whole communities and populations, its ethical justification and its ethical challenges are closely intertwined: the same features that make the research valuable (population-level, real-world scope) also make its ethical governance more complex than that of controlled clinical research.

Research ethics in the public health context accordingly focuses more on interaction with communities and populations than on the individual patient-physician or research participant-researcher relationship typical of clinical research. Gaining consent may need to involve more than individuals, for example a school authority acting on behalf of students, and key considerations include that communities may gain benefits (direct or indirect) while also bearing risks, that not only individuals but entire populations may be vulnerable, and that the social, political, and economic context in which the research takes place may itself pose ethical challenges.

  • Justification rests on generating the scientific and informational basis for effective public health policy and programs
  • Ethical focus shifts from individual dyadic relationships to community- and population-level interaction
  • Both individuals and whole populations can be considered vulnerable in this context

Key readings: Ethics in Public Health Research course notes; Public Health Research Ethics (2025)

Informed Consent, Vulnerable Groups, and the Belmont Principles

The Belmont principles for biomedical research, including respect for persons, provide the ethical foundation for informed consent in public health research. Respect for persons means appreciating individuals' autonomy to make an informed decision to participate in research voluntarily; as the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) states, participation by individuals capable of giving informed consent as research subjects must be voluntary. For those whose capacity to make decisions is compromised, such as children or individuals with impaired cognitive function, surrogate consent applies, and researchers must identify who counts as a legally authorised representative within the relevant society. Children are additionally required to give assent, calibrated to their age and understanding: no assent is required from birth to under seven years old, verbal assent is obtained from seven to under twelve, a simplified assent form is used from twelve to under fifteen, and adolescents aged fifteen to under eighteen co-sign the informed consent form together with their parents.

The process of obtaining informed consent requires communicating with the potential participant using appropriate and understandable language, giving the participant an opportunity to ask questions, assessing and ensuring the participant's understanding of the research information, providing consent documents in the participant's native language, and ensuring the consent process itself is conducted by a researcher able to communicate with the participant in that language. Research conducted within institutional, geographical, or ethnic settings, such as schools, raises additional considerations: multiple stages of permission or "consent" may be required (for example, involving the school, the Ministry of Education, or the parents of children), gatekeepers may have varying familiarity with research, participants may be vulnerable simply by virtue of being at the selected institution, voluntariness requires a genuine opportunity to opt out of an interventional or control group, and strong social hierarchies can render participants vulnerable to being disallowed from participating, or pushed to participate, for the benefit of someone higher in that hierarchy.

  • Respect for persons and voluntary informed consent are grounded in the Declaration of Helsinki (2013)
  • Child assent requirements are age-graded: none under 7, verbal 7-under 12, simplified form 12-under 15, co-signed consent 15-under 18
  • Institutional/community research (e.g. schools) requires multi-stage permission and attention to social hierarchy and voluntariness

Key readings: Ethics in Public Health Research course notes; Declaration of Helsinki (2013)

Privacy, Consent Waivers, and Institutional Review Board Oversight

Protection of privacy and confidentiality is a core ethical obligation: the Declaration of Helsinki (2013, Paragraph 24) states that every precaution must be taken to protect the privacy of research subjects and the confidentiality of their personal information. This extends to protecting data owners from intrusion or observation without permission, including in surveillance and population-based survey data, and covers practices such as data de-identification, anonymisation, and awareness of "deducible information" that could indirectly re-identify a participant, including information inadvertently exposed during focus group discussions. A breach of data privacy risks stigmatising or discriminating against individuals or groups. When identifiable private information is subsequently analysed, questions arise about which parties, including the Institutional Review Board (IRB), have access to that information once it has been collected by the researchers.

Consent may be waived, at the discretion of the IRB, for a study of an intervention affecting an entire community if research participants are at minimal risk; if it would not be feasible to complete the standard consent process, for instance for a community-level intervention such as water fluoridation, in which case the community should still be engaged through meetings, awareness campaigns, and open communication channels with researchers, with consent obtained from community leaders or heads of household; and if the ethics committee has conducted a risk-benefit analysis and determined that waiving consent does not increase the risk of harm to participants, as may occur in research conducted during emergencies, epidemics, or natural disasters where the intervention or outcome time frame is narrow.

  • Confidentiality obligations extend to surveillance and population-level survey data, not only clinical records
  • De-identification and awareness of deducible/indirectly identifying information are essential safeguards
  • Consent waivers require IRB-conducted risk-benefit analysis and are typically limited to minimal-risk, community-level, or emergency research contexts

Key readings: Ethics in Public Health Research course notes; Declaration of Helsinki (2013, Paragraph 24)

Justice, Outbreak Research, and Emerging Research Methods

Justice in public health research concerns the fair distribution of the burdens and potential benefits of research participation. The Declaration of Helsinki (2013, Paragraph 13) states that groups underrepresented in medical research should be provided appropriate access to participate in research. In practice, this requires equal opportunity to participate, recognising that those who are difficult to reach or are marginalised are less likely to be invited or to participate, that sub-populations with the greatest health needs should receive priority for participation and benefit, and that where research is approved through a community or organisational leader, careful attention must be paid to how that leader influences the study, including whether the leader decides who is recruited and whether the leader has authority to prevent certain individuals from participating.

Research conducted during disease outbreaks is justified by the need to respond adequately to local realities, but carries specific concerns: it should not compromise the public health response or drain critical health-related resources, new treatments and preventive measures still require sufficient safety and efficacy data through ethical risk assessment, research must be clearly distinguished from the provision of care, there is a risk of disease exposure and transmission between research staff and participants during data collection, accelerated ethics review may be required, and equitable access to study findings must be ensured. A further ethical consideration across public health research generally is that research conducted with flawed methodology is itself unethical, since it wastes resources, subjects participants to procedures unlikely to be meaningful or beneficial, and can produce misleading results; this is compounded by the fact that public health research uses a wide variety of study designs and methodologies, not all of which are the standard parallel-group comparisons typical of clinical trials.

Newer research approaches, such as "armchair research" using social media data, introduce additional ethical questions. Social media users indirectly provide data through their platform interactions, and researchers may gather this information through crowd-sourcing strategies, often without access to raw, personally identifiable data and at low cost, for qualitative or quantitative analysis. Ethical issues specific to this approach include verifying the age of online users and obtaining parental consent where minors are involved (research involving minors cannot be considered exempt), the waiver of consent documentation, privacy, security, and confidentiality of online data, and the lack of face-to-face contact and physical interaction when obtaining online informed consent.

  • Justice requires equitable access to participation and prioritisation of populations with greatest health need
  • Outbreak research must not divert critical resources from the public health response and must be clearly separated from care provision
  • Flawed methodology is itself an ethical violation, not merely a technical shortcoming
  • Social media/"armchair" research raises novel issues of age verification, consent waiver, and lack of face-to-face contact

Key readings: Ethics in Public Health Research course notes; Declaration of Helsinki (2013, Paragraph 13); Facilitating the Ethical Use (of public health data/methods)
