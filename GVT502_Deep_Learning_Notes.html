1. Sanitation and Hygiene Behavioural Change
Deep-dive learning notes · compiled from lecture material (anonymised) · DrPH specialist level
📚Overview

This topic covers the definitional foundations of sanitation and wastewater terminology, the global SDG6/JMP monitoring architecture, the burden of disease attributable to poor sanitation and hygiene (with a strong Malaysian/Orang Asli evidence base on intestinal parasitic infection), and a six-domain strategy framework for improving sanitation and hygiene, including Malaysia-specific implementation challenges.

📋Key Definitions & Concepts
  • Sanitation — the provision of facilities and services for the safe disposal of human urine and faeces (WHO, 2017).
  • Wastewater — water discharged by domestic residences, commercial properties, industry and agriculture that has been adversely affected in quality by anthropogenic influence; contains contaminants from multiple sources.
  • Sewage — mainly liquid waste containing some solids produced by humans (washing water, faeces, urine, laundry waste and other material entering drains/toilets from households and industry); technically a subset of wastewater, though the term is often used loosely to mean any wastewater.
  • Sullage — used water from washing clothes/kitchen utensils, showering/bathing and other domestic water not containing excreta; composed of 99.94% water.
  • Sewerage — the physical infrastructure (sewers) that conveys sewage and surface runoff (stormwater, rainwater).
  • Related terms named in the slides: black water, compost, excreta, storm water, grey water.
🔬SDG6 and the WHO/UNICEF JMP Monitoring Framework
  • SDG Target 6.2: by 2030, achieve access to adequate and equitable sanitation and hygiene for all and end open defecation, with special attention to the needs of women, girls and those in vulnerable situations.
  • The WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) for Water Supply, Sanitation and Hygiene is the international mechanism for tracking SDG6 progress, using an "improved vs unimproved" facility classification (and further ladders — basic/safely managed).
  • JMP reporting on Malaysia was drawn from two reference points in the slides: the WHO 2013 report and the WHO 2020 report, alongside a global conclusion section summarising worldwide sanitation and hand-hygiene service status.
  • Malaysia made a formal statement to the United Nations in 2005 on its sanitation status, and a separate Malaysia sanitation assessment was conducted by the Japan Sanitation Consortium in 2011.
  • Septage (septic tank sludge) — a by-product of household wastewater pretreatment in a septic tank, accumulating over time; one type of faecal sludge, distinguished from sludge accumulating in pit latrines.
📊Burden of Disease Linked to Poor Sanitation/Hygiene

Diarrhoeal disease

  • Identified in the slides as the most important faecal-oral disease globally.
  • 1.6-2.5 million deaths annually, the majority in children under 5 in developing countries (Lopez, 2006).
  • Common paediatric diarrhoeal pathogens named: Rotavirus, Enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), Enteropathogenic E. coli (EPEC), Shigella spp., Campylobacter jejuni.
  • A systematic review (Waddington & Snilstveit, 2009) found improved sanitation reduces diarrhoeal illness by 32-37%.
  • Malaysian under-5 mortality context (MOH, 2008 data cited) and global "Causes of Death among Children Under 5, 2017" data were both presented as framing evidence.

Tropical/parasitic disease burden

  • Poor sanitation drives substantial disability-adjusted life year (DALY) loss in developing countries, largely via the faeco-oral transmission pathway.
  • The slides note that global policy responses have disproportionately emphasised medication-based treatment over sanitation-based prevention.

Schistosomiasis (water-based infection)

  • ~190 million infected globally.
  • Vector: aquatic snails; transmission via urine/faeces contaminating water sources.
  • Health impact: haematuria, impaired growth, bladder and colorectal cancers, vital organ malfunction.
  • Sanitation and water interventions are essential for long-term control and elimination.

Indirect effects - undernutrition and stunting

  • Three underlying causes identified: poor quality/quantity of food, poor care practices, infectious disease.
  • Main pathways: repeated diarrhoeal episodes, soil-transmitted helminth infections, and environmental enteropathy.
  • Poor sanitation, hygiene and water access are stated to be responsible for ~50% of the consequences of childhood and maternal underweight, reflecting a synergism between diarrhoeal disease and undernutrition.
⚖️Evidence on Intervention Effectiveness
  • Fewtrell et al. (2005), Lancet Infectious Diseases - systematic review/meta-analysis of water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) interventions to reduce diarrhoea in less-developed countries, comparing five intervention categories: (1) hygiene, (2) sanitation, (3) water supply, (4) water quality, (5) multiple/combined interventions.
  • Key findings (from slide notes): handwashing was the single most effective intervention in reducing illness, followed by point-of-use water treatment; hygiene interventions act by reducing contamination of hands, food, water and fomites and are at least as effective as other single interventions; water-quality (point-of-use treatment) interventions were found to be more effective than earlier reviews suggested; multiple/combined interventions were not more effective than single-focus interventions - the effect of combining interventions does not appear to be additive.
🏥Six-Domain Strategy Framework to Improve Sanitation & Hygiene
  1. Political leadership - establishing clear institutional responsibility and accountability for sanitation; raising sanitation on policy-maker agendas; strengthening cross-agency collaboration (health, water resources, utilities); cross-country learning. The Sanitation and Water for All (SWA) global partnership (over 250 country governments, private sector, civil society, external support agencies, research institutions) is named as the vehicle for catalysing political leadership, improving accountability and using resources more effectively.
  2. Increased public awareness / health promotion - raising public understanding of the importance of sanitation, hygiene and clean water.
  3. Sustainable sanitation systems - improving the efficiency of Malaysia's sewerage system via regulatory monitoring against key performance indicators; improving financing through better bill-payment enforcement, fair charging, and innovative cost-reducing technologies.
  4. Targeting vulnerable groups - children under 5 (via parent/teacher education); UNICEF's WASH (Water, Sanitation and Hygiene) programme, including school handwashing promotion; improving rural/Orang Asli access via the BAKAS (Bekalan Air dan Kebersihan Alam Sekitar) unit.
  5. Behavioural change - framed via the WHO/UNICEF JMP behaviour-change lens (slide title only; specific behaviour-change theory not elaborated in the source material).
  6. Emergency/disaster preparedness - anticipating disaster-specific vulnerabilities across the disaster cycle: prevention/mitigation (relocating people from hazard zones where feasible, or otherwise reducing future hazard impact), emergency preparedness (long-term development activity, e.g. ensuring planned shelters have sanitation/clean water access), response (disease screening, provision of clean water and sanitation access), and recovery.
🛡️Malaysia-Specific Challenges & Barriers (five named)
  1. Reducing inequalities of access - urban vs rural disparities; Orang Asli communities in mountain/jungle areas with limited improved-sanitation access and poor hygiene practice; illegal immigrants (from Indonesia, Bangladesh and other countries) living in packed/overcrowded conditions with limited sanitation access; refugees (including Rohingya) in refugee centres; broader vulnerable groups include displaced populations and returnees.
  2. Sustainability and maintenance of the sewerage system - sewerage in Malaysia is provided/maintained by Indah Water Konsortium (IWK); government currently funds ~10% of IWK's budget, with the remainder charged to consumers; only ~85% of consumers pay their IWK bills, and IWK requests an additional RM300 million from government annually.
  3. Keeping pace with population growth - Malaysia's continuing positive population growth requires sanitation system capacity to expand proportionately.
  4. Raising awareness and behavioural change, particularly among vulnerable groups.
  5. Emergency resilience - flooding: flood is identified as Malaysia's most common natural disaster, with severe health implications before, during and after an event; during floods there is limited access to improved sanitation and clean water, shared latrines and limited excreta disposal, crowding and poor hygiene, and elevated risk of faecal-oral communicable disease transmission. The 2014 Kelantan flood is cited as producing 162 acute gastroenteritis (AGE) cases.
📈Key Guidelines, Standards & Frameworks for Good Practice
  • WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) service ladder for water, sanitation and hygiene - named in the slides; the primary international framework for classifying and comparing sanitation access.
  • Sustainable Development Goal 6, Target 6.2 - named in the slides as the global policy anchor for universal, equitable sanitation and hygiene access.
  • Sanitation and Water for All (SWA) global partnership - named in the slides as the political-leadership catalysing mechanism.
  • UNICEF WASH (Water, Sanitation and Hygiene) programme - named in the slides as the operational vehicle for school/child-focused hygiene promotion.
  • Supplementary standard (not named in the slides): WHO Guidelines on Sanitation and Health (2018), which provide health-based recommendations for safe excreta management, sanitation systems, and safe use of wastewater/excreta in agriculture.
  • Supplementary reference (not named in the slides): Sphere Handbook minimum standards for WASH in humanitarian response, directly relevant to the disaster/emergency-preparedness strategy domain discussed.
💩Framework Deep-Dive: Key Published Frameworks Explained

This section unpacks the major published, internationally recognised frameworks referenced above in more depth, for readers who want to understand not just the name but the structure and mechanics of each framework.

WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) Service Ladder - Publisher: WHO and UNICEF jointly, ongoing since 1990, methodology substantially revised for the SDG era (2017 update). Purpose: the JMP is the official mechanism for global monitoring of SDG6 (water, sanitation, hygiene). Structure: it classifies household access along a "ladder" of increasingly stringent service levels. For sanitation the rungs are, from lowest to highest: open defecation -> unimproved -> limited -> basic -> safely managed. A "safely managed" sanitation service requires an improved facility not shared with other households, with excreta safely disposed of in situ or transported and treated off-site. The same ladder logic is applied separately to drinking water and to hygiene (handwashing) services. Application here: the lecture's "improved vs unimproved" classification and the Malaysia estimates cited from the WHO 2013/2020 reports are direct JMP outputs, and this ladder is the standard metric DrPH researchers use to operationalise "sanitation access" as a measurable exposure or outcome variable.

Sustainable Development Goal 6 (SDG6) and Target 6.2 - Publisher: United Nations General Assembly, adopted 2015 as part of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development. Structure: SDG6 ("Clean Water and Sanitation") has 8 targets and 11 indicators; Target 6.2 specifically commits to "achieve access to adequate and equitable sanitation and hygiene for all and end open defecation... by 2030," tracked via indicator 6.2.1 (population using safely managed sanitation services, including a handwashing facility with soap and water). Application here: SDG6.2 is the policy anchor cited in the lecture and is the standard reference point against which Malaysia's JMP-reported progress is benchmarked.

Sanitation and Water for All (SWA) - Publisher: a global multi-stakeholder partnership (not a single UN agency), hosted administratively by UNICEF, launched 2010. Structure: SWA operates through "Mutual Accountability Mechanisms" - biennial High-Level Meetings where governments, donors, and civil society make and track public commitments against four collaborative behaviours (enhancing government leadership, strengthening and using country systems, strengthening sector financing, and improving sector monitoring). Application here: the lecture cites SWA as the vehicle for "political leadership," the first of its six-domain improvement strategy; in DrPH policy-analysis research, SWA commitments are a useful documentary source for tracking country-level accountability.

🏷️Public Health Relevance

Sanitation and hygiene sit at the foundation of faecal-oral disease transmission control. The slides directly connect inadequate WASH access to under-5 mortality, DALY loss from tropical/parasitic disease, and childhood/maternal undernutrition, all recognised MOH Malaysia and SDG priority areas. Explicitly named vulnerable groups (Orang Asli, illegal immigrants, refugees, children under 5) represent populations with disproportionate exposure and limited access to remediation, making this a core equity issue within Malaysian environmental health practice.

  • A prospective cohort study following infants in WASH-intervention versus non-intervention Orang Asli villages to assess the effect of improved sanitation access on linear growth and stunting trajectories.
  • A mixed-methods qualitative study (framework analysis) exploring behavioural and structural determinants of open defecation and hygiene practice among refugee and undocumented migrant populations in urban Malaysia.
  • A scoping review synthesising Malaysian and regional evidence on the relationship between WASH access, intestinal parasitic infection, and stunting among indigenous populations.
  • A policy/regulatory impact analysis of IWK bill-payment enforcement reforms on sewerage system financing sustainability and downstream environmental health outcomes.

2. Waste Reduction and Recycling for Future Environment Sustainability
Deep-dive learning notes · compiled from lecture material (anonymised) · DrPH specialist level
📚Overview

This topic examines the full life cycle of municipal and industrial solid waste - its generation, classification, storage, collection, treatment, and disposal - and situates Malaysia's waste management performance within global and regional benchmarks. It links waste mismanagement to environmental degradation (air, water, soil, ecosystems) and human health outcomes (respiratory disease, vector-borne disease, cancer, occupational hazards), and then builds toward solutions: the waste hierarchy, the 3R/5R/Zero Waste frameworks, Malaysian policy instruments (Act 672, EPR, source separation, circular economy roadmap), and international case studies (Taiwan, Thailand) as models for reform.

📋Key Definitions & Concepts
  • Solid waste (US EPA definition): any garbage or refuse, sludge from a wastewater treatment plant, water supply treatment plant, or air pollution control facility, and other discarded material resulting from industrial, commercial, mining, and agricultural operations and from community activities. Not limited to physically solid material - many "solid wastes" are liquid, semi-solid, or contained gaseous material.
  • Waste (Malaysia, Environmental Quality Act 1974): includes any matter prescribed to be scheduled waste, or any matter - solid, semi-solid, liquid, gas or vapour - which is emitted, discharged or deposited in the environment in such volume, composition or manner as to cause pollution.
  • Solid waste management: the discipline associated with control of generation, storage, collection, transport/transfer, processing and disposal of solid waste materials in a way that best addresses public health, conservation, economic, aesthetic, engineering and other environmental considerations.
  • Municipal Solid Waste (MSW): everyday household, school and office waste - organic matter, paper, plastics, glass, metals - managed by local authorities (PBT) / SWCorp.
  • Waste reduction / source reduction: the practice of using less material and energy to minimise waste generation and preserve natural resources; broader in scope than recycling because it prevents materials from ever becoming waste (US EPA).
  • Recycling: the process of collecting and processing materials that would otherwise be discarded as trash, and turning them into new products or substances.
  • Zero Waste (Zero Waste International Alliance, ZWIA): goal of sending no trash to landfills, incinerators, or the ocean; requires a massive redesign of how materials flow through society, based on prevention principles that redesign resource life cycles so products are reused. Zero Waste Malaysia established 2016.
  • Leachate: the toxic liquid formed when water percolates through decomposing waste, carrying VOCs, nitrates and heavy metals; highly corrosive and capable of contaminating groundwater if containment fails.
  • NIMBY ("Not In My Backyard"): community opposition to new waste facility siting, a major barrier to landfill/incinerator expansion.
  • Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR): policy approach shifting end-of-life management cost/responsibility from taxpayers/government to producers (take-back schemes, deposit-refund, eco-fees).
  • Pay-As-You-Throw (PAYT): charging households based on the actual volume/weight of waste generated rather than a flat fee.
  • Wishful recycling: public habit of placing non-recyclable items into recycling bins in the hope they will be recycled - slows sorting and can damage MRF machinery.
  • Downcycling: progressive quality loss of a material (e.g., plastic) each time it is recycled, until it can no longer be recycled at all.
🔬Historical Disease-Hazard Framework (Hanks, 1967)
  • Hanks, R.W. (1967), "Disease hazard from solid waste," APWA Reporter, 34(3), 8-11 - a foundational model still referenced today, positing that to link solid waste to human disease requires: (1) discovery of harmful/potentially harmful agents in the waste; (2) demonstration that such agents develop within or in association with the waste; (3) discovery of disease/effects in an exposed population plausibly linked to these agents; (4) demonstration of the pathway(s) by which the effects occur; (5) demonstration that effects disappear when the pathway is interrupted, or are absent in unexposed populations.
  • Disease agents require an environmental association with the victim - direct (e.g. handling waste) or indirect (via a vector such as fly, mosquito, flea; via contaminated water supply; or via airborne particles).
  • Four principal exposure/hazard pathways: air (inhalation of gas/dust), water (leachate, surface water contamination), soil/food (direct contact, food-chain contamination), vector (insect/rodent bites).
  • Undesirable effects of solid waste classified into five types: biological (human pathogens in faeces), chemical (industrial waste/effluent), physical (fire/explosion from flammable materials), mechanical (injury from broken glass/sharp waste), psychological (nauseating smell, unpleasant sight of scavenging animals at dump sites).

Three main categories of solid waste (Ribic et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2018):

  1. Municipal Solid Waste (MSW) - everyday household/school/office waste; sub-types: general, bulky, kitchen, recyclables; managed by PBT/SWCorp.
  2. Industrial/Production Waste - generated during manufacturing; often chemical-containing; includes C&D waste; requires licensed operators.
  3. Hazardous Waste - harmful to health/environment if mishandled; identified via signal words (POISON, DANGER, WARNING, CAUTION); examples: pesticides, batteries, paint, motor oil; cannot go to regular landfills/incinerators.

Additional specialised streams: e-waste, biomedical/clinical waste, agricultural waste, radioactive waste.

E-waste:

  • 54 million tonnes generated globally per year (2019) - fastest-growing waste stream (UNEP, 2024).
  • Only ~20% formally recycled worldwide; 80% ends up in dumpsites.
  • Malaysia generated ~127,000 tonnes of e-waste in 2022, managed under the Environmental Quality Act 1974 (DOE Malaysia, 2022).
  • Hazards: lead, mercury, cadmium, brominated flame retardants - toxic if landfilled/openly burned; also contains recoverable materials (gold, copper, aluminium, iron).
  • Malaysia: 193 e-waste collection centres (DOE, 2023).

🏥Global and Malaysia Statistics - Scale of the Problem

Global (World Bank, 2018 What a Waste 2.0; UNEP, 2024 Global Waste Management Outlook; Challcharoenwattana & Pharino, 2015):

  • 2.1 billion tonnes MSW generated globally per year (2023) [note: the Conclusion slide separately cites 2.24 billion t/yr].
  • Projected 3.8 billion tonnes by 2050 - nearly double.
  • 38% of MSW uncontrolled - openly dumped or burned (2020).
  • 270,000 premature adult deaths/year from uncontrolled waste burning; 0.4-1 million deaths/year from poor waste management overall (WHO/UNEP, 2024).
  • High-income countries generate up to 2.1 kg/capita/day vs 0.6 kg/day in low-income countries.
  • World Bank (2022) What a Waste 2.0: 93% of waste in low-income nations ends up in open dumps vs only 3% in high-income countries.

Malaysia national context (Ministry of Housing & Local Government [MHLG], 2024; SWCorp, 2024; MIDA):

  • 15.2 million tonnes of waste generated in 2024, up from 14.25 million tonnes in 2023; projected to reach ~17 million tonnes by 2035.
  • Alternative dataset (MIDA/JPSPN): 13.95 million tonnes MSW in 2021 (~38,207 tonnes/day), up from 13.91 Mt (2020) and 13.88 Mt (2019); projected to reach 49,670 tonnes/day by 2030 (Tang et al., 2021).
  • National recycling rate: 37.9% (2024), up from 35.38% (2023); separately, SDG/VNR reporting cites 35.4% (2023); earlier JPSPN data reports 31.5% (2022) and 31.52% (2021).
  • Government recycling target: 40% by 2025; further targets 50% by 2030; Zero Waste to Landfill vision by 2050.
  • Malaysia classified as upper-middle income; waste collection rate ~82%.
  • Malaysia's waste sector contributes ~10 Mt CO2-eq/year (MENRE Biennial Update Report, 2022).
  • Per-capita waste generation rose from 0.8 kg/day (2000) to 1.17 kg/day (2022/2021).
  • SWCorp's 4-colour bin system: Blue = paper, Green = glass, Orange = metal, Red = plastics (note: a later slide describes a 3-stream/colour system - Blue = dry recyclables [paper/plastic/metal], Brown = organic/food waste, Green = glass - indicating some inconsistency in bin colour schemes across sources/years).
🛡️Key Drivers of Solid Waste Generation

(World Bank, 2018; UN, 2018 World Urbanization Prospects; UNEP, 2021 From Pollution to Solution)

  1. Population growth - world population 8.1 billion (2024); each additional person generates ~0.74 kg/day of MSW; urban population projected at 68% of world population by 2050.
  2. Urbanisation - urban residents generate 2x more waste than rural populations; Malaysia's urbanisation rate: 78% (2020).
  3. Economic development - rising GDP correlates with higher consumption/waste; high-income 2.1 kg/capita/day vs low-income 0.6 kg/day.
  4. Consumer culture - planned obsolescence, over-packaging, fast fashion, impulse buying ("throw-away society"); single-use plastics contribute ~300 million tonnes annually (UNEP, 2021).
  5. Industrialisation - industrial waste can be 3-4x the volume of municipal waste in rapidly developing economies.
  6. Weak governance - inadequate legislation, enforcement gaps, insufficient infrastructure, lack of financing.

Also noted separately: globalisation (export of waste/secondary materials to markets with lax environmental infrastructure, leading to crude handling and low-yield recycling).

📈Waste Management Hierarchy

Five-tier framework, from most to least preferred:

  1. Reduce - most preferred; prevent waste generation ("the best waste is the waste that never exists").
  2. Reuse - extend product life through repair, donation, repurposing.
  3. Recycle - transform materials into new products (still requires energy, water, processing).
  4. Recover - energy recovery via Waste-to-Energy (WtE), e.g. incineration generating electricity.
  5. Dispose - last resort: landfill / incineration without energy recovery.
  • Codified in the EU Waste Framework Directive (2008) - legally binding hierarchy for all EU member states.
  • Malaysia Act 672 (Solid Waste and Public Cleansing Management Act 2007) aligns with this framework: Prevent -> Prepare for reuse -> Recycle -> Other recovery (energy) -> Dispose.
  • Citation: Ribic et al. (2017), J. Air & Waste Management, 67(2), 241-259.
💩Extended 5R / Zero Waste Framework
  1. Refuse - decline what is not needed (single-use items, excess packaging, junk mail); most powerful prevention step.
  2. Reduce - minimise consumption; buy less/durable/local; since food waste = 44.5% of Malaysian MSW, meal planning reduces surplus.
  3. Reuse - repair, refill, borrow, share (Libraries of Things, clothing swaps, refill stations).
  4. Recycle - material recovery as fallback; correct sorting critical (contamination rejects whole batches); compost organic fraction.
  5. Repurpose/Recover - biological recycling of organics (aerobic composting -> soil amendment); Malaysia's potential: 2,500 tonnes/day divertable.

Citations: Bea Johnson (2013), Zero Waste Home; ZWIA (2023); SWCorp Malaysia (2022).

Additional applied "goal" typology from PDF source (source reduction taxonomy):

  • Preventive - stop production of unnecessary materials (e.g., ban plastic bags, promote paper alternatives).
  • Reduce - redesign products to use less virgin/hazardous material, extend life, avoid single-use.
  • Reuse - retain product function/original form; repair/refurbish; refill cartridges, use durable plates/mugs.
  • Recycle - turn waste into new substance/product (includes composting); requires energy/water/chemicals and proper infrastructure.
  • Recover - anaerobic digestion, incineration for energy recovery, gasification/pyrolysis producing biomass gas.
  • Dispose - landfill/incineration without energy recovery; unsustainable ("1 tonne of food waste = ~450 kg carbon emission").

⚡Volume Reduction - On-Site (Decentralised) Methods
  1. Compaction & baling - waste compressed in RCVs and baling facilities; recyclables (paper, cardboard, aluminium, plastics) pressed into dense blocks; reduces transport costs by up to 70%.
  2. Home composting - aerobic decomposition by bacteria, fungi and worms, producing humus; can divert up to 30% of household waste stream.
  3. Source reduction ("pre-cycling") - avoiding over-packaging, buying in bulk, using reusable containers; the most effective method because it prevents waste before formation.
  • Evidence: source reduction of edible food waste reduces GHG emissions by 103.1 million tonnes CO2e vs baseline - far more effective than recycling alone (Costello et al., 2017, Sustainability, 9(7), 1236).
  • Recycling aluminium saves 95% of energy vs virgin production (Waite, 1995).
📚Composting - Organic Waste Recovery
  • Process: collection -> shredding -> active composting -> curing -> finished compost.
  • Requirements: Carbon:Nitrogen ratio ~25-30:1 (browns = carbon; greens = nitrogen); Moisture 40-60% (like a wrung-out sponge), regular turning for aeration; Temperature 55-65C - kills pathogens and weed seeds; Time: 6-12 weeks to finished compost.
  • Benefits: diverts organic waste from landfill (~30% of MSW); prevents methane generation from buried organics; produces soil conditioner worth MYR 200-500/tonne; reduces need for chemical fertilisers.
  • Malaysia context: SWCorp's 'Komuniti Berkitar Semula' programme; MyBiocompost - national organic waste composting initiative; organic waste = ~45% of Malaysian MSW; target: divert 15% of organic waste by 2025 (12th Malaysia Plan).
📋Anaerobic Digestion (AD) & Biogas Recovery
  • Definition: decomposition of organic waste without oxygen, in a sealed digester (35-55C), via multi-stage bacterial breakdown.
  • Products: (1) Biogas (~60-70% methane) usable for electricity/heat via Combined Heat and Power (CHP) systems; (2) Digestate - nutrient-rich liquid residue usable as agricultural fertiliser.
  • AD vs Composting: AD captures energy as biogas; composting does not. AD is better suited to wet organic waste; composting is better for dry organic matter.
  • Malaysia AD projects: Ladang Tanah Merah, Pahang (food waste-to-biogas); Hospital Kuala Lumpur (biomedical waste AD); National Biomass Strategy 2020 (promotes AD from agricultural residues).
  • Global examples: Germany - 9,000+ AD plants processing food/agricultural waste; Taiwan - food waste steam-treated (75% used as pig feed, 25% composted); South Korea - 95% food waste diversion from landfills.
🔬Central Volume Reduction - Material Recovery Facility (MRF)

Sequential process flow for mixed waste sorting:

  1. Waste delivery
  2. Manual sorting - removes plastic film, textiles and hazardous items that could damage machinery.
  3. Trommel & screen - rotating drum separates by size (small items like glass fall through; cardboard continues on conveyor).
  4. Magnetic separation - overband magnet lifts ferrous metals (steel cans) off the belt; non-ferrous materials (aluminium) continue.
  5. Eddy current separator - spinning magnetic rotor generates forces that "throw" aluminium cans out of the waste stream.
  6. Optical/NIR sorter - infrared sensors identify polymer types (PET, HDPE, PVC) and glass colours; air jets divert materials to designated chutes.
  7. Baling & dispatch - sorted materials baled and sent to recycling industries.
  • Malaysia note: MRFs separating MSW into paper/metal/glass components are uncommon in Malaysia; the Kota Kinabalu City Hall MRF in Sabah is cited as the only such facility in one source (contrasted with a later claim of 9 operational MRFs nationally in 2023 - a discrepancy across sources/years reflecting different data vintages).
📊Solid Waste Disposal Methods
  1. Sanitary Landfill - engineered layers: compacted soil cover -> clay barrier layer -> HDPE plastic liner -> subgrade foundation. Leachate (VOCs, nitrates, heavy metals, corrosive pH) must be captured, monitored, treated; liner failure causes groundwater contamination. Methane (CH4) is a potent GHG generated by anaerobic decomposition, with fire/explosion risk; modern landfills harvest it for electricity. Post-1991 lined landfills must be monitored for 30 years post-closure; Malaysia's Act 672 mandates engineered design. NIMBY problem: community opposition (example - 7 of 121 Thai landfills suspended due to protests, 2012). Landfilling is the most common MSW disposal method in Malaysia, handling ~89% of total solid waste (MHLG, 2015); an estimated 65% of waste disposed in landfills is recyclable material (Samsudin & Mat Don, 2013).
  2. Incineration / Waste-to-Energy (WtE) - process: waste reception & pre-treatment -> combustion chamber at 850-1,000C -> boiler (heat recovery -> steam) -> turbine/generator (electricity) -> flue-gas treatment (scrubbers, fabric filters, activated carbon) -> ash residue (~10% of original volume). Pros: volume reduction ~90%, energy recovery, reduces methane emissions vs landfilling. Cons: high CAPEX, dioxin emission risk if poorly managed, "lock-in effect" (disincentivises recycling). Malaysia: several WtE projects planned (Kepong, Johor Bahru, Kuantan). National policy mandates that all clinical waste be incinerated; Malaysia has 5 regional medical waste incinerators (200-500 kg/h) and 7 smaller incinerators (20-50 kg/h).
  3. Open Dumping - illegal in Malaysia under Act 672, yet persists in informal settlements/rural areas with weak enforcement; very high leachate risk, very high GHG emissions, universally rejected by communities.

🛡️Structural Challenges to Waste Management (World Bank framework)
  1. Lack of sound management/organisation - ambiguous organisational structure/responsibility across government levels.
  2. Lack of financial resources - dedicated funding shortfalls, under-recovering fees, unenforced tariffs; in low-income countries SWM expenditure averages ~19% of municipal budgets.
  3. Lack of data management/information systems.
  4. Lack of public participation - Tadesse et al. (2008) found inadequate container supply/distance increases illegal dumping relative to communal container use.
  5. Informal sector (waste pickers) - serious challenge but often achieves notable recycling rates in developing countries; if it ceased, formal city systems would be overwhelmed.
  6. Urbanisation - waste composition shifts from mostly food waste (less urbanised) to more diverse paper/plastic waste (more urbanised).
  7. Globalisation - waste/secondary material export to markets with weak environmental infrastructure results in crude handling, low-yield recycling.
  8. Legislation and enforcement gaps - Malaysia's National Solid Waste Management Policy faced limited financing, low staff technical capacity, and ambiguous guidelines (Wee et al., 2017).

Contemporary Malaysian challenges (SWCorp 2023; MHLG 2022; World Bank 2022): low recycling rate 31.5% (2022) vs 50%+ in EU nations; infrastructure gap in rural areas; funding constraints (RM3.4 billion annual cost); source-separation compliance <15%; Act 672 under-enforced; per-capita waste rose from 0.8 to 1.17 kg/day (2000-2022).

Water & soil contamination (DOE Malaysia, 2022; WHO, 2021): leachate BOD 2,000-30,000 mg/L (vs WHO drinking-water limit of 2 mg/L); lead from e-waste up to 10x WHO soil quality guideline in dump sites; open dumps raise groundwater nitrate by 30-60 mg/L above background; microplastics detected in 81% of Malaysian river samples (2021); 14 Malaysian rivers classified Class IV-V (heavily polluted), partly attributable to leachate.

Air pollution (IPCC AR6 WGIII Ch. 11; MENRE Malaysia, 2022): Malaysia's waste sector ~10 Mt CO2-eq/year; open burning produces PM2.5 levels 19x above WHO guidelines plus carcinogenic dioxins.

📊Framework Deep-Dive: Key Published Frameworks Explained

EU Waste Framework Directive (2008/98/EC) and the Waste Hierarchy - Publisher: European Parliament and Council of the European Union, adopted 2008 (amended 2018). Structure: Article 4 legally codifies a five-step waste hierarchy - prevention, preparing for re-use, recycling, other recovery (e.g. energy recovery), and disposal - as the order of priority EU member states must apply in waste-prevention and management legislation/policy. It is not merely advisory: member states must justify departures from the hierarchy on life-cycle-thinking grounds. Application here: Malaysia's Act 672 explicitly mirrors this five-step ordering (Prevent -> Prepare for reuse -> Recycle -> Other recovery -> Dispose), making the EU Directive the intellectual template for Malaysia's national law, even though Malaysia is not an EU member and adopted the hierarchy voluntarily as international best practice.

Hanks (1967) Disease-Hazard Framework - Publisher: R.W. Hanks, published in the APWA (American Public Works Association) Reporter, 1967. Structure: a five-step causal-proof framework used to establish that solid waste causes human disease: (1) discovery of a harmful/potentially harmful agent in the waste; (2) demonstration that the agent develops within or in association with the waste; (3) discovery of disease/effects in an exposed population plausibly linked to the agent; (4) demonstration of the specific pathway(s) of effect; (5) demonstration that effects disappear when the pathway is interrupted, or are absent in unexposed groups. Application here: this is essentially a precursor to modern causal-inference criteria (similar in spirit to the later Bradford Hill criteria used in epidemiology); DrPH students can use it as a structured checklist when designing exposure-outcome studies linking waste exposure to specific disease endpoints.

Malaysia's National Solid Waste Management Policy (2016) and Act 672 (2007) - Publisher: Ministry of Housing and Local Government (MHLG) Malaysia / Parliament of Malaysia. Structure: Act 672 centralises solid waste management authority (previously a state matter) under a federal regulator (now SWCorp), mandates licensing of waste operators, and establishes offences for illegal dumping and non-compliant disposal; the 2016 Policy overlays quantitative targets (e.g. the 40%-by-2025 recycling rate) onto this legal base. Application here: because Act 672 was adopted piecemeal by state (fully enforced in only a subset of states as of the cited source), it is itself a useful natural policy-variation setting for DrPH quasi-experimental research comparing waste outcomes in "Act 672 states" versus "non-Act-672 states."

Zero Waste International Alliance (ZWIA) Hierarchy - Publisher: ZWIA, an international non-profit; its widely cited "Zero Waste Hierarchy" (2018 revision) extends the basic waste hierarchy with a top tier ("Redesign/Rethink") and a bottom "residuals management" tier explicitly excluding waste-to-energy incineration as a "highest and best use," a point of methodological divergence from the EU/Malaysia frameworks, which do count energy recovery as an acceptable (if lower-tier) step. This distinction is relevant for DrPH researchers comparing policy frameworks, since not all "zero waste" claims are measured against the same hierarchy.

⚖️Public Health Relevance

Solid waste mismanagement operates as a determinant of health through multiple, interacting exposure pathways - air (open burning, landfill gas), water (leachate-contaminated groundwater and surface water), soil/food (heavy metal and chemical uptake into crops), and vector proliferation (Aedes mosquitoes breeding in discarded containers, rodents attracted to food waste). The Hanks (1967) disease-hazard model remains a useful causal framework for public health practitioners to trace exposure-to-outcome pathways and identify intervention points. In Malaysia, this manifests concretely: dengue (131,368 cases in 2022) and leptospirosis outbreaks are directly linked to poor waste storage and collection; asthma and other respiratory conditions cluster around open-burning sites and landfills; occupational health surveillance is needed for both the ~50,000 formal waste workers (only 61% PPE-compliant) and the ~150,000 informal scavengers who work essentially unprotected. From a health-systems and economic-evaluation perspective, the WHO's cited RM3-8 return per RM1 invested in waste management makes solid waste management a cost-effective public health investment, not merely an environmental/engineering concern.

3. Environmental Toxicology
Deep-dive learning notes · compiled from lecture material (anonymised) · DrPH specialist level
📚Overview

This topic introduces environmental toxicology as the multidisciplinary science studying how chemical, biological, and physical agents released into the environment affect the structure, function, and health of ecological systems, including humans. The slides move from foundational definitions and toxicity-testing classification systems, through the levels-of-interaction framework linking xenobiotic chemistry to ecosystem effects, to applied human-health-risk content covering exposure pathways, named pollutant classes (PCBs, DDT, pesticides, heavy metals/trace elements), two illustrative published case studies (soil and drinking-water contamination), and natural/man-made disaster scenarios (Fukushima, Chernobyl) as extreme exposure events.

📋Key Definitions & Concepts
  • Environmental toxicology: "The study of the impacts of chemical substances (potential and actual pollutants) upon the structure and function of ecological systems, including man." Uses representative species from the ecosystem or laboratory test organisms to examine responses to chemicals, from which data are extrapolated to the whole ecosystem.
  • Ecological system: A complex system spanning from the molecular level, through individual organisms and communities, to the ecosystem.
  • Environmental toxicology (restated): A multidisciplinary domain of science occupying a niche overlapping toxicology, environmental health, and public policy; studies adverse health effects of chemical, biological, and physical agents on living organisms in ecosystems, with a focus on humans.
  • Environmental health (WHO): Comprises human health aspects, including quality of life, determined by physical, biological, social, and psychosocial factors in the environment. Refers to the theory and practice of assessing, correcting, controlling, and preventing environmental factors that could adversely affect the health of current and future generations.
  • Environmental burden of disease: Diseases or conditions caused by environmental factors such as toxic chemicals, pesticides, and air and water pollution.
  • QSAR (Quantitative Structure-Activity Relationship): The contribution of the physical-chemical characteristics of a compound to its observed toxicity; used to predict the environmental consequences of toxicants via mathematical models, and by analogy with known toxic substances of similar structure.
  • Xenobiotic: A foreign chemical substance entering a biological system; its chemical structure determines its impact at the molecular level.
  • Bioaccumulation: Build-up of a chemical substance within an organism's tissues over time.
  • Biomagnification: Amplification of chemical concentration up a food web/food chain as predators consume contaminated prey (illustrated with DDT and fish/bird populations).
  • Ecotoxicology (aquatic/terrestrial): Sub-disciplines dealing with toxic effects within aquatic (pioneer field) or terrestrial ecosystems specifically.
🔬Position and Role of Environmental Toxicology (Conceptual Model)

The slides present a schematic (Landis and Yu, 2003) describing how a chemical substance entering the environment is tracked through successive levels: (1) fate and transport of chemicals in the environment; (2) interaction of the chemical substance with the site of action (receptor), described via biotransformation and biochemical parameters; (3) effects of the chemical substance on the ecosystem, expressed at increasing levels of biological organization - physiology and behaviour, population, community, whole ecosystem. Level 0 is the xenobiotic itself: its molecular structure determines toxic impact, formalized through QSAR modeling.

📊Rationale for Studying Environmental Toxicology

Cited motivation (Bijlsma & Cohen, 2018): "Most clinicians feel ill-equipped to assess or educate patients about toxicant exposures, and it is unclear how expert environmental medicine clinicians assess these exposures or treat exposure-related conditions." Full citation: Bijlsma, N., & Cohen, M. M. (2018). Expert clinician's perspectives on environmental medicine and toxicant assessment in clinical practice. Environmental Health and Preventive Medicine, 23(1), 19. Regional rationale ("Scenario in Asia"): In East Asia, rising environmental problems create a pressing need for immediate health solutions; environmental toxicology is expected to play an increasing role. Air pollution (natural and anthropogenic sources) is identified as currently one of the greatest public health challenges in East Asia.

⚖️Classification of Environmental Toxicity Tests

Seven-axis classification scheme for ecotoxicological testing: (1) duration relative to organism lifespan; (2) number of species used; (3) type of test organism; (4) ecosystem tested; (5) exposure scenario; (6) measurement endpoint; (7) aim of testing.

Duration relative to organism lifespan: Acute toxicity test - typically 24-48 hours (Daphnia, fish, rats, birds); example Aliivibrio fischeri bioluminescence inhibition test; named standards - Daphnia magna immobilization test (ISO 6341:1996; OECD Test Guideline 202). Chronic toxicity test - relatively long period of organism lifespan; sublethal endpoints (movement, feeding, growth, reproduction, immune/endocrine function, developmental dysfunctions); named standard - Daphnia magna reproduction test (ISO 10706:2000; OECD Test Guideline 211 - 21-day duration).

Number/type of test organisms: bacteria, algae, fungi, plant, animal, multispecies.

Tested ecosystem: aquatic (water + sediment - marine or freshwater; the pioneering domain of ecotoxicology) or terrestrial.

Exposure scenario: whole-body test (organism immersed directly, direct dermal/gill/mucosal contact - e.g. Heterocypris incongruens for water/sediment, Collembola for soil) or feeding studies (exposure via food/drinking water, with the known confound that test animals may eat less when detecting toxic material; controlled-dose alternatives are gavage and injection).

Measurement endpoints: general (growth, survival/mortality, immobilisation, respiration, enzyme activities, ATP production, reproduction, luminescence) and specialised (mutagenicity - number of mutants/revertants/chromosome abnormalities; carcinogenicity - tumours; teratogenicity - reproductive success/cytogenetic characteristics; biodegradation - O2/substrate consumption, CO2 production; bioaccumulation - chemical analysis of accumulated tissue substances).

Aim of testing: screening toxicity/mutagenicity/teratogenesis of single chemicals, environmental samples, or mixtures/waste materials; biomonitoring; early warning systems; establishing effect-based environmental quality criteria; direct effect-based decision-making.

🏥Human Exposure Pathways and Environmental Health Framing

Environmental burden of disease: conditions attributable to toxic chemicals, pesticides, air, and water pollution. Named statistic: air pollution from vehicles, industry and power generation was estimated to kill 800,000 people a year. Indoor air pollution is associated with asthma; named toxicants include asbestos, lead, mercury, and PCBs (from paints, pipes) and pesticides. Human exposure routes (general): inhalation and ingestion/absorption from atmosphere, food, soil, and water. Uptake and accumulation of pollutants in edible plant tissues is highlighted as a major human exposure pathway, relevant to urban/peri-urban agriculture.

🛡️Risk Assessment Linkage

Risk assessment is described as increasingly topical, particularly for marketable edible crops from urban and peri-urban agriculture. Function of risk assessment indices: predict probable exposure routes and levels of pollutant exposure to humans, assisting in evading health hazards through timely decisions, policies, and management actions. Two risk metrics referenced in the case studies (supplementary standard risk-assessment terms): Hazard Quotient (HQ) - ratio of exposure to a reference (safe) dose, with HQ > 1 indicating potential non-carcinogenic risk; and Cancer risk value - probabilistic estimate of excess lifetime cancer risk from a carcinogenic exposure.

📈Named Pollutant Classes Covered

Polychlorinated Biphenyls (PCBs): organic pollutants still present in the environment despite bans in countries such as the United States and Canada; persistent in aquatic ecosystems; farmed salmon shown to have significantly higher PCB levels; health effects include chloracne, neuro-behavioural changes, and immunological changes in children.

Pesticides (general): major source of environmental toxicity, chemically synthesized and persistent; poor biodegradability leads to bioaccumulation and biomagnification; categorized as insecticides (eliminate agricultural pests) and herbicides (target weeds).

DDT (Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane): organochlorine insecticide, now banned due to adverse effects on humans and wildlife; historically widely used by farmers. 1962: Rachel Carson's book The Silent Spring detailed the harmful effects of DDT's widespread, uncontrolled use. DDT and its metabolite DDE released in large quantities are toxic to animals and humans; not easily biodegradable, accumulating in soil/sediment and contaminating water systems; fish and shellfish accumulate DDT in tissue; biomagnification amplified when predators (including birds) consume contaminated fish, documented in rapid declines in bird populations globally.

💩Case Studies (Published Literature Cited in Slides)

Case Study 1 - Soil Pollution (Natasha et al., 2020b): reported accumulation of arsenic (As) and lead (Pb) by spinach following foliar application. Pb mainly sequestered in leaves; As transferred toward root tissues. Both trace elements affected photosynthetic apparatus, pigment content, and plant growth; Pb was less toxic than As. Health risk assessment: possible carcinogenic effects from both Pb and As; non-carcinogenic effects (hazard quotient > 1) from As only, in children. Recommendation: continuous monitoring of trace elements in the atmosphere to ensure food safety and human health.

Case Study 2 - Drinking Water Pollution (Khalid et al., 2020b, Heavy metal contamination and exposure risk assessment via drinking groundwater in Vehari-Pakistan, Environ Sci Pollut Res Int): sampled 129 groundwater samples from Vehari District, Pakistan, analyzing copper, lead, nickel, cadmium, chromium, manganese, zinc, and iron. Findings exceeding WHO threshold values: cadmium 68% of samples, lead 93% of samples, iron 100% of samples. Majority of samples unfit for drinking due to non-compliance with physicochemical parameters. Cadmium cancer risk value range: 0.0007-0.03 (potential carcinogenic risk). Lead showed possible non-carcinogenic risk (mean hazard quotient > 1). Recommendation: urgent mass awareness campaigns and remediation/risk-mitigation planning.

📋Framework Deep-Dive: Key Published Frameworks Explained

QSAR (Quantitative Structure-Activity Relationship) Modelling - Origin: developed across 20th-century medicinal/environmental chemistry, formalised as a regulatory tool by agencies including the US EPA and the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) under REACH. Structure: QSAR models use statistical/mathematical relationships between a molecule's physicochemical descriptors (e.g. octanol-water partition coefficient, molecular size, electronic structure) and its measured or predicted toxicity, allowing prediction of the toxicity of untested compounds from structurally similar, previously tested compounds. Application here: the lecture's Level-0 "xenobiotic" starting point in the fate-transport-effect model relies on QSAR logic to predict how a pollutant's structure will determine its downstream toxicological behaviour; it is a core tool DrPH-affiliated researchers use in preliminary chemical risk screening before committing to expensive biomonitoring studies.

OECD Test Guidelines and ISO Ecotoxicological Standards - Publisher: the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD Test Guidelines Programme) and the International Organization for Standardization (ISO), both maintaining internationally harmonised, legally referenced protocols for chemical safety testing. Structure: OECD Test Guideline 202 (Daphnia sp. Acute Immobilisation Test, paired with ISO 6341:1996) and OECD Test Guideline 211 (Daphnia magna Reproduction Test, paired with ISO 10706:2000) specify exact experimental conditions (species, exposure duration, endpoints, statistical analysis) so that toxicity results are comparable across laboratories and countries. Application here: these are the named standards underlying the acute/chronic toxicity classification scheme in the lecture, and are the reference protocols a DrPH environmental-toxicology study would cite to justify its exposure-testing methodology.

WHO Definition of Environmental Health - Publisher: World Health Organization, most commonly cited from the 1993 WHO consultation definition. Structure: frames environmental health as comprising both (a) the direct pathological effects of chemical, physical and biological agents, and (b) the effects (often indirect) on health and well-being of the broad physical, psychological, social and aesthetic environment, including housing, urban development, land use and transport. Application here: this definitional framework underlies why environmental toxicology is taught as a public-health discipline rather than a purely chemical one - toxicological data only becomes public-health-relevant once linked to a defined population's exposure and outcome.

🔬Public Health Relevance

Environmental toxicology sits at the intersection of chemistry, ecology, clinical medicine, and policy, making it foundational to public health practice in sanitation and environmental health management. The topic demonstrates how contaminants move from source (industrial emissions, pesticide application, nuclear accidents, plastic waste) through environmental compartments (air, soil, water, sediment) into biological systems, ultimately producing measurable human health outcomes - cancer, reproductive disorders, respiratory disease, immune dysfunction, and neuro-behavioural changes in children. The case studies (arsenic/lead in spinach; cadmium/lead/iron in Pakistani groundwater) exemplify a translational chain directly relevant to Malaysian and broader Asian contexts given similar irrigation practices, industrial run-off, and reliance on informal urban/peri-urban food production. The explicit statistic that air pollution kills an estimated 800,000 people annually underscores the urgency of applying environmental toxicology tools (biomonitoring, QSAR modeling, risk assessment indices) to inform policy and regulatory decision-making.

4. Drinking Water Management and Surveillance
Deep-dive learning notes · compiled from lecture material (anonymised) · DrPH specialist level
📚Overview

This topic examines how drinking water is treated, monitored, and regulated in Malaysia, using a Kelantan case study (Air Kelantan Sdn Bhd / AKSB water treatment plant at Kampung Puteh, and the Ministry of Health's third-party monitoring unit KMAM - Kawalan Mutu Air Minum). It covers the full water safety chain: raw water abstraction, multi-barrier treatment, disinfection choice, laboratory/field surveillance, legal frameworks, complaint management, and emerging treatment technologies. The material situates these operational details within broader public health issues - water availability, water quality, waterborne disease, contamination sources, susceptible populations, aging infrastructure, and the case for a dedicated Drinking Water Quality Act (DWQA).

📋Key Definitions & Concepts
  • AKSB (Air Kelantan Sdn Bhd): State water operator for Kelantan, established 1994; manages 36 water treatment plants across all 12 districts.
  • KMAM (Kawalan Mutu Air Minum): Ministry of Health (MOH) unit that performs independent, third-party drinking water quality surveillance under the District Health Office (Pejabat Kesihatan Daerah, PKD).
  • NDWQSP (National Drinking Water Quality Surveillance Programme): MOH programme established in 1983 to prevent waterborne disease and ensure wholesomeness of public drinking water. Comprises 4 components: Sanitary Survey; Data Processing & Assessment; Remedial Action; Institutional Inspection.
  • SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition): PC-based control system allowing plant operators to view and control treatment processes in real time; at AKSB Kg. Puteh it is installed but "not yet fully utilised."
  • NRW (Non-Revenue Water): Water produced but "lost" before reaching/billing the customer; composed of physical losses (leaks) and commercial/apparent losses (theft, metering inaccuracy).
  • PAAB (Pengurusan Aset Air Berhad): Federal-Government-owned water asset management company; under the "asset-light" reform model, state water assets are transferred to PAAB (30-45-year lease) so operators can focus on service delivery instead of funding infrastructure.
  • GWQI (Malaysia Groundwater Quality Index): 0-100 scale index based on 7 parameters: pH, Iron, TDS, Nitrate, E. coli, Phenol, Sulphate.
  • WQI (Water Quality Index) for rivers: classifies rivers as clean/slightly polluted/polluted based on parameters including BOD, Ammoniacal Nitrogen (AN), Suspended Solids (SS).
  • Safely managed drinking water service (SDG indicator 6.1.1): drinking water from an improved source located on premises, available when needed, and free from faecal and priority chemical contamination.
  • FWBD: Faecal-Waterborne Diseases (cholera, diarrhoea, dysentery, typhoid, hepatitis A, polio).
  • Point vs. non-point (diffuse) contamination sources: point = identifiable discharge (industrial effluent, sewage overflow, illegal dumping, mining); non-point = diffuse (agricultural runoff, urban stormwater, livestock, land clearing, natural geology, aging pipe leaching).
🔬AKSB Kampung Puteh Water Treatment Plant - Overview

Area: 4.5 acres; began operation 1984; upgraded 2002 to comply with WHO & MOH Drinking Water Quality standards. Design capacity: 45 million litres/day (MLD); current output: 44 MLD. Raw water: 35 tube boreholes (25 at Kampung Puteh, 10 at Kg. Kota). Statewide (Kelantan) source mix: River 37%, Groundwater 63%. Regulatory framework: Act 654 (SPAN Act 2006) and Act 655 (Water Services Industry Act 2006).

River vs. borehole (groundwater) source characteristics: river water has higher turbidity/organic matter and higher pH, easier to treat via coagulation-flocculation-sedimentation; borehole water has elevated iron & manganese and lower pH, requiring more complex/costly treatment (oxidation, filtration, ion exchange, pH adjustment to prevent distribution corrosion).

📊Drinking Water Treatment Process (AKSB, groundwater-based)

Sequential multi-barrier train: Raw water (underground) -> Aeration -> Flocculation (+ACH/polymer) -> Sedimentation (Lamella plate, horizontal flow) -> Filtration -> Disinfection (+Chlorine) -> Storage (+Calcium carbonate, +Fluoride) -> Distribution.

  1. Aeration: passing large volumes of air/venting through water to oxidise the water, reduce volatile organic compounds, remove dissolved gases (hydrogen sulphide, methane, radon), oxidise dissolved metals (Fe, Mn) into solid/removable state, increase O2 and remove CO2. Methods: tower aerator, diffused bubble aerator, spray aerator.
  2. Coagulation & Flocculation: Coagulant is Aluminium Chlorohydrate (ACH), positively charged, neutralising negatively charged suspended particles; flocculant aid is Polymer (e.g., polyelectrolyte), bridging particles into larger flocs. Combining ACH + polymer reduces required dosage of both. Residual/adverse effects (from lecture notes): ACH acute large-dose effects include nausea, joint pain, oral ulcer, and long-term use is linked to Alzheimer's disease; polymer carries immunotoxicity risk.
  3. Sedimentation: gravity settling using Lamella plate (increases settling surface area/efficiency) to remove solids, sludge, heavier particles, improving clarity and reducing turbidity.
  4. Filtration: multilayer sand (and gravel) filter, gravity-flow, removing suspended, floating and sinkable particles; backwash trigger after 72 hours of use OR when maximum head-loss level reached, whichever earlier.
  5. Disinfection & final conditioning: additives at storage stage include calcium carbonate (pH correction, corrosion prevention), chlorine, and fluoride. Target distribution pH: 6.5-8.5; WHO-recommended fluoride: 0.5-1.0 mg/L; target residual chlorine at consumer tap: ~0.2-0.5 mg/L.
  6. Storage -> Distribution.
⚖️Disinfection Choice: Chlorine Gas vs. Chloramine

Chlorine gas (chosen disinfectant at AKSB): disinfects bacteria, parasites, viruses; effective, dissolves well, least expensive chlorine method; removes taste/odour, nitrogen compounds, algae; provides residual protection through distribution network; rapid action. Cons: water becomes more acidic (requires Ca(OH)2/CaCO3 addition); reacts with organic matter to form Trihalomethanes (THMs) and haloacetic acids, carcinogenic (bladder, colon, rectal cancer); occupational hazard (poisonous gas); limited effectiveness against Cryptosporidium oocysts. Chloramine (alternative, not chosen): longer-lasting disinfecting effect through distribution network, possibly better taste/odour, but needs longer contact time and produces higher levels of by-products. AKSB's decision: chlorine gas remains preferred primary disinfectant based on cost-effectiveness, rapid action, proven performance.

🛡️Water Sampling & Analytical Methods

Sampling protocol (before/during/after): before - select sites, sterilise containers, prepare preservatives, calibrate field equipment, prepare Chain of Custody (COC) form; during - flush tap 2 minutes, fill bacteriological containers aseptically, record in-situ measurements immediately, label; after - maintain cold chain at 4C, bacteriological samples analysed within 6 hours, COC signed at lab handover, results compared to NSDWQ 2004.

In situ (field) analysis: pH, temperature, conductivity/TDS, turbidity, residual chlorine (DPD colorimetric kit), dissolved oxygen. Advantage: immediate results for parameters that change rapidly. Limitation: semi-quantitative, needs regular calibration.

Laboratory analysis: bacteriological (membrane filtration/MPN), heavy metals (ICP-MS), pesticides (GC-MS), nitrate/ammonia/fluoride (ion chromatography/spectrophotometry), THMs & VOCs (GC-FID). Advantage: quantitative/accurate. Limitation: 24-72 hour lag; requires accredited lab.

💧Legal, Regulatory & Standards Framework

National Standard for Drinking Water Quality (NSDWQ) 2004 (MOH): primary national benchmark setting maximum permissible limits across 4 groups. Act 654 (SPAN Act 2006) & Act 655 (Water Services Industry Act 2006): SPAN implements/enforces water supply and sewerage laws, licenses operators, monitors compliance; Act 655 mandates operator compliance with NSDWQ 2004. Malaysia Groundwater Quality Standards and Index 2019 (DOE): adapted from MOH raw water quality values, especially relevant given elevated Fe/Mn in Kelantan boreholes. WHO Guidelines for Drinking-water Quality, 4th Edition, 2017: international gold standard, health-based targets for 160+ contaminants. Food Act 1983 (Act 281) & Food Regulations 1985: govern commercially sold drinking water; non-compliance prosecuted under Section 4(1)(f)/Regulation 360C(4).

⚡The Case for a Dedicated Drinking Water Quality Act (DWQA)

Current situation: no single comprehensive Act; regulation fragmented across NSDWQ 2004, Food Act 1983, SPAN Act 2006, Water Services Industry Act 2006. NDWQSP (since 1983) and BAKAS (Water Supply and Environmental Sanitation Unit, 50+ years) have achieved near-full national coverage but enforcement remains fragmented. 2022: Ministry of Health announced intent to introduce a national drinking water law. Case for DWQA: standardisation, regular monitoring mandate, public health protection, clear accountability/penalties. Comparator cited: US Safe Drinking Water Act 1974, associated with improved public health outcomes (EPA, 2012).

📚Water Coverage, Non-Revenue Water (NRW), and Infrastructure

Kelantan coverage: 73.9% (2022) - lowest among Malaysian states; national average >95% in most states (Household Income and Basic Amenities Survey Report 2019; SPAN Annual Report 2022). Contributing factors: remote geography, frequent supply interruptions, turbid/odorous supply, aging pipe networks -> rising reliance on private (unmonitored) groundwater. PAAB asset-light reform: as of 31 December 2022, 10 states completed restructuring/asset transfer agreements with PAAB (30-45-year lease).

Non-Revenue Water (SPAN Annual Report 2022): Peninsular Malaysia & FT Labuan NRW rose from 33.7% (2021) to 34.4% (2022); national NRW rose from 36.3% (2021) to 37.2% (2022). Physical losses (leaks) = 70-75% of total NRW; commercial losses = 25-30%. Federal allocation: RM1.9 billion under RMK-12 National NRW Reduction Programme. Asbestos cement (AC) pipes: 29.3% of 135,975 km total distribution pipe network in Peninsular Malaysia/Labuan, installed 30-50 years ago, prone to leaking/bursting. Replacement capacity: ~150-180 km per state per year. Replacement cost: ~RM900,000 per km.

📋Preserving Water Quality - Source Water Monitoring

Drinking water source split: Surface water 81%, Groundwater ~3% nationally (though much higher reliance in Kelantan). River Water Quality Index (2022): 8,136 samples from 1,353 manual monitoring stations covering 672 rivers nationwide - 495 rivers (74%) clean WQI, 148 (22%) slightly polluted, 29 (4%) polluted. Key WQI indicators: BOD, Ammonia Nitrogen (AN), Suspended Solids (SS). Groundwater monitoring established 1997, 120 monitoring stations nationwide.

🔬Contamination Pathway (Source to Consumer) and Sources of Contamination

Point sources: industrial effluent discharge, municipal wastewater, sewage treatment plant overflow, illegal dumping, mining activities. Non-point/diffuse sources: agricultural runoff, urban stormwater, livestock farming, uncontrolled land clearing/earthworks, natural geology, aging pipes. Five-stage contamination pathway: source water -> treatment plant -> storage reservoir -> distribution network -> consumer premises, each with distinct failure modes.

📊Drinking Water and Population Health (Waterborne Disease Burden)

Globally (2022): 1.7 billion people use a drinking water source contaminated with faeces. ~505,000 diarrhoeal deaths/year globally attributable to unsafe water (WHO, 2023), ~395,000 of these in children under 5. Chemical health effects: Nitrates -> methemoglobinemia ("blue baby syndrome") in infants; Arsenic -> skin, lung, bladder cancer; Lead -> neurodevelopmental impairment; THMs -> bladder, colon, rectal cancer; excess Fluoride -> dental/skeletal fluorosis. Other specific risks: Manganese -> neurodevelopmental toxicity in children/infants; Atrazine -> small-for-gestational-age, low birth weight, preterm birth; DBP (disinfection by-products) -> colon and rectal cancer.

⚖️Susceptible/Vulnerable Populations

Children: developing immune/neurological systems, higher water intake per body weight, lead-related neurodevelopmental impairment, nitrate-related methemoglobinemia in infants <6 months. Elderly: immunosenescence + comorbidities, higher risk of severe outcomes from Legionella, increased vulnerability to cholera/typhoid dehydration complications. Pregnant women: nitrate/lead/mercury risks to fetus, higher Cryptosporidium infection risk. Marginalised/underserved communities: rural areas (especially Kelantan, Sabah, Sarawak) relying on untreated private water; Orang Asli communities dependent on river water/rainwater harvesting without treatment.

🏥Aging Water Infrastructure - Malaysia Case Example

Pipes aged 30-50 years past design life are a major cause of unscheduled supply disruption nationwide. National NRW Reduction Programme launched 2018, total allocation RM1.907 billion. BAKAS (Rural Environmental Sanitation Programme) has 50+ years of service; example noted: Kampung Kuala Lah, Bertam, Gua Musang (2019) - hill catchment water supply built via community gotong-royong with Kelantan Health Department engineers, resolving inadequate supply for 22 families.

🛡️New/Emerging Water Treatment Technologies

Drivers for innovation: limitations of conventional chlorination (THM formation, ineffective against Cryptosporidium), emerging contaminants (pharmaceuticals, microplastics, PFAS) not removed by conventional treatment, climate/source water changes, cost/sustainability goals.

  1. Ozonation: mechanism via Corona Discharge or UV radiation producing O3; pros - most powerful commercially available oxidiser, effective incl. against Cryptosporidium, no THM by-product; cons - no residual in distribution system, higher capital/maintenance cost, corrosive, minor bromate by-product.
  2. Chemiless Biological Treatment System (applied in Kelantan - Loji Rawatan Air Kampung Chap, AKSB): biological system using nitrate and iron bacteria only, no chemicals; ~4x faster than conventional chemical treatment; pros - environmentally friendly, reduced costs; cons - limited to specific contaminants, requires careful bacterial-level monitoring.
  3. Deferum and Demagnum System: hydro-automatic system using "Polymer Floating Filtering Media," replacing conventional aeration/coagulation/sedimentation; requires raw water pressure >4.0 bar; pros - no chemicals, compact footprint; cons - pressure requirement limits applicability, higher initial capital cost.
  4. Ultrafiltration (UF): membrane filtration removing particles 0.1-0.01 microns using hollow fibre filters; cleaning via normal backwash and Chemical Enhanced Backwash (NaOH, Hypochlorite); pros - effective at removing bacteria/small particles, fully automated; cons - membrane fouling, significant energy consumption, does not remove dissolved substances.

💩Framework Deep-Dive: Key Published Frameworks Explained

WHO Guidelines for Drinking-water Quality (GDWQ), 4th Edition (2017, with addenda) - Publisher: World Health Organization. Structure: the GDWQ is built around the Water Safety Plan (WSP) approach - a preventive risk-management framework (distinct from purely end-of-pipe testing) comprising three core components: (1) system assessment (does the system as a whole deliver water meeting health-based targets?), (2) operational monitoring (are the individual control measures - coagulation, filtration, disinfection etc. - working as intended, monitored via simple, rapid indicators?), and (3) management plans, including documentation and communication, describing actions to be taken from normal operation through incident conditions. The GDWQ sets "health-based targets" for over 160 chemical, microbial and radiological parameters via guideline values derived from tolerable daily intake / acceptable risk calculations. Application here: Malaysia's NSDWQ 2004 numeric limits are explicitly harmonised with (and in places more conservative than) WHO GDWQ values; the WSP's "multi-barrier" logic (protect source -> treat effectively -> protect during storage/distribution) is the conceptual skeleton behind the AKSB treatment train and the KMAM surveillance system described in the case study.

National Standard for Drinking Water Quality (NSDWQ) 2004 - Publisher: Ministry of Health Malaysia. Structure: divides parameters into four monitored groups (I: physical/bacteriological, II: chemical/inorganic, III: heavy metals, IV: pesticides) each with its own maximum permissible limit and mandated monitoring frequency, escalating in stringency and frequency from treatment plant to distribution system. Application here: NSDWQ 2004 is the direct legal/technical basis for the entire monitoring frequency table and violation-response protocol described in this topic, and is the benchmark a Malaysian DrPH water-quality study would cite as its regulatory comparator.

SDG Target 6.1 and Indicator 6.1.1 ("Safely Managed Drinking Water") - Publisher: United Nations, tracked jointly by WHO/UNICEF JMP. Structure: defines "safely managed" as drinking water from an improved source that is (a) accessible on premises, (b) available when needed, and (c) free from faecal and priority chemical contamination - a stricter three-part composite standard than the older "improved source" indicator used pre-2015. Application here: Kelantan's cited 73.9% coverage figure should be interpreted against this indicator's stringent three-part definition, meaning true "safely managed" access may be materially lower once water-quality testing (not just source type) is factored in.

🏷️Public Health Relevance

Drinking water management sits at the intersection of environmental engineering and population health protection: every treatment failure or infrastructure lapse translates directly into disease risk, from acute microbial outbreaks to chronic, often irreversible harms from heavy metals and pesticide bioaccumulation. The Kelantan case illustrates how technical constraints (groundwater high in Fe/Mn, low coverage, aging infrastructure) compound social vulnerability, driving households toward unmonitored private groundwater. The dual-track surveillance model (operator self-monitoring by AKSB plus independent MOH verification by KMAM) exemplifies a check-and-balance approach essential for public accountability, but also shows persistent gaps: regulatory fragmentation, under-monitoring of emerging contaminants, enforcement limitations in rural/remote areas, and disproportionate risk to children, the elderly, pregnant women, and marginalised/indigenous communities.

5. Environmental Health in Emergency and Disaster
Deep-dive learning notes · compiled from lecture material (anonymised) · DrPH specialist level
📚Overview

This topic examines how environmental health principles apply before, during, and after emergencies and disasters. It distinguishes "environmental health in emergencies" (managing environmental hazards that arise during a crisis) from "environmental health emergencies" (crises that are themselves caused by environmental exposures), and walks through the disaster management continuum of prevention, mitigation, preparedness, and response, with detailed operational content on water, sanitation, vector control, disease surveillance, and logistics.

📋Key Definitions & Concepts
  • Environmental health and protection (National Environmental Health Association, NEHA): protection against environmental factors that may adversely impact human health or the ecological balances essential to long-term human health and environmental quality, whether in the natural or man-made environment.
  • Environmental health (WHO, 1993): those aspects of human health, including quality of life, that are determined by physical, chemical, biological, social, and psychosocial processes in the environment; also the theory and practice of assessing, correcting, controlling, and preventing environmental factors that can potentially adversely affect the health of present and future generations.
  • Emergency (American Heritage Dictionary): a serious situation or occurrence that happens unexpectedly and demands immediate action; a condition of urgent need for action or assistance - a state of emergency.
  • Environmental health in emergencies vs. environmental health emergencies: a conceptual distinction drawn explicitly in the lecture - the former is the practice of managing environmental health risks that arise during/after a disaster; the latter refers to emergencies that originate from environmental/hazardous exposures themselves.
  • Emergency preparedness (WHO, 2002, Environmental Health in Emergencies and Disasters: A Practical Guide): a program of long-term development activities whose goals are to strengthen the overall capacity and capability of a country to manage efficiently all types of emergency and to bring about an orderly transition from relief through recovery and back to sustainable development.
  • Prevention: activities taken to prevent a natural phenomenon or potential hazard from having harmful effects on either people or economic assets.
  • Mitigation: recognizing that disasters will occur, attempts are made to reduce the harmful effects of a disaster and to limit their impact on human suffering and economic assets.
  • Preparedness: the readiness to predict, prevent, mitigate, respond to and cope with the effects of a disaster.
  • Natural disaster: an act of nature of such magnitude as to create a catastrophic situation in which day-to-day patterns of life are suddenly disrupted and people are plunged into helplessness and suffering.
  • Complex emergency: situations of disrupted livelihoods and threats to life produced by warfare, civil disturbance, and large-scale movements of people, requiring emergency response in a difficult political and security environment.
  • Deliberate event: an act (or threat) involving the intentional release of hazardous substances (chemical, biological, or radiological materials) to cause harm.
  • Disease outbreak: the occurrence of disease cases in excess of normal expectancy.
  • Poisoning: occurs when people drink, eat, breathe, inject, or touch enough of a hazardous substance to cause illness or death; onset may be immediate or may develop over years of long-term exposure.
🔬Types of Environmental Health Emergencies
  1. Natural Disasters - earthquakes, tsunamis, volcanic eruptions, landslides, hurricanes, floods, wildfires, heat waves, droughts, tornadoes. Every year, natural disasters kill around 90,000 people and affect close to 160 million people worldwide.
  2. Technological or Industrial Incidents - accidents at hazardous installations (chemical or nuclear power plants) and accidents during transport of hazardous substances (oil tankers, chemical transport). With proliferation of nuclear power and chemical plants, disasters on the scale of Chernobyl or Bhopal cannot be ruled out.
  3. Complex Emergencies - combine internal conflict with large-scale displacement, mass famine/food shortage, and fragile institutions; often exacerbated by concurrent natural disasters. WHO's response is coordinated by the Department of Health Action in Crisis.
  4. Deliberate Events - intentional release of hazardous substances to cause harm. Case study - Tokyo subway sarin attack (1995): members of the Aum Shinrikyo sect deliberately released sarin nerve gas into the Tokyo subway. Case study - methanol adulteration of illicit alcohol: Turkey 2005 (at least 21 died from counterfeit raki); Nicaragua 2006 (788 poisoned, 44 died, from contaminated guaron).
  5. Disease Outbreaks - usually caused by infection transmitted person-to-person, animal-to-person, or via environment; may also follow exposure to chemicals or radioactive materials (example: Minamata disease, caused by mercury exposure).
  6. Poisoning - natural and technological disasters can release toxic chemicals harming large numbers of people. Case study - Gaoqiao, China 2003: a natural gas well eruption released hydrogen sulphide, killing more than 240 people, with 9,000 attending hospital and 64,000 evacuated. Case study - Hurricane Katrina, New Orleans 2005: increase in carbon monoxide poisoning from unsafe use of portable gasoline-powered generators during power outages. Case study - Bangladesh: mass chronic arsenic poisoning from tube wells drawing on contaminated groundwater. Case study - Slovenia: outbreak of tropane alkaloid poisoning from buckwheat flour contaminated with Datura stramonium seeds.
📊Agents, Diseases & Other Threats (Preparedness Tool Categories)

Bioterrorism emergencies: anthrax, brucellosis, plague, tularemia. Mass casualties: burns, explosions/blasts, injuries. Chemical emergencies: ricin, chlorine, nerve agents, toxic alcohols. Natural disasters & severe weather: hurricanes, floods, extreme heat, wildfires, earthquakes, tornadoes. Radiation emergencies: acute radiation syndrome, dirty bombs, nuclear blast, polonium. Recent outbreaks & incidents: Salmonella Saintpaul, measles.

⚖️Prevention, Mitigation and Preparedness

Prevention activities: hazard identification (identifying actual threats facing a community); vulnerability assessment (evaluating risk and capacity to handle consequences). Mitigation activities: strengthening building/construction codes; land use restrictions; legislative mandates; relocation of vulnerable populations; reinforcing critical services. Preparedness - framed as a triad/cycle: Mitigation -> Vulnerability Reduction -> Disaster Response. Public health preparedness capabilities involve activities directed at preventing possible public health emergencies and activities directed at planning to ensure an adequate response. Component activities: regulating environmental conditions and food/water safety (setting standards, inspecting facilities, monitoring conditions, abating disease-fostering conditions, enforcing compliance); planning for emergency medical and public health response capabilities. How preparedness is achieved (national level): national legislation and policy; plans and procedures for disaster management and coordination; strengthening institutional and human resources; establishing/managing stocks of relief supplies; identifying transportation options; public education and community participation; collecting/analysing/disseminating relevant information.

🏥Response - Structure and Operational Components

Emergency response is a cyclical process - repeated assessment, planning, action and review. Ten components:

  1. Assessment - determines whether assistance is needed, whether local capacity is adequate, priorities/strategy, necessary resources, baseline data, and fund-raising/advocacy information needs.
  2. Evacuation/warning communication principles - simple non-technical language; multiple warning systems must not conflict; messages should clearly state the threat and implications; potential victims should be clearly identified.
  3. Environmental Health Measures - core operational priorities: facilities for safe excretion, protecting water supplies, minimum drinking/cooking/hygiene water, water containers, cooking utensils/fuel, disease-avoidance knowledge, soap for hand washing, containing/removing chemical or radiological contamination sources.
  4. Organization of Environmental Health Activities.
  5. Personnel Management - basic personal needs, family support, safety/security, emotional support/counseling, office facilities, transportation/communication facilities.
  6. Equipment and Supplies - personnel equipment, emergency water/sanitation equipment, materials/tools for repairing damaged networks, vector control equipment, monitoring/surveillance items, laboratory equipment, maps/reports, administrative items.

Transportation and Logistics (sub-component): moving teams, road clearance, moving affected people, moving equipment/supplies, trucking water, moving human bodies, moving solid waste, moving animal corpses (especially after floods/cyclones), repair and reconstruction.

  1. Telecommunication - telephone, fax, email/internet, telex, radio.

8-10. Financial procedures; rules, standards and guidelines; international assistance.

🛡️Other Aspects of Response - Disease Surveillance and Public Health Action
  1. Detecting a disease outbreak - prompt reporting is critical; authorities use pattern recognition software and GIS to determine patterns.
  2. Conducting epidemiological investigations - determining identity, source, and modes of transmission of the disease agent.
  3. Performing laboratory analyses - clinical labs diagnose and identify agents, forwarding results to public health labs for confirmation.
  4. Pursuing public health interventions to limit spread - educational efforts (boil-water notices, food safety advisories/recalls, safe mail-handling practices).
  5. Assuring emergency medical treatment and prophylaxis - antibiotics, antitoxins, chemical antidotes, prophylactic vaccines, mental health services; decontamination and triage.
  6. Remediating environmental conditions - decontaminating exposed sites/facilities.
  7. Preventing secondary public health emergencies. Case study - World Trade Center attack: mass evacuation led to abandonment of food supplies in surrounding homes/restaurants; NYC officials secured premises to prevent rodent/pest proliferation.

WHO (2002) Environmental Health in Emergencies and Disasters: A Practical Guide - Publisher: World Health Organization (authors B. Wisner and J. Adams). Structure: the guide organises the entire disaster management cycle into the four phases used throughout this lecture - prevention, mitigation, preparedness, and response - and, within response, itemises ten operational components (assessment, evacuation, environmental health measures, organisation of activities, personnel management, equipment/supplies, telecommunication, financial procedures, rules/standards/guidelines, and international assistance). Sphere Handbook / Sphere Minimum Standards (Humanitarian Charter and Minimum Standards in Humanitarian Response) - Publisher: the Sphere Project (now Sphere Association), a consortium of humanitarian NGOs and the Red Cross/Red Crescent movement, first published 1998, most recently revised 2018. NEHA (National Environmental Health Association) Definition of Environmental Health and Protection - Publisher: NEHA (a US-based professional body, though its definitional statement is widely cited internationally). Structure: frames "environmental health and protection" as protection against environmental factors that may adversely affect human health or the ecological balances essential to long-term human health and environmental quality. Application here: provides the professional/disciplinary framing (distinguishing "protection" as an active practice from "health" as a state) underlying the lecture's opening definitions.

⚡Public Health Relevance

Environmental health emergency management sits at the intersection of environmental health, epidemiology, and health systems/emergency management. In Malaysia and the broader Southeast Asian region, the topic is directly relevant given recurrent exposure to floods, landslides, industrial/chemical incidents, and the potential for complex emergencies involving cross-border population movements. 6. Hazardous Waste: Case Study (Asia's Pandemic Waste Emergency)
Deep-dive learning notes · compiled from lecture material (anonymised) · DrPH specialist level
📚Overview

This case study, based on an investigative documentary on Asia's pandemic waste emergency, examines how the COVID-19 pandemic triggered a secondary crisis: an emergency of clinical/pandemic waste mismanagement across Asia (Indonesia, the Philippines, Vietnam, India). Malaysia is used throughout as a comparative "regional model" for its formalised clinical waste legal and concession framework, while honestly mapping Malaysia's own strengths and gaps during COVID-19. The review is structured around critical issues/causes, Malaysian legal violations, impacts, control measures, Malaysia-vs-documentary comparison, and lessons learned.

📋Key Definitions & Concepts
  • Clinical waste: waste generated by healthcare activities that is potentially infectious; in Malaysian law classified as Scheduled Waste code SW401 under the Environmental Quality (Scheduled Wastes) Regulations 2005.
  • PPE (Personal Protective Equipment): N95 respirators, surgical masks, nitrile gloves, face shields, isolation gowns, coveralls.
  • MCO (Movement Control Order): Malaysia's COVID-19 lockdown.
  • Informal waste workers/pickers: individuals (globally 15-20 million) who depend on scavenging recyclable materials for livelihood, operating outside formal regulation.
  • Waste manifest/consignment note: legal document that must accompany every movement of SW401 waste, creating a cradle-to-grave audit trail.
  • eSWIS (Electronic Scheduled Waste Information System): DOE Malaysia's online scheduled-waste monitoring platform; the manifest system nonetheless remains largely paper-based in practice.
  • One Health: framework recognising interdependence of human, animal, and ecosystem health.
  • Waste hierarchy: Reduce -> Reuse -> Recycle -> Recover -> Dispose, applied to both clinical waste and pandemic plastics.
  • Fomite transmission: disease transmission via contaminated surfaces/objects.
  • PEP (Post-Exposure Prophylaxis): treatment that can prevent infection if started within 72 hours of exposure; documented as effectively unavailable to informal workers.

📊Five Critical Issues and Four Underlying Causes

Critical Issue 1 - Unprecedented surge in clinical waste: mandatory single-use PPE across all tiers, ~3.4 kg/patient/day additional waste, new household clinical-waste category from home quarantine/self-testing, vaccination campaigns generating hundreds of millions of syringes/vials, treatment supply chains unable to scale as fast as healthcare delivery.

Critical Issue 2 - Widespread improper disposal: open burning (generating dioxins/furans since combustion temperatures fall far below the 850C minimum for safe thermal destruction), bin mixing, illegal dumping, landfilling untreated clinical waste, sewage discharge, littering.

Critical Issue 3 - Informal waste workers unprotected: pandemic collapsed recyclable markets, economic necessity forced continued handling of infectious waste. Needlestick injury risks per exposure: HBV 6-30%, HCV 0-7%, HIV 0.3%; PEP effectively unavailable to informal workers.

Critical Issue 4 - Treatment infrastructure at breaking point: incinerators run at 120-150% of design capacity, equipment breakdowns, geographic maldistribution, rural/remote facilities defaulting to open burning.

Critical Issue 5 - Pandemic plastic explosion: drivers include food delivery services tripling in Asian cities, e-commerce packaging up 200-300%, reusable bag/cup bans reversed for infection control; consequences include 25,900 t COVID plastics entering oceans by 2021, PPE washing onto Asia-Pacific beaches, marine fauna entanglement.

Four underlying causes: (1) regulatory gaps - several ASEAN states lacked specific clinical waste laws pre-2020; (2) infrastructure deficit - clinical waste treated as a cost centre; (3) low public awareness - no pre-pandemic education on household COVID waste segregation; (4) economic & social inequity - informal workers with no alternative income, facilities choosing cheapest (illegal) disposal.

🏥Impacts - Public Health, Environment & Society

Infectious disease transmission: SARS-CoV-2 survives up to 72 hours on plastic and stainless steel, 24 hours on cardboard (van Doremalen et al., 2020, NEJM); fomite transmission risk from discarded PPE.

Occupational injury & bloodborne pathogens: needlestick risk HBV 6-30%, HCV 0-7%, HIV 0.3% per exposure; injuries structurally invisible in national reporting systems for informal workers.

Air pollution from open burning: toxic combustion products include dioxins & furans (IARC Group 1 carcinogen, from PVC-containing clinical waste burned below 850C), particulate matter, heavy metals, hydrogen chloride. Health effects: acute (eye/throat irritation, respiratory distress) and chronic (lung cancer, cardiovascular disease, developmental toxicity in children).

Vectors & water contamination: accumulated waste breeds Aedes mosquitoes (dengue), attracts rodents (leptospirosis), flies (diarrhoeal disease); leachate from unlined sites contaminates groundwater. Malaysian case study: 2020 Klang Valley water crisis - illegal chemical dumping caused extended supply cuts affecting 1+ million households.

Environmental impacts: marine pollution (25,900 t COVID plastic in oceans by 2021), soil & groundwater contamination, biodiversity loss, climate dimension (methane from decomposing organic clinical waste).

Social & economic impacts - Environmental Justice: low-income communities disproportionately near landfills/dumpsites; children in dumpsite communities face developmental toxicity from heavy metals; gender dimension - women disproportionately represent the informal waste workforce.

🛡️Control Measures

Waste hierarchy & source minimisation: Reduce (reusable PPE, right-size procurement), Reuse (reusable gowns, sterilised PAPRs), Recycle (non-contaminated packaging), Recover (energy recovery via pyrolysis), Dispose (licensed incineration/autoclave last resort).

💧Framework Deep-Dive: Key Published Frameworks Explained

WHO (2020) Safe Management of Wastes from Health-Care Activities (2nd edition) - Publisher: World Health Organization, in collaboration with UNEP and the Basel Convention Secretariat. Structure: establishes the internationally recognised colour-coded waste segregation scheme (yellow = infectious, red = anatomical/pathological, black = general/domestic, and rigid yellow/puncture-resistant containers = sharps), sets recommended treatment options ranked by preference (non-burn technologies such as autoclaving preferred over incineration where feasible), and specifies minimum operator training, PPE, and occupational-exposure-management requirements. Application here: Malaysia's own colour-coded system (as legislated in the Environmental Quality (Clinical Waste) Regulations 2009) is a direct national transposition of this WHO scheme, and the case study's comparison of treatment technologies (incineration, autoclave, chemical disinfection, microwave, pyrolysis, plasma gasification) mirrors the WHO guide's technology-ranking logic.

One Health Framework - Publisher: jointly promoted by WHO, the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH, formerly OIE), and UNEP, formalised as the "One Health Quadripartite" collaboration (2021-2022). Structure: a collaborative, cross-sectoral approach recognising that human health, animal health, and environmental/ecosystem health are interdependent, and that effective public health interventions (particularly for zoonoses, antimicrobial resistance, and food safety) require simultaneous, coordinated action across all three domains rather than siloed human-health-only responses. Application here: the case study's framing of pandemic waste as harming "human, animal, and ecosystem health simultaneously" is a direct application of One Health logic, and is increasingly the standard framework donors and WHO expect national pandemic-preparedness plans to explicitly incorporate.

Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and Their Disposal - Publisher: United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP), adopted 1989, entered into force 1992; Malaysia is a party. Structure: requires prior informed consent before hazardous waste (including certain categories of clinical/infectious waste) crosses international borders, and obliges parties to minimise the generation and transboundary movement of hazardous waste and manage it in an environmentally sound manner as close as possible to its source. Application here: relevant supplementary context for the case study's recommendation on "ASEAN harmonisation" of clinical waste standards, since any regional emergency-sharing of treatment capacity across borders would need to operate within Basel Convention obligations.

7. Transportation-Related Illness
Deep-dive learning notes · compiled from lecture material (anonymised) · DrPH specialist level
📚Overview

This topic examines the health impacts of transportation systems, with a primary focus on Road Traffic Injury (RTI), as a global and Malaysian public health problem. It covers types of transportation and who is exposed, the spectrum of transportation-related physical and mental illnesses, the burden and risk factors for RTI, vulnerable groups, the DPSEEA framework for organizing a systems-level response, the public health roles in injury prevention, and the Haddon Matrix as the classic tool for structuring injury prevention and control strategies.

📋Key Definitions & Concepts
  • Injury (WHO definition): physical damage that occurs when a human body is suddenly or briefly subjected to intolerable levels of energy.
  • Road Traffic Injury (RTI): any injury due to a crash involving one or more vehicles and originating or terminating on a public roadway (Razak et al., 2005).
  • DPSEA/DPSEEA framework: a framework (Driving force, Pressure, State, Exposure, Effect, Action) used as a global perspective for managing transportation-related illnesses; requires multifaceted action targeting multiple points in the causal chain.
  • Haddon Matrix (Haddon, 1972): a systems-approach matrix crossing three temporal phases of a crash (pre-event, event, post-event) with three factor categories (host, agent/vehicle & equipment, environment).
  • Vulnerable road users: population subgroups at disproportionately higher risk of death/injury in traffic - pedestrians, cyclists, motorcyclists, children, and the elderly.
  • Injury severity scoring terms: GCS (Glasgow Coma Scale), AIS (Abbreviated Injury Scale), ISS (Injury Scoring System), TRISS (Trauma Score Injury Severity Score), RTS (Revised Trauma Score).
  • Cost-benefit analysis vs. cost-effectiveness analysis: the former assesses economic costs of investment projects relative to their benefits; the latter measures the number of road users killed/injured avoided by a road safety measure relative to its cost.
🔬Benefits of Transportation and Malaysian Context

Transportation is a basic human need, enabling access to food, employment, education, health care, maintenance of relationships, and country development. However, the slides note that Malaysia has a very poor public transport system, characterized by a lack of service quality and a low rate of public usage (Almselati et al., 2011).

📊Types of Transportation

Land transport (rail, road - the most dominant mode), Air, Water (sea, river, canal).

⚖️Who is Closely Related to Transportation? (Exposed Populations)

Driver/pilot, passenger, conductor, porter, counter services staff, maintenance staff, steward, traffic controller, navigator, manufacturer, rescuer, and others such as pedestrians and cyclists.

🏥Classification of Transportation-Related Illnesses
  1. Physical injuries: road traffic injuries, other work injuries.
  2. Illnesses: pollution-related illness, noise-related illness, musculoskeletal pain/ergonomic illness, metabolic syndrome, heat exhaustion.
  3. Mental illness: stress.
  4. Others: fatigue, shift work effects.
🛡️The Injury Pyramid (Pyramid of Effects)

(Lithuanian Health Information Centre, 2004): death (1, base reference); injuries resulting in hospitalization (14); injuries resulting in ambulatory/emergency treatment/outpatient clinic admissions (79); injuries treated by paramedics only or at home (unquantified, "thousands"). Key teaching point: mortality statistics alone drastically underestimate the true burden of road traffic injury.

📈Burden of Transportation-Related Illness (Global)

(Preventing Road Traffic Injury: A Public Health Perspective For Europe, 2004): 2.4 million people injured and 127 thousand killed every year. Road traffic deaths and injuries were forecast to rise by 65% between 2000 and 2020, with an 80% rise projected in low- and middle-income countries. Road traffic injury draws a cost of roughly 2% of a country's GDP. The majority of deaths are among "vulnerable road users" - pedestrians and cyclists have a fatality risk per distance travelled 7-9 times higher than for car travel; fatal motorcycle accidents account for 60% of RTI deaths in Malaysia (Kareem, 2003).

💩Malaysian Trends

(MIROS, 2011): fatality trends plotted against registered vehicles over time, illustrating the relationship between motorization growth and road fatality trends.

🏷️Risk for Road Traffic Injury (RTI): Four-Element Risk Model
  1. Exposure excess (need to travel) - driven by social, economic, job-related, transport growth factors.
  2. Risk of crash, given exposure - unsafe behaviour: 40% of motor vehicle accidents (MVA) in Malaysia caused by following the vehicle in front too closely; 13% by dangerous overtaking; 12% by over-speeding; distractions (cell-phone usage) implicated in 20-30% of fatal accidents; hazardous environment (dangerous cornering implicated in 26% of MVA causation); lack of regulations/enforcement/safety training/culture.
  3. Risk of injury, given a crash - hazardous environment; protective measures (helmet use, internal car compartment design).
  4. Risk of severity/death - hazardous environment; weaknesses in emergency services/trauma care; economic conditions/policies leading to poor transport quality.
💧Vulnerable Groups

Male drivers (associated with risky driving behaviour); young people aged 15-29; child pedestrians/cyclists (evolving traffic-coping ability, limited visibility, drivers focus on other vehicles not children, undeveloped physical/cognitive skills, smaller stature); elderly (physical frailty, transport systems failing to meet needs, osteoporosis, impaired homeostasis, decreased alertness/response, poor eyesight/judgment/hearing).

⚡DPSEA (DPSEEA) Framework - Detailed Components

(Solanki, Ahamed, Gupta, & Nongkynrih, 2016, Indian Journal of Community Medicine, 41(1), 16) Driving Force: political, social and economic transition; industrialization; motorization; urbanization. Pressure: economic pressure, productivity pressure, competition pressure, peer pressure, time pressure. Exposure Parameters: number/type of vehicles, vehicle miles travelled, air pollution parameters, number of people exposed in excess of guideline values, biological monitoring data (e.g., blood lead, blood COHb). Effects (health-related): life-long disability, death, hospital admissions/ED visits, injury severity (GCS, AIS, ISS, TRISS, RTS), Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (including phobic travel anxiety), occupational disease, transport-related health effects from air pollution, global warming, noise, and increasingly sedentary lifestyles.

📚Economic Costs - Public Health Perspective

Loss to GDP, loss of manpower (especially young), loss of productivity/earnings, property damage, hospital costs (emergency, trauma, rehabilitation). Malaysia example: in 1999 alone, general insurers paid RM1.67 billion, averaging RM4.6 million per day, on motor claims (Kareem, 2003).

📋Public Health Roles in RTI Prevention and Control

Overall aims: raise awareness, reduce risk, reduce impact.

  1. Injury Surveillance - systematically collecting data on magnitude, scope, characteristics, and consequences of road traffic crashes.
  2. Research - investigating causes/correlates of road crash injury, risk-increasing/decreasing factors, factors modifiable through intervention.
  3. Policy - translating science-based information into policies protecting pedestrians, cyclists, vehicle occupants. Current Malaysian policies/acts: The Road Transport Act 1987 [Act 333]; Malaysian Institute of Road Safety Research Act 2012 (Act 748); Road Transport Rules; Environmental Quality Act 1974 (requiring HIA for large road-building projects).
  4. Services - implementing promising interventions, disseminating outcomes, evaluating cost-effectiveness. Pre-hospital (bystander response, EMS access), hospital care, rehabilitation (physical/occupational therapists, prosthetists, neuropsychologists, speech therapists).
  5. Advocacy - persuading policy-makers of the necessity to address injuries as a major public health issue.
  6. Prevention, Control, Evaluation - designing, implementing, monitoring, evaluating interventions, feeding back into revision of the Haddon Matrix.

A scientific approach is essential in every country so that road safety policies are founded on reliable data and matched to each region's financial resources. Multisectoral commitment is required.

📈Framework Deep-Dive: Key Published Frameworks Explained

The Haddon Matrix (Haddon, 1972) - Publisher: William Haddon Jr., first Administrator of the US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration; published in the American Journal of Public Health, 1972 ("A logical framework for categorizing highway safety phenomena and activities"). Structure: a 3x3 matrix crossing three temporal phases of any injury event (pre-event/crash prevention, event/injury prevention during the crash, and post-event/life-sustaining) against three factor categories (host - the person; agent/vehicle - the vector of energy transfer; and environment - physical and socio-cultural). Each of the nine cells identifies a distinct category of intervention point (e.g. pre-event/host = driver training; event/agent = crash-protective vehicle design; post-event/environment = EMS response time). Application here: the Haddon Matrix is presented in the lecture as the master organising tool for road traffic injury prevention, and remains, more than 50 years after publication, the standard framework taught in injury-epidemiology curricula worldwide, including as the analytic scaffold WHO itself uses in its Global Status Report on Road Safety.

DPSEEA Framework (Driving force-Pressure-State-Exposure-Effect-Action) - Publisher: originally developed by WHO in the 1990s (Corvalan, Briggs and Kjellstrom) as a general environmental-health indicator framework, subsequently adapted to specific domains including transportation health (as cited via Solanki et al., 2016). Structure: traces a causal chain from underlying societal Driving forces (e.g. urbanisation, motorization) through the Pressures they create (e.g. increased vehicle-kilometres travelled), the resulting State of the environment (e.g. road/vehicle condition, congestion), human Exposure to that state (e.g. air pollutant concentration, crash risk), the health Effects that follow (injury, disease), and finally the Actions available to intervene at any point in the chain. Application here: the DPSEEA structure is used in the lecture to demonstrate that transportation health is not limited to crash injury but extends to air pollution, noise, and sedentary-lifestyle effects operating through the same causal chain, and it is a widely used framework in environmental health indicator development more broadly (e.g. by WHO Europe for environmental burden-of-disease reporting).

WHO "Safe System" Approach (WHO Global Status Report on Road Safety) - Publisher: World Health Organization, formal basis of the UN Decade of Action for Road Safety (2021-2030). Structure (supplementary, not named in the slides but a natural extension of the Haddon Matrix logic taught): the Safe System approach holds that humans will always make mistakes and that the transport system itself (not just individual behaviour) should be designed to prevent those mistakes from resulting in death or serious injury, organised around five pillars - safe road users, safe vehicles, safe roads and roadsides, safe speeds, and post-crash response. This is a useful contemporary complement for DrPH students building on the classical Haddon Matrix taught in this lecture.

💩Public Health Relevance

Transportation-related illness sits at the intersection of injury epidemiology, environmental health, and health systems policy. Road traffic injury alone produces a disproportionate burden in low- and middle-income countries such as Malaysia, where motorization has outpaced investment in road safety infrastructure, enforcement, and public transport quality. The injury pyramid illustrates why mortality statistics chronically understate true burden, and the four-element risk model demonstrates that RTI is not a single random event but a product of layered, modifiable systemic failures. Beyond acute injury, the DPSEA framework explicitly connects transportation to broader environmental and occupational health outcomes: air pollution, noise, climate impact, sedentary-lifestyle-related metabolic disease, and occupational illness among transport workers.

  • A retrospective cohort study following hospital-admitted RTI patients from admission through rehabilitation to quantify long-term disability outcomes and incidence of post-traumatic stress disorder/phobic travel anxiety.

8. Sewage and Wastewater Case Study
Deep-dive learning notes · compiled from lecture material (anonymised) · DrPH specialist level
📚Overview

This topic examines how sewage and wastewater affect human health through direct occupational exposure (sewage/sanitation workers) and indirect community exposure (contaminated drinking water, food, seafood, floodwater, and antimicrobial resistance transmission). It integrates global case studies (from 1854 London to 2025 Texas), a technical review of treatment processes, dose-response/risk-assessment concepts, and a Malaysia-specific case study built around a field visit to Indah Water Konsortium (IWK) Kelantan, covering infrastructure, regulatory frameworks, and public health surveillance links.

📋Key Definitions & Concepts
  • Wastewater: a complex mixture of water generated from domestic, agricultural, and industrial activities, containing physical (solids), chemical (detergents, metals), and biological (bacteria, viruses) contaminants.
  • Domestic wastewater: sewage from households, divided into greywater (sinks, laundry, bathing) and blackwater (toilet waste).
  • Leachate: liquid formed when rainwater passes through solid waste, dissolving pollutants.
  • H2S "silent killer": hydrogen sulfide gas that paralyzes olfactory nerves at high concentration so workers can no longer smell the danger; mechanism - inhibits cytochrome c-oxidase, blocking mitochondrial ATP production, halting cellular aerobic respiration.
  • Manual scavenging: manually removing human excrement with minimal/no PPE; most extreme form of direct sewage exposure, persisting in parts of South Asia/Africa despite legal prohibition.
  • AMR (antimicrobial resistance) via wastewater: WWTPs act as reservoirs/amplifiers of antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB) and resistance genes (ARGs).
  • Individual Septic Tanks (ISTs): on-site systems for single dwellings with population equivalent (PE) <=150 where no central sewerage exists; only partially treat sewage.
  • QMRA (Quantitative Microbial Risk Assessment): framework linking pathogen dose, infectivity, and host factors to probability of infection/disease.
🔬Wastewater Treatment Process (Stages)
  1. Influent receipt - raw wastewater enters the treatment plant.
  2. Preliminary treatment - removal of large debris and grit.
  3. Primary treatment - removal of suspended solids via sedimentation.
  4. Secondary treatment - microorganisms break down organic matter (biological treatment).
  5. Tertiary treatment - advanced filtration and nutrient removal, followed by disinfection.
  6. Sludge management - treated sludge safely disposed of or reused as an energy source.

Mechanised sewage treatment (IWK Kelantan example) uses integrated electro-mechanical systems (pumps & blowers, bar screens, scrapers & clarifiers, sludge dewatering centrifuges), giving a smaller land footprint, faster treatment, and better odour control, at higher initial investment cost.

📊Global Disease Burden of Waterborne Illness

~2 billion people lack access to safe drinking water. ~3.4 million deaths annually linked to inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH). ~88% of diarrhoeal diseases associated with unsafe water and poor sanitation. Diarrhoea is the second leading cause of death in children under 5 globally - approximately 525,000 deaths annually attributable to unsafe water/sanitation.

⚖️Hazard Profile of Wastewater

Biological hazards: bacterial (E. coli, Salmonella, Vibrio, Leptospira - diarrhoea, cholera, typhoid, leptospirosis), viral (Hepatitis A, Rotavirus, Norovirus), protozoa (Giardia, Cryptosporidium), helminths (chronic intestinal infection, malnutrition). Chemical & physical hazards: toxic gases (hydrogen sulfide, methane, ammonia accumulating in confined spaces), heavy metals (mercury, lead, cadmium, arsenic - bioaccumulate, organ damage), pharmaceutical residues (contribute to AMR), physical contaminants (sludge, suspended solids, bioaerosols).

🛡️Waterborne Pathogens - Clinical Details

Bacterial: E. coli O157:H7 (bloody diarrhoea, haemolytic uraemic syndrome/kidney failure); Campylobacter jejuni (gastroenteritis, rare Guillain-Barre syndrome); Vibrio cholerae (profuse watery diarrhoea, severe dehydration); Salmonella typhi (typhoid fever, intestinal perforation); Leptospira spp. (leptospirosis, Weil's disease). Viral/protozoal/helminthic: Hepatitis A & E (liver inflammation, jaundice; HEV especially dangerous in pregnancy); Cryptosporidium/Giardia (prolonged diarrhoea, malabsorption); Ascaris/Hookworm (anaemia, malnutrition).

📈Case Studies (Direct - Occupational Exposure)

Case Study 1 - Trinity County, Texas, H2S Triple Fatality (2025): three municipal sewer workers responding to a backup died in a cascade rescue failure; root causes included no permit-required confined space program and lack of PPE/SCBA. Lesson: never enter a confined space after a collapsed colleague without full SCBA and rescue harness. Case Study 2 - Outdoor H2S Fatality, Leachate Tanker Workers (Italy, 2019): a geyser-like H2S release from a landfill leachate tanker killed two operators outdoors, demonstrating that outdoor settings are not automatically safe. Case Study 3 - Hepatitis A Outbreak, Sewage Plant Workers (France): three sewage treatment workers developed confirmed HAV infection with no other risk factor besides occupational exposure; anti-HAV prevalence in sewage workers found to be 80.5% vs 48.1% in office workers in related literature (OR 2.0-3.73).

💩Indirect Pathways (Community-Level) and Case Studies

Six main indirect pathways: faulty/leaking ISTs contaminating shallow groundwater/wells; sewage overflow during flooding; untreated effluent discharge to rivers; wastewater irrigation of crops; bioaerosols from WWTP/lagoons; contaminated seafood harvest.

Case Study 4 - The Broad Street Pump, John Snow (London, 1854): first documented proof that sewage-contaminated water causes infectious disease, preceding germ theory. Snow mapped cholera cases and identified the Broad Street pump as epicentre; removing the pump handle stopped the epidemic - the founding case for the global sanitation movement.

Case Study 5 - Walkerton, Ontario, E. coli Drinking Water Disaster (Canada, 2000): heavy rainfall washed E. coli O157:H7 and Campylobacter jejuni from cattle manure into a shallow well; inadequate chlorination by untrained operators. 2,300 seriously ill (46% of town population), 7 deaths. Long-term effects included chronic kidney disease, liver damage, and irritable bowel syndrome years later; a 9-month judicial inquiry led to new Ontario Drinking Water Safety Regulations.

Case Study 6 - Cholera Outbreak, Kampala City Slums (Uganda, 2019): heavy rainfall washed faecal matter into unprotected shallow wells; case-control design confirmed unprotected well water as the primary exposure, while the piped water system remained uncontaminated - demonstrating the protective effect of treated piped supply.

Case Study 7 - Typhoid Outbreak from Contaminated Well Water (Pakistan, 2009): over 300 ill within one week from a well contaminated with dead animals, faecal matter, and garbage; multidrug-resistant Salmonella Typhi found in 100% of well water samples.

🏷️Emerging Threat: AMR from Wastewater

Pathway: antibiotics consumed -> excreted into wastewater -> WWTP cannot fully remove antibiotic-resistant bacteria and resistance genes -> resistant bacteria enter rivers/soil -> community exposure. Global sewage surveillance detected antibiotic resistance genes in sewage from 101 countries (Nature Communications 2022). XDR Typhoid Outbreak, Karachi/Sindh, Pakistan (2018-2019): world's first large-scale extensively drug-resistant typhoid outbreak, over 10,000 confirmed/suspected cases, linked to municipal water contamination with human sewage. Ganga River, India: supports ~500 million people; more than 60% of sewage entering the river is untreated, making it a reservoir for cholera, typhoid, dysentery, hepatitis A, and AMR bacteria.

💧Vulnerable Populations

Children under 5: diarrhoea is the 2nd leading cause of death (~525,000 deaths/year from unsafe water/sanitation); repeated GI infection leads to malnutrition and impaired cognitive/developmental outcomes. Pregnant women: Hepatitis E mortality 15-25% in pregnancy vs <1% general population; Listeria monocytogenes linked to miscarriage, stillbirth, neonatal sepsis.

⚡Mechanisms, Dose-Response & Risk Assessment

Six portals of entry: ingestion (highest risk - cholera, typhoid, Hep A/E), inhalation (bioaerosols - WWTP workers, nearby residents), skin contact (sewage workers, flood victims, irrigation farmers), eye/mucosa (splashing), needle-stick (rare but documented), transplacental (Listeria, Toxoplasma, Hep E crossing the placenta).

Dose-response examples: Norovirus (ID50 18 viral particles), Cryptosporidium (<10 oocysts, chlorine-resistant), E. coli O157:H7 (10-100 cells), Vibrio cholerae (10^8 cells in normal host), Salmonella typhi (~10^3-10^6 cells), Hepatitis A virus (~10-100 particles, heat-stable). Risk modifiers that increase susceptibility: immunocompromise, malnutrition, age extremes, pregnancy, chronic liver/kidney disease, no prior immunity. Risk modifiers that decrease susceptibility: vaccination, prior immunity, water treatment/boiling, PPE, hand hygiene, sanitation infrastructure.

📚Malaysia-Specific Context and Risks

Annual monsoon flooding affects Kelantan, Terengganu, Sabah, Johor; an estimated 1.2 million un-desludged ISTs pose overflow risk during floods, with documented leptospirosis case spikes following flood events.

Leptospirosis in Malaysia: bacterial zoonosis, rodents are the primary reservoir; incubation 2-30 days; major public health concern with case spikes after floods; high-risk occupational groups include sewage workers, paddy farmers, military personnel, recreational water users; clinical spectrum ranges from mild fever to severe Weil's disease (acute liver/renal failure, pulmonary haemorrhage); case fatality rate 5-40% for the severe form.

IWK Kelantan Field Visit Case Study: Kelantan was excluded from IWK's original 1994 concession; IWK took over Kelantan sewerage on 1 January 2021, inheriting 33 public sewage treatment plants (STPs), 13 network pump stations, ~81,275 individual septic tanks, and ~259,343 traditional toilet tanks - many using older oxidation-pond technology with inadequate nutrient removal. Auditor-General's Report 2024: rural sewerage coverage in Kelantan only 63% (below national average); nationally, 94.2% of septic tanks scheduled for desludging 2021-2023 were not cleaned.

IWK National Sewage Management - seven key challenges: (1) poor public awareness ("out of sight, out of mind" mindset); (2) rising operational costs; (3) low bill-payment commitment; (4) Individual Septic Tanks - over 1 million ISTs nationally, with mandatory desludging every 2 years under the Water Services Industry Act (Desludging Services) 2021, effective 30 March 2021; (5) rapid urbanisation/industrialisation; (6) low sewerage tariff - domestic tariff unchanged since 1994 at RM8/month vs actual operating cost >RM17/month per premise; (7) illegal discharge - 29 illegal-discharge cases at IWK STPs in 2021.

Sustainability commitment (Green Technology Master Plan): target 100% reuse of sludge (biosolids) by 2030 and 33% recycling of treated effluent by 2030.

📋Prevention Framework

For sewage workers: mandatory confined-space entry permits, atmospheric testing before every entry, SCBA (not just masks), no untrained colleague rescue, Hepatitis A + B vaccination mandate, full PPE, regular medical surveillance. For communities: piped treated drinking water for all, mandatory IST desludging every 2 years (enforced), public health advisories during/post-flood, avoidance of floodwater contact, typhoid/Hepatitis A vaccination in endemic areas. For wastewater management: full treatment before discharge, tertiary treatment for irrigation-destined wastewater, AMR/ARG monitoring at STP effluent points, enforcement of penalties for illegal discharge.

📊Framework Deep-Dive: Key Published Frameworks Explained

WHO Water Safety Planning and QMRA (Quantitative Microbial Risk Assessment) Framework - Publisher: World Health Organization (Water Safety Plan methodology, as also detailed in the GDWQ) and the broader QMRA scientific literature (foundational work summarised in WHO/Bull WHO publications, e.g. Mara et al.). Structure: QMRA formally links four elements - hazard identification (which pathogen), exposure assessment (dose received via a defined pathway), dose-response assessment (probability of infection/illness given a specific dose, often modelled via exponential or beta-Poisson mathematical models), and risk characterisation (population-level burden, e.g. expressed as annual disease burden in DALYs) - to translate a measured or estimated pathogen concentration in water into an estimated human health risk. Application here: the topic's dose-response table (Norovirus ID50 18 particles, Cryptosporidium <10 oocysts, etc.) is exactly the kind of dose-response input QMRA models require, and this framework is the standard quantitative tool a DrPH researcher would use to formally estimate infection risk from a given level of sewage contamination in drinking or recreational water, rather than relying only on descriptive epidemiology.

WHO Guidelines for the Safe Use of Wastewater, Excreta and Greywater (WHO, 2006, multi-volume) - Publisher: World Health Organization. Structure: introduces a "multiple-barrier" risk-reduction approach for wastewater reuse in agriculture, combining several partial risk-reduction measures in series (e.g. wastewater treatment + restricted crop selection + irrigation technique + pathogen die-off time before harvest + food washing/cooking) so that the cumulative effect achieves an acceptable health-based target, rather than relying on any single barrier (such as treatment alone) to achieve full pathogen removal. Application here: directly relevant supplementary framework for the topic's wastewater-irrigation-linked disease case studies (salmonellosis, cholera, Hepatitis A among farmers/consumers), and for evaluating whether Malaysian agricultural wastewater reuse practices meet an internationally recognised multi-barrier standard.

Water Services Industry Act (Desludging Services) 2021 (Malaysia) - Publisher: Parliament of Malaysia / Ministry of Environment and Water (then KASA). Structure: creates a legally mandated desludging cycle (every 2 years) for Individual Septic Tanks, assigns enforcement and vendor-licensing responsibility (SPAN-registered Permit E vendors), and is intended to close the gap between the very large number of ISTs nationally and historically low voluntary desludging compliance. Application here: this is the direct legal mechanism the case study cites as (on paper) addressing the 94.2% national desludging non-compliance figure, and is the natural regulatory "intervention" a DrPH quasi-experimental study on IST-related disease incidence would evaluate for policy impact.

⚖️Public Health Relevance

Sewage and wastewater mismanagement sits at the intersection of occupational health, environmental health, and infectious disease control. Direct occupational exposure demonstrates that sewage work remains one of the most hazardous occupations globally, with real, recent, and preventable fatalities from H2S and documented infectious risks. Indirect community exposure shows that sewage-contaminated drinking water remains among the world's largest causes of preventable death and chronic morbidity, disproportionately affecting children, pregnant women, and the immunocompromised. The emerging AMR dimension reframes wastewater management as central to combating antimicrobial resistance. In the Malaysian context, the combination of over a million poorly-desludged ISTs, recurrent monsoon flooding, endemic leptospirosis, and the specific infrastructural and governance gaps revealed in the IWK Kelantan case demonstrates that sewerage management is inseparable from drinking water safety.
