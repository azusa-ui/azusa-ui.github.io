🖥️ SPSS Interface — Data View vs Variable View

SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) is a point-and-click statistics package widely used in health and social sciences. Every dataset window ("Data Editor") has two tabs at the bottom-left:

ViewWhat it showsWhat you do here
Data ViewThe actual spreadsheet of data — one row per case/subject, one column per variableEnter or inspect raw values (e.g., subject 1's age = 45, sex = 1)
Variable ViewOne row per variable, describing its propertiesDefine Name, Type, Width, Decimals, Label, Values, Missing, Columns, Align, Measure, Role for every variable before or while entering data

Key Variable View columns to always set correctly (this is where most data-entry marks are lost in practicals/exams):

  • Name — short internal variable name (no spaces), e.g. age, sex, sbp.
  • Type — usually Numeric (for continuous or coded categorical data) or String (free text, generally avoided for variables you plan to analyse).
  • Label — the full descriptive text shown in output, e.g. Name = sex, Label = "Respondent's sex".
  • Values — value labels for coded categorical variables, e.g. for sex: 1 = "Male", 2 = "Female"; for smoke: 0 = "Non-smoker", 1 = "Smoker". Always assign value labels for every categorical variable — SPSS output and crosstabs will otherwise show meaningless numeric codes.
  • Missing — user-defined missing-value codes (e.g. 99 = "Not answered") so SPSS excludes them from calculations rather than treating 99 as a real value.
  • Measure — the level of measurement: Nominal, Ordinal, or Scale (interval/ratio). SPSS uses this to decide which menu options/icons are offered for a variable, and getting it wrong (e.g. leaving a Likert item as "Scale") can silently make certain non-parametric procedures unavailable or mislabelled.
A dataset with correctly completed Variable View is what separates a usable, auditable dataset from an unreadable column of numbers — always complete Variable View before you start entering data (or immediately after importing).

1. Descriptive Statistics

📊 Measures of Central Tendency

Central tendency summarises an entire distribution with one 'typical' value. The right choice depends on the variable's level of measurement (nominal / ordinal / interval / ratio) and its shape (symmetric vs skewed):

  • Mean (x̄ = Σx / n) — the arithmetic balance point of the data; uses every observation, so a single extreme value pulls it toward the tail.
  • Median — the middle value once data are ranked low→high (average of the two central values when n is even); splits the sample exactly 50/50 and is unaffected by extreme values, making it the preferred summary for skewed data (e.g., income, length of stay, viral load).
  • Mode — the most frequently occurring value; the only valid measure for pure nominal data (e.g., blood group) and useful for describing multimodal distributions (e.g., bimodal age-at-onset when a disease has both congenital and acquired causes).
  • Geometric mean = ⁿ√(x₁·x₂·…·xₙ), equivalently exp(mean of ln x) — used for log-normally distributed biomarkers (antibody titres, viral load, serum IgE) where multiplicative/ratio changes are more meaningful than additive ones.
MeasureBest useSensitive to outliers?
MeanSymmetric / interval-ratio dataYes
MedianSkewed data / ordinal dataNo

For skewed data, report median (IQR) rather than mean (SD) — and state this explicitly in a results table, since readers otherwise default to assuming mean ± SD.

🧮 Worked example — mean vs median in a skewed sample▼

Length of stay (days) for 9 ward patients: 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 40 (one patient had a prolonged, complicated admission).

  • Mean = (2+3+3+4+4+5+5+6+40) / 9 = 72 / 9 = 8.0 days — inflated by the single outlier.
  • Median = the 5th ranked value = 4 days — much closer to the typical patient's experience.
  • Mode = 3 and 4 days (bimodal, each occurring twice).

Because mean ≫ median, the distribution is right- (positively) skewed: a long tail of a few long-stay patients drags the mean upward. Comparing mean and median before choosing which to report is a classic exam trap to watch for (Gordis, Epidemiology).

📏 Measures of Dispersion

A measure of central tendency alone can hide important differences — two groups can share the same mean but very different spread, which often has direct clinical meaning (e.g., glycaemic variability, blood pressure variability). Key measures:

  • Range = maximum − minimum; simple but unstable, driven entirely by the two most extreme values.
  • Variance (s²) = Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1) for a sample — the average squared deviation from the mean; the (n−1) divisor ("Bessel's correction") corrects the downward bias that arises because x̄ is itself estimated from the same data.
  • Standard deviation (SD, s) = √variance — expressed in the original units, so more directly interpretable than variance.
  • Interquartile range (IQR) = Q3 − Q1, the middle 50% of ranked data; robust to outliers/skew, pairs naturally with the median.
  • Coefficient of variation (CV) = SD / mean (×100%) — unit-free, allowing comparison of variability across variables measured on different scales (e.g., birth weight in grams vs gestational age in weeks).
  • Standard error (SE) = SD / √n — not a measure of spread of the raw data, but of the precision of the sample mean as an estimate of the population mean; SE shrinks as n grows, SD does not.
SD describes the spread of individual observations; SE describes the precision of an estimate (e.g., the sample mean). Confusing "mean ± SD" (describing the sample) with "mean ± SE" or a 95% CI (describing precision of the estimate) is one of the most common reporting errors in student write-ups.

🔔 Distributions

A distribution describes how values of a variable are spread across its possible range. Three distributions recur constantly in health data:

  • Normal (Gaussian) distribution — symmetric bell curve fully described by mean μ and SD σ. Many continuous biological variables (height, BP within a homogeneous group) approximate normal, and the Central Limit Theorem means sample means tend toward a normal distribution even when the underlying variable is not, provided n is reasonably large (rule of thumb: n ≥ 30).
  • Binomial distribution — the number of "successes" in n independent yes/no trials with constant probability p (e.g., number of smokers in a sample of 50); underlies proportions and their confidence intervals.
  • Poisson distribution — models rare, independent events over time or space (e.g., number of new TB cases per district per month); underlies incidence-rate analysis and rate ratios.

Empirical rule for a normal distribution: ~68% of values lie within ±1 SD of the mean, ~95% within ±2 SD (more precisely ±1.96 SD), and ~99.7% within ±3 SD.

A value's position can be standardised as a z-score: z = (x − μ) / σ — how many SDs a value sits from the mean. z-scores allow comparison of values on different scales, and are exactly how paediatric growth charts express weight-for-age or height-for-age.

Skewness describes asymmetry (positive/right skew: mean > median, long right tail; negative/left skew: mean < median, long left tail). Kurtosis describes "tailedness" — how much probability mass sits in the tails relative to a normal curve.

If data are not normal, either transform them (e.g., log-transform right-skewed variables such as viral load or CRP before applying parametric tests) or switch to the equivalent non-parametric test (see Section 3).

Assessing Normality — what to check and how

  • Sample size rule of thumb: if n > 30, normality of the sample mean can generally be assumed via the Central Limit Theorem, even if the raw variable is not perfectly normal; if n < 30, formally check normality of the raw variable.
  • Shapiro-Wilk test — preferred for small-to-moderate samples (n ≤ ~50); H0: the data are normally distributed. A significant result (p < 0.05) suggests departure from normality.
  • Kolmogorov-Smirnov (with Lilliefors correction) — used for larger samples; same H0/logic as Shapiro-Wilk, but generally less powerful for detecting non-normality in small samples.
  • Skewness and kurtosis — as a rule of thumb, values roughly within ±1 (some sources allow ±2) suggest approximate normality; dividing skewness/kurtosis by their standard errors gives a z-value, and |z| > 1.96 suggests significant departure from normality.
  • Visual checks — histogram with a normal curve overlay (bell shape vs skew), and a Q-Q plot (points should fall close to the diagonal reference line; systematic curvature indicates skew, S-shaped deviation indicates heavy/light tails).

🧮 Worked example — using z-scores and the empirical rule▼

In a population of adult men, systolic BP is approximately normal with mean μ = 120 mmHg and SD σ = 10 mmHg.

  • A man with SBP = 140 mmHg has z = (140 − 120)/10 = +2.0 — he is 2 SDs above the mean; by the empirical rule roughly the top 2.5% of men have SBP this high or higher (half of the ~5% falling outside ±2 SD).
  • The reference range covering the central 95% of the population is μ ± 1.96σ ≈ 120 ± 19.6 = 100.4 to 139.6 mmHg — this is precisely the logic used to define normal laboratory reference ranges.
🧭 Measures of Disease Frequency

Before groups can be compared, epidemiology needs a way to quantify how often disease occurs. Two families of measures matter:

  • Prevalence = (existing cases at a point in time) / (population at that time) — a snapshot; influenced by both how many people get the disease and how long they have it. In steady state, prevalence ≈ incidence rate × average duration of disease.
  • Cumulative incidence (incidence proportion / attack rate) = (new cases during a period) / (population at risk at the start of the period) — a proportion, unitless, requires a fixed follow-up period and a population free of the outcome at baseline.
  • Incidence rate (incidence density) = (new cases during a period) / (total person-time at risk) — accounts for varying follow-up time between individuals (loss to follow-up, staggered entry); expressed as events per person-year (or per 1,000 / 100,000 person-years).
Prevalence is affected by anything that changes disease duration — better treatment that prolongs survival increases prevalence without any change in incidence (Porta, A Dictionary of Epidemiology). Always ask whether a "rising rate" reflects more new cases or people living longer with existing disease.
🧮 Worked example — incidence rate using person-time▼

A cohort of 500 factory workers is followed for up to 5 years. 20 develop occupational asthma. Follow-up times vary — most complete all 5 years, but some leave employment early (censored).

Total person-time accrued across the cohort = 2,150 person-years.

Incidence rate = 20 cases / 2,150 person-years = 0.0093 per person-year = 9.3 cases per 1,000 person-years.

Contrast with the cumulative incidence if everyone had completed the full 5 years: 20/500 = 4.0% over 5 years. The incidence rate uses person-time (denominator shrinks as people leave), while cumulative incidence treats the denominator as fixed — this is why loss to follow-up and staggered recruitment make incidence rates the more appropriate measure in most open cohort studies.

2. Hypothesis Testing & Estimation
Source: GKT 515 Course Notes — Hypothesis Testing and Estimation; Sample Size & Power
🎯 Core Concepts

Hypothesis testing is a formal procedure for deciding whether observed data are compatible with a stated assumption about the population. The general logic follows five steps:

  1. State H0 and H1. H0 (null): no effect / no difference / no association in the population. H1 (alternative): an effect exists — can be two-sided (≠, the default, no assumed direction) or one-sided (> or <, used only when a directional effect is the sole clinically plausible outcome and is specified a priori).
  2. Choose a significance level α (conventionally 0.05) — the pre-specified threshold probability of wrongly rejecting a true H0.
  3. Select and compute an appropriate test statistic (t, χ², F, z, etc.) based on data type, number of groups, and distributional assumptions.
  4. Compare the test statistic to its reference distribution to obtain a p-value.
  5. Decide: if p < α, reject H0 in favour of H1 ("statistically significant"); otherwise, fail to reject H0 — which is never the same as "proving H0 true".
Choosing a one-sided test only after seeing the data (to make a borderline result look "significant") is a form of p-hacking and is considered poor practice — the direction must be justified and stated before analysis begins.
⚖️ Type I / II Errors & Power

Reject H0Type I error (α)Correct decision (1−β, power)
Fail to rejectCorrect decision (1−α)Type II error (β)
  • Type I error (α) — a "false positive": concluding an effect exists when it does not. Controlled directly by the significance threshold you set.
  • Type II error (β) — a "false negative": failing to detect a real effect. Commonly accepted at β = 0.20 (i.e., 80% power).
  • Power = 1−β — probability of correctly detecting a true effect of a given size; aim for ≥0.80 (some fields require ≥0.90).
  • Power increases with larger sample size, a larger true effect size, a higher α (looser threshold, but at the cost of more Type I risk), and lower outcome variability.
A 'non-significant' result is not proof of no effect — it may simply reflect an under-powered study (absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence). Always check whether a 'null' study's CI is wide enough to still include a clinically important effect.
🧮 Sample Size & Power Calculations

Sample-size calculations are performed before data collection so that a study has an adequate chance of detecting a pre-specified, clinically meaningful effect. Standard formulae share the same ingredients:

  • α — Type I error tolerance, usually 0.05 two-sided → Zα/2 ≈ 1.96.
  • Power — usually 1−β = 0.80 → Zβ ≈ 0.84 (or 0.90 → Zβ ≈ 1.28).
  • Effect size the study is designed to detect — the smallest difference considered clinically important, not the difference you hope to find.
  • Variability of the outcome — SD for continuous outcomes, baseline proportion for binary outcomes.

For comparing two independent means, a simplified formula is: n per group ≈ 2 × ((Z_α/2 + Z_β) × σ / δ)², where δ is the minimum clinically important difference and σ the assumed common SD.

Larger n narrows CIs and increases power, but also raises cost, time, and — in trials — the number of participants exposed to an unproven intervention; sample-size calculation is as much an ethical safeguard as a statistical one (Rothman, Modern Epidemiology).
🧮 Worked example — sample size for comparing two means▼

A trial compares fasting glucose (mmol/L) between a lifestyle-intervention arm and a control arm. Pilot data suggest SD σ ≈ 1.2 mmol/L. The study should have 80% power (Zβ = 0.84) to detect a clinically important difference of δ = 0.5 mmol/L at two-sided α = 0.05 (Zα/2 = 1.96).

n per group ≈ 2 × ((1.96 + 0.84) × 1.2 / 0.5)² = 2 × (2.80 × 1.2 / 0.5)² = 2 × (6.72)² = 2 × 45.2 ≈ 91 per group (≈182 total) before inflating for dropout — dividing by (1 − 0.10 expected dropout) gives ≈101 per group.

Notice how sensitive n is to δ: halving the detectable difference to 0.25 mmol/L would roughly quadruple the required sample size, since n scales with 1/δ².

🔬 p-values & Confidence Intervals

p-value = the probability of observing data as extreme as (or more extreme than) what was found, if H0 were true. A small p-value indicates the observed data are unlikely under H0.

  • Confidence interval (CI) gives a plausible range for the true population parameter; a 95% CI means that if the study were repeated many times, 95% of such intervals would contain the true value.
  • For a difference/ratio measure, a 95% CI that excludes the null value (0 for a difference, 1 for a ratio) corresponds to p<0.05 — the two are mathematically linked.
  • CI is more informative than a bare p-value because it conveys both the direction and precision (width) of the estimate, letting the reader judge clinical, not just statistical, significance.

General CI formula (large-sample, normal approximation): estimate ± Z × SE, where Z = 1.96 for a 95% CI.

p < 0.05 does not mean 'clinically important' — a tiny, trivial effect can be 'significant' with a large enough sample. Conversely, a clinically important effect in a small study can be 'non-significant'. Always interpret the point estimate and CI width alongside the p-value.
Common misinterpretations to avoid: (1) p is not the probability that H0 is true; (2) p = 0.05 does not mean "a 5% chance this specific result is due to chance"; (3) a 95% CI does not mean "a 95% probability the true value lies in this specific interval" — the true value is fixed, not random, so the probability statement applies to the long-run procedure, not to any single interval.

Reading p-values & CIs from SPSS output

Almost every SPSS test procedure below reports the same two ingredients you need, just under slightly different column headers — learning to spot them quickly is a core exam and practical skill:

🧮 Worked example — 95% CI for a mean difference▼

A study compares mean BMI reduction between a diet programme (n=40, mean = 2.4 kg, SD = 1.8 kg) and a control group (n=40, mean = 0.6 kg, SD = 1.6 kg).

Mean difference = 2.4 − 0.6 = 1.8 kg. Pooled SE ≈ √(1.8²/40 + 1.6²/40) = √(0.081 + 0.064) = √0.145 ≈ 0.381 kg.

95% CI = 1.8 ± 1.96 × 0.381 = 1.8 ± 0.747 → 1.05 to 2.55 kg. Since this excludes 0, the difference is statistically significant at α = 0.05 (equivalent to t ≈ 1.8/0.381 ≈ 4.73, p < 0.001).

3. Categorical, Parametric & Non-Parametric Tests
Source: GKT 515 Course Notes — Univariate Analysis of Categorical Data; Univariate Analysis of Numerical Data; Non-Parametric Tests; Measures of Association & Screening Tests
🧮 Chi-square, Fisher's Exact & McNemar

These tests assess whether two categorical variables are associated (independent or not) using a contingency table, or (for goodness-of-fit) whether one categorical variable's observed distribution matches a hypothesised one.

  • Chi-square goodness-of-fit test — compares one categorical variable's observed frequencies against hypothesised/expected proportions (e.g., "does brand preference match last year's 40/30/20/10 split?"). H0: no difference between observed and hypothesised distribution.
  • Chi-square (χ²) test of independence — compares observed counts in a two-variable contingency table to counts expected under independence: expected count = (row total × column total) / grand total. Requires expected counts ≥5 in at least 80% of cells (and no cell = 0; if df > 1, minimum expected frequency must be >1), otherwise the approximation is unreliable.
  • Degrees of freedom = (rows − 1) × (columns − 1); for a standard 2×2 table, df = 1.
  • Fisher's exact test — computes the exact probability directly from the hypergeometric distribution; used instead of χ² for small samples / sparse 2×2 tables where >20% of cells have expected count <5 (the alternative fix is Yates' continuity correction).
  • McNemar's test — for paired/dependent categorical data (the same subjects measured twice, e.g. BMI category before vs after an intervention), rather than two independent groups.
  • χ², Fisher's, and McNemar's all test whether an association/difference exists — they do not quantify its strength or direction. Pair them with a risk ratio, odds ratio, or difference in proportions (see below).
χ² tests independence; it does not by itself imply causation, direction, or magnitude of effect — always report an effect measure (RR/OR) alongside the test.

📐 Measures of Association & Screening Test Performance

A 2×2 table is also the starting point for quantifying strength of association and for evaluating diagnostic/screening tests.

Risk ratio, odds ratio, and number needed to treat

  • Risk ratio (RR) = risk in exposed / risk in unexposed — appropriate for cohort studies and RCTs, where incidence can be directly calculated.
  • Odds ratio (OR) = odds of outcome in exposed / odds of outcome in unexposed (equivalently, odds of exposure in cases / odds of exposure in controls) — the only valid measure in case-control studies, since true incidence cannot be calculated there; approximates RR when the outcome is rare ("rare disease assumption", generally outcome prevalence <10%).
  • Absolute risk reduction (ARR) = risk in control − risk in treatment. Number needed to treat (NNT) = 1 / ARR — the number of patients who must receive the intervention to prevent one additional adverse outcome.
🧮 Worked example — RR, OR, and NNT from a cohort▼

A cohort of 400 statin users and 400 non-users is followed for 5 years for incident MI: 20 events among statin users, 40 among non-users.

Risk (statin) = 20/400 = 0.05 (5%). Risk (non-statin) = 40/400 = 0.10 (10%).

RR = 0.05/0.10 = 0.50 — statin users have half the risk of MI compared with non-users.

OR = odds(statin)/odds(non-statin) = (20/380)/(40/360) = 0.0526/0.1111 = 0.47 — close to the RR because the outcome is relatively uncommon (~7.5% overall), illustrating the rare-disease approximation. Note the OR always sits further from 1 than the RR for a common outcome.

ARR = 0.10 − 0.05 = 0.05. NNT = 1/0.05 = 20 — 20 patients need to be treated with statins for 5 years to prevent one additional MI.

Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV

Disease +Disease −
Test +a (true positive)b (false positive)
Test −c (false negative)d (true negative)
  • Sensitivity = a/(a+c) — probability the test is positive given disease is present; a highly sensitive test, when negative, helps rule out disease (mnemonic: SnNOUT).
  • Specificity = d/(b+d) — probability the test is negative given disease is absent; a highly specific test, when positive, helps rule in disease (mnemonic: SpPIN).
  • Positive predictive value (PPV) = a/(a+b) — probability of disease given a positive test; depends heavily on prevalence, unlike sensitivity/specificity, which are intrinsic properties of the test.
  • Negative predictive value (NPV) = d/(c+d) — probability of no disease given a negative test.
Sensitivity and specificity are properties of the test and stay roughly constant across populations; PPV and NPV are properties of the test in a specific population and swing dramatically with prevalence — the same test performs very differently as a screening tool in a low-prevalence general population versus a high-prevalence referral clinic.
🧮 Worked example — sensitivity, specificity, PPV/NPV, and the effect of prevalence▼

A screening test is evaluated against a gold standard in 1,000 people, of whom 100 truly have the disease (10% prevalence): 90 true positives, 10 false negatives, 100 false positives, 800 true negatives.

Sensitivity = 90/100 = 90%. Specificity = 800/900 = 88.9%.

PPV = 90/(90+100) = 90/190 = 47.4% — fewer than half of test-positives truly have the disease, even with a fairly accurate test, because false positives (100) outnumber true positives (90) at this prevalence.

NPV = 800/(800+10) = 800/810 = 98.8% — a negative result is highly reassuring here.

Now apply the identical test in a high-risk clinic with 50% prevalence (500 diseased, 500 not): true positives = 0.90×500 = 450, false negatives = 50, false positives ≈ 0.111×500 ≈ 56, true negatives ≈ 444. PPV = 450/(450+56) ≈ 89% — the test looks far more "accurate" purely because prevalence rose, not because the test changed. This is a core reason population-wide screening in low-prevalence groups generates many false positives (WHO Wilson–Jungner screening criteria).

🔗 Correlation (Pearson & Spearman)

Correlation quantifies the strength and direction of a linear (Pearson) or monotonic (Spearman) relationship between two continuous (or ordinal) variables — it does not distinguish which variable is "outcome" vs "predictor", and does not imply causation.

  • Pearson correlation coefficient (r) — for two approximately normally distributed continuous variables; ranges from −1 to +1. r > 0: positive/direct relationship; r < 0: negative/inverse; r = 0: no linear relationship.
  • Spearman's rank correlation (ρ / rs) — the non-parametric alternative, based on ranks; used when data are ordinal, or continuous but non-normal / non-linear-but-monotonic, or when outliers distort Pearson's r.
  • Rough strength bands (magnitude of r, either sign): 0.00–0.19 very weak/negligible, 0.20–0.39 weak, 0.40–0.59 moderate, 0.60–0.79 strong, 0.80–1.00 very strong (bands vary somewhat by field/textbook).
  • r² (coefficient of determination) = proportion of variance in one variable "explained" by the other (only strictly meaningful for Pearson's r in a linear context).

Assumptions

  • Pearson: both variables continuous, approximately normally distributed, relationship is linear, and free of substantial outliers (check via scatter plot — should look roughly elliptical).
  • Spearman: variables at least ordinal; no assumption of normality or linearity, only a monotonic relationship.

Because the relationship is only moderate (not strong), age alone would be a poor single predictor of an individual's cholesterol level, even though the association is statistically significant — a reminder that statistical significance and predictive/clinical usefulness are different things.

📈 Linear Regression (OLS)

Models a continuous outcome as a linear function of one or more predictors: Y = β0 + β1X1 + β2X2 + … + ε, fitted by ordinary least squares (minimising the sum of squared residuals).

  • Each β = the change in Y for a 1-unit increase in that X, holding all other predictors constant — the "adjusted" effect.
  • β0 (intercept) = predicted Y when all X = 0 (not always meaningful if X = 0 lies outside the observed data range — consider centring predictors).
  • R² = proportion of variance in Y explained by the model (0–1); adjusted R² penalises adding uninformative predictors.
  • Assumptions: linearity, independence of observations, homoscedasticity (constant residual variance), and approximate normality of residuals (not of the raw variables themselves).
  • Multiple regression is the primary tool for adjusting for confounders in observational data; ANCOVA is simply ANOVA re-expressed as a regression with a categorical predictor plus a continuous covariate.
Regression coefficients are adjusted associations — the workhorse for confounding control when randomisation isn't possible. Adjustment only handles measured confounders; unmeasured confounding can still bias β (Rothman, Modern Epidemiology).

🧮 Worked example — interpreting a fitted regression equation▼

A model predicting systolic BP (mmHg) from age (years) and BMI (kg/m²) in 200 adults yields: SBP = 90.2 + 0.55×Age + 1.10×BMI (R² = 0.34).

  • R² = 0.34 → age and BMI jointly explain 34% of the variance in SBP in this sample; the remaining 66% reflects other factors (genetics, sodium intake, medication use, measurement error, etc.).
🔢 Logistic Regression

Models a binary outcome via the log-odds (logit): ln(odds) = β0 + β1X1 + …, so that odds = exp(β0 + β1X1 + …).

  • exp(β) = the odds ratio (OR) for a 1-unit increase in that predictor, adjusted for the other covariates in the model.
  • OR > 1 → predictor raises the odds of the outcome; OR < 1 → protective; OR = 1 → no association.
  • Adjusted OR (from multivariable logistic regression) controls for covariates, unlike a crude OR from a simple 2×2 table.
  • Model diagnostics: Hosmer–Lemeshow test for goodness-of-fit, check multicollinearity (VIF), and examine influential points (Cook's distance / leverage).
At low outcome probability, adjusted OR ≈ adjusted RR — but as baseline risk rises, OR increasingly exaggerates the RR's distance from 1. When the outcome is common and RR is the desired measure, consider Poisson regression with robust SE, or binomial regression with a log link.

Assumptions

  • Dependent variable is binary/dichotomous categorical.
  • Random sample; observations independent of each other.
  • Independent variables can be numerical or categorical (categorical predictors need indicator/dummy coding).
  • No severe multicollinearity among predictors; adequate events-per-variable (rule of thumb ≥10 events per predictor).

🧮 Worked example — converting a logistic coefficient to an OR▼

A logistic model for diabetes (yes/no) gives β(smoking) = 0.53 (SE = 0.21), adjusted for age and BMI.

OR = exp(0.53) ≈ 1.70 — smokers have 70% higher adjusted odds of diabetes than non-smokers, holding age and BMI constant.

95% CI for β: 0.53 ± 1.96(0.21) = 0.53 ± 0.41 → (0.12, 0.94). Exponentiate the CI limits (not the SE): exp(0.12) = 1.13, exp(0.94) = 2.56 → 95% CI for OR = 1.13 to 2.56, which excludes 1, so the association is statistically significant.

⚠️ Key Assumptions & Pitfalls
  • Inspect residual-vs-fitted and Q-Q plots; transform variables or use robust/generalised methods if linearity or normality of residuals is violated.
  • Avoid overfitting: a common rule of thumb for logistic regression is ≥10 events per variable (EPV); with few events, coefficients become unstable and CIs unrealistically wide.
  • Multicollinearity (highly correlated predictors) inflates standard errors and destabilises coefficient estimates — check Variance Inflation Factor (VIF); VIF > 5–10 flags a problem.
  • Distinguish confounding (a third variable distorts the exposure–outcome association and should be adjusted for) from effect modification/interaction (the exposure's effect genuinely differs across strata and should be reported separately, not averaged away) — these require different statistical handling (an interaction term vs simple adjustment).
  • Extrapolating predictions beyond the range of observed X values is unreliable — the fitted line's behaviour outside the data is unverified.

⏳ Kaplan–Meier & Log-rank

Estimates the survival function S(t) = probability of remaining event-free beyond time t, while correctly handling censoring — participants who leave the study, are lost to follow-up, or reach the end of the study period without experiencing the event (their status after that point is simply unknown, not "no event").

  • The KM curve is a step function: it drops only at observed event times; censored observations are marked with ticks and do not cause a drop, but they do leave the risk set at that point.
  • Log-rank test compares survival distributions between ≥2 groups by summing observed vs expected events at each event time — it is unadjusted (cannot control for covariates) and gives the most weight to differences later in follow-up.
  • Median survival = the time at which S(t) first drops to 0.5; unlike a mean, it can be estimated even when many subjects are still event-free (censored) at the end of the study.
Censoring is exactly why you cannot simply average "time to event" — subjects who haven't yet had the event still contribute information (they are known to have survived at least that long), and KM/survival methods are built specifically to use that partial information correctly.

⚡ Cox Proportional Hazards

A semi-parametric regression model for time-to-event data: h(t|X) = h0(t) × exp(β1X1 + β2X2 + …), where h0(t) is an unspecified baseline hazard and covariates act multiplicatively on it. Output: Hazard Ratio (HR) = exp(β).

  • HR > 1 = higher instantaneous hazard (faster time to event) associated with the predictor; HR < 1 = protective / slower time to event.
  • Proportional-hazards assumption: the HR is assumed constant across the whole follow-up period. Check with Schoenfeld residuals or by inspecting log(−log S(t)) plots for parallel curves; if violated, consider stratification or time-varying covariates.
  • Handles censoring natively and allows simultaneous adjustment for multiple predictors, unlike the (unadjusted) log-rank test.
  • Because Cox uses only the rank order of event times ("semi-parametric"), it does not require specifying the shape of the baseline hazard — a major advantage over fully parametric survival models (e.g., Weibull) when that shape is unknown.
HR ≠ RR — HR compares instantaneous event rates conditional on having survived to time t, not cumulative risk over the whole study. In occupational and toxicological cohort studies (e.g., benzene exposure and leukaemia risk), Cox HR is the standard reported effect measure.

Logic of ANOVA: partitions total variance into between-group variance and within-group (residual) variance, then compares them via the F-statistic = (between-group variance) / (within-group variance). A large F (relative to its reference F-distribution) indicates group means differ more than would be expected by chance alone.

Assumptions

  • Dependent variable numerical/continuous.
  • Random samples; groups independent of each other (except repeated-measures, where measurements within a subject are dependent by design).
  • Normal distribution of the outcome within each group — assumed if n > 30 per group (CLT), otherwise check via histogram/Shapiro-Wilk.
  • Homogeneity of variances across groups — checked via Levene's test.
  • Model-level assumptions for two-way ANOVA: normality of residuals, equal variance of residuals, overall model fitness.

🧮 Worked example — one-way ANOVA logic▼

Mean weight loss (kg) after 12 weeks on three diets: Diet A = 4.2 (n=20), Diet B = 5.8 (n=20), Diet C = 4.0 (n=20); grand mean = 4.67 kg.

A large F-statistic here (e.g., F = 6.4, df = 2, 57, p = 0.003) tells us at least one diet differs from the others — but not which. A Tukey post-hoc test might then show Diet B differs significantly from both A and C (p < 0.05), while A and C do not differ from each other — a pattern the omnibus test alone would not have revealed.

🧬 ANCOVA (Analysis of Covariance)

ANCOVA extends ANOVA by statistically adjusting group-mean comparisons for one or more continuous covariates (e.g., baseline value, age) — mathematically it is ANOVA re-expressed as a regression with a categorical group predictor plus continuous covariate(s). It increases power by removing variance attributable to the covariate before testing group differences, and reduces confounding by known continuous covariates.

Assumptions

  • All standard ANOVA assumptions (numerical dependent variable, random samples, independent groups, normality within groups, homogeneity of variances via Levene's test).
  • Covariate(s) must be numerical/continuous, reliably measured (measurement error in the covariate biases the adjustment).
  • Absence of multicollinearity between covariate(s) and factors.
  • Homogeneity of regression slopes — the relationship between the covariate and the outcome must be the same across all groups (i.e., the covariate × group interaction must be non-significant); if violated, ANCOVA is not appropriate as specified.
  • Model-level: normality of residuals, equal variance of residuals, linear relationship between each covariate and the outcome, overall model fitness.

Q1 Estimation 15 marks
A trial reports a risk difference of −0.08 (95% CI −0.12 to −0.04) for an intervention vs control. Interpret the result and state what the CI tells you.
Marks guide: 15 marks: point estimate 4 · CI meaning 4 · significance 4 · clinical translation 3
Interpretation

The intervention reduces the outcome risk by 8 percentage points compared with control (point estimate, risk difference = −0.08). For every 100 people given the intervention instead of control, approximately 8 fewer would experience the outcome.

CI meaning

We are 95% confident the true risk difference in the population lies between a reduction of 4 and 12 percentage points. Correct frequentist interpretation: if the study were repeated many times, 95% of the CIs so constructed would contain the true risk difference — it is a statement about the procedure, not a 95% probability about this one fixed interval. The interval sits entirely below zero (entirely in favour of benefit), so both bounds are consistent with a genuinely protective effect.

Significance

Because the 95% CI excludes the null value 0, this corresponds to p < 0.05 — we reject H0 of no difference between arms. Best practice is to report the estimate and CI together, not the p-value alone, since the CI additionally conveys precision.

Extension — translating RD into NNT

Number needed to treat = 1 / |RD| = 1 / 0.08 = 12.5, rounded up to 13: approximately 13 patients need to receive the intervention (rather than control) to prevent one additional adverse outcome. Applying the CI bounds gives NNT ranging from 1/0.12 ≈ 8 (best case) to 1/0.04 = 25 (worst case) — reporting this range communicates the uncertainty in NNT that a single point value hides.

Q2 Test Selection 15 marks
You compare systolic BP (continuous, non-normal) between three independent diet groups, and association between smoking (yes/no) and stroke (yes/no). Name an appropriate test for each and justify.
Marks guide: 15 marks: BP test 6 · justification 2 · smoking/stroke test 5 · justification 2
BP across 3 groups

Data are continuous but non-normal, and there are three independent groups, so the appropriate test is the Kruskal–Wallis test (the non-parametric analogue of one-way ANOVA, based on ranks rather than raw values). If BP were approximately normal (or the sample large enough for the CLT to apply to the group means), one-way ANOVA with post-hoc pairwise comparisons (e.g., Tukey) would be the parametric choice.

Smoking vs stroke

Both variables are binary/categorical, so the appropriate test is the chi-square test of independence on the resulting 2×2 table (or Fisher's exact test if any expected cell count is <5). The test alone only establishes whether an association exists; report an odds ratio with its 95% CI to quantify the strength and direction of the association.

Extension — why not a t-test for smoking vs stroke?

A t-test compares means of a continuous variable between two groups; here both smoking and stroke are binary, so there is no continuous outcome to average — the data structure (two categorical variables cross-tabulated) calls for chi-square/Fisher's, not a t-test. Matching the test to the type of both variables, not just the number of groups, is a frequent source of lost marks in exams.

Q3 Survival 20 marks
In a cohort study of benzene-exposed workers, you model time to leukaemia diagnosis. (a) Why use survival analysis rather than a t-test? (b) Name the model and interpret an HR of 2.3 (95% CI 1.4–3.8).
Marks guide: 20 marks: (a) rationale 6 · (b) model naming 4 · interpretation 6 · assumption check 4
(a) Why survival analysis, not a t-test

The outcome is time to a specific event (leukaemia diagnosis) and follow-up inevitably produces censoring — workers still healthy at the end of the study, lost to follow-up, or who die of unrelated causes before diagnosis. A t-test would require a complete, event-experienced time value for every worker and would have to either exclude censored workers (wasting information and likely introducing bias, since those with longer follow-up are systematically different) or wrongly treat "still healthy at study end" as if it were the true event time. Survival methods correctly use the censored workers' partial information — known to be event-free up to their last observed time.

(b) Model

Cox proportional hazards regression — appropriate here because it (i) handles censoring, (ii) allows simultaneous adjustment for multiple predictors (e.g., cumulative benzene exposure dose, age, smoking, other occupational exposures), and (iii) does not require specifying the shape of the baseline hazard, only that hazards are proportional across exposure groups over time.

Interpretation

HR = 2.3 means that at any point during follow-up, benzene-exposed workers have 2.3 times the instantaneous hazard of a leukaemia diagnosis compared with unexposed workers, after adjustment for the model's covariates. The 95% CI (1.4 to 3.8) excludes 1, so the association is statistically significant at α = 0.05; the point estimate and both CI bounds are compatible with a genuinely elevated, occupationally important hazard — a classic exposure-response finding consistent with benzene as a recognised leukaemogen.

What else should be checked / reported

Before accepting the HR at face value, check the proportional-hazards assumption (e.g., via Schoenfeld residuals) — if the excess hazard from benzene grows or shrinks over calendar time (e.g., due to changing workplace exposure limits), a single summary HR could be misleading and a time-varying-coefficient model may be needed. Also confirm adequate follow-up length and completeness of exposure ascertainment, since misclassified exposure biases the HR toward or away from the null.

Extension — quick sanity check on the CI

A 95% CI of 1.4–3.8 around a point estimate of 2.3 is reasonably wide (ratio of upper to lower bound ≈ 2.7), suggesting only moderate precision — likely reflecting a modest number of leukaemia events despite a possibly large cohort, since event count (not cohort size) drives precision in survival analysis. A natural follow-up question is how many additional years of follow-up, or how much larger a cohort, would be needed to narrow this interval.
