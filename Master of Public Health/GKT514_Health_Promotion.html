📚 How this topic fits into public health practice

Health promotion sits at the "upstream" end of the public health spectrum — before disease has occurred, and often before an individual risk factor has even emerged. It complements, but is distinct from, clinical prevention and disease control. A useful way to hold the whole field in your head is Tannahill's model: health promotion = health education + prevention + health protection, with the greatest impact where all three overlap (e.g. tobacco control combines education about harm, medical prevention/cessation support, and legal protection via smoke-free law and taxation).

Throughout these notes, worked numerical examples (incidence, risk reduction, screening test performance, price elasticity, sample size, hazard ratios) are included where they clarify why a strategy works, not just what it is — this mirrors how exam questions increasingly ask you to interpret, not just recall.

1. Concepts & Principles of Health Promotion
Sources: Ottawa Charter (WHO, 1986); Bangkok Charter (WHO, 2005); Nairobi Call to Action (2009); Shanghai Declaration (2016); Tannahill's model; Beattie's typology; Porta, Dictionary of Epidemiology
📣 Definition

Health promotion = "the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health" (Ottawa Charter, WHO 1986). It goes beyond health education to address the determinants and the environment.

Two things make this definition doctrinally important. First, the verb is enabling, not persuading or instructing — health promotion is fundamentally about building capacity and agency, not just transmitting facts. Second, the object is control over health, not simply "health status" — it links directly to the concept of empowerment (individual empowerment = ability to make decisions and exert control over one's own life; community empowerment = collective action by groups to gain control over the determinants of health and quality of life in their community, WHO Health Promotion Glossary, Nutbeam).

DimensionHealth EducationHealth PromotionDisease Prevention
FocusKnowledge, attitudes, individual skillsDeterminants, environment, policy, empowermentAverting a specific disease/exposure
Level of actionIndividual / groupIndividual → community → societal/structuralIndividual or population at risk
Typical toolLecture, counselling, pamphletPolicy, settings change, social marketing, educationVaccination, screening, chemoprophylaxis
RelationshipOne component of health promotionUmbrella term (education + protection + prevention)Overlaps with, but is narrower than, promotion
Distinct from disease prevention: promotion builds capacity & empowers; prevention averts a specific disease. In practice they are complementary, not competing — most real programmes blend both (e.g. a diabetes programme educates on diet and screens for pre-diabetes).
Deep dive: Beattie's typology & Tannahill's model ▼

Beattie's typology (1991) classifies health promotion interventions along two axes — mode of intervention (authoritative vs. negotiated) and focus (individual vs. collective) — producing four paradigms:

  • Health persuasion (authoritative + individual): expert-led advice to individuals, e.g. a doctor advising a patient to quit smoking.
  • Personal counselling (negotiated + individual): client-centred, e.g. motivational interviewing.
  • Legislative action (authoritative + collective): top-down structural change, e.g. seatbelt law, smoke-free legislation.
  • Community development (negotiated + collective): bottom-up, participatory, e.g. a village committee designing its own dengue-control plan.

Tannahill's model (Downie, Tannahill & Tannahill, 1990) depicts health promotion as three overlapping circles — health education, prevention, and health protection — with seven overlap zones. The strongest, most sustainable interventions sit in the central zone where all three combine (e.g. tobacco control = education on harms + smoking-cessation services + legislation/tax).

Exam tip: if asked to "critically discuss" a single-strategy programme (e.g. a poster campaign alone), use Tannahill's model to argue it is incomplete — it only touches education, not protection or prevention services.
📜 Ottawa Charter — 5 Action Areas
  1. Build healthy public policy — put health on the agenda of policy-makers in all sectors, at all levels, directing them to be aware of the health consequences of their decisions (fiscal policy, taxation, legislation, organisational change).
  2. Create supportive environments — generate living and working conditions that are safe, stimulating, satisfying and enjoyable; recognises the inseparable link between people and their environment (socio-ecological approach to health).
  3. Strengthen community action — concrete and effective community action in setting priorities, making decisions, planning strategies; empowerment and ownership of communities over their own efforts and destinies.
  4. Develop personal skills — health education and life-skills that support personal and social development, enhancing options available for people to exercise more control over their own health and environments.
  5. Reorient health services — the health sector must move beyond its curative/clinical mandate towards health promotion, sharing responsibility with individuals, community groups, professionals, and government.
Prerequisites for health (Ottawa Charter): peace, shelter, education, food, income, a stable eco-system, sustainable resources, social justice & equity. These are essentially the social determinants of health (cf. Marmot, WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health, 2008) — health promotion cannot succeed if these foundations are absent.

🛡️ Levels of Prevention

Health promotion operates mainly at the primordial & primary levels; treatment/rehabilitative services operate mainly at secondary/tertiary — but the settings approach and health-service reorientation (Ottawa action area 5) mean health promotion principles increasingly reach into secondary/tertiary care too (e.g. self-management education after a stroke).
Worked example: quantifying the impact of a primary-prevention programme ▼

A factory employs 2,000 workers. Over one year of routine surveillance, 40 workers are newly diagnosed with hypertension.

Step 1 — Baseline incidence

Incidence = new cases / population at risk over the period = 40 / 2,000 = 0.02 = 2% per year (equivalently 20 per 1,000 person-years, assuming full-year follow-up — Gordis, Epidemiology).

Step 2 — After a canteen salt-reduction policy (primary prevention)

The following year, with the same workforce size, only 30 new cases occur: Incidence(after) = 30 / 2,000 = 1.5%.

Step 3 — Absolute and relative effect
  • Absolute risk reduction (ARR) = 2.0% − 1.5% = 0.5 percentage points (5 cases per 1,000 workers per year averted).
  • Relative risk reduction (RRR) = ARR / baseline risk = 0.5% / 2.0% = 25%.
  • Number needed to "treat" (i.e. number of workers who need to be exposed to the policy for one year to prevent one new hypertension case) = 1 / ARR = 1 / 0.005 = 200.
Interpreting the numbers: a 25% relative reduction sounds dramatic, but the absolute reduction (0.5%) tells you how much of the population's risk was actually removed — always report both in an evaluation, and be alert to authors who quote only the more impressive-looking relative figure (a classic epidemiological framing effect, Gordis; Rothman).
2. Theories & Models of Health Behaviour
Sources: Glanz, Rimer & Viswanath, Health Behavior; NCI, Theory at a Glance; Bandura (1986, 1997); Ajzen (1991); Prochaska & DiClemente (1983); Rogers, Diffusion of Innovations (2003)
🧩 Health Belief Model (HBM)

Behaviour depends on the balance between perceived threat and perceived benefit/cost:

  • Perceived susceptibility — "could I get this?"
  • Perceived severity — "would it be serious if I did?" (susceptibility × severity together form perceived threat).
  • Perceived benefits of action minus perceived barriers to action.
  • Cues to action — internal (symptoms) or external (a reminder SMS, a poster, a friend's diagnosis).
  • Self-efficacy — confidence in one's ability to successfully perform the behaviour (added by Rosenstock, Strecher & Becker, 1988, borrowing from Bandura).
Modifying factorExample
DemographicAge, sex, ethnicity
Socio-psychologicalPersonality, social class, peer/reference group pressure
StructuralKnowledge of, and prior contact with, the disease
Useful for screening & prevention uptake (its original use was explaining why people didn't take up free TB X-ray screening in the 1950s US); weaker on social/environmental, economic and habitual/addictive determinants of behaviour — it assumes a rational, deliberative decision-maker.
Worked example: why perceived barriers include the maths of screening itself ▼

Suppose a district runs a cervical pre-cancer screening test with sensitivity = 80% and specificity = 95%, in a population where the true prevalence of the pre-cancerous lesion is 2%. Consider 10,000 women screened.

Disease presentDisease absentTotal
Test positive160 (true positive)490 (false positive)650
Test negative40 (false negative)9,310 (true negative)9,350
Total2009,80010,000
Calculation
  • True positives = sensitivity × diseased = 0.80 × 200 = 160; false negatives = 200 − 160 = 40.
  • True negatives = specificity × non-diseased = 0.95 × 9,800 = 9,310; false positives = 9,800 − 9,310 = 490.
  • Positive predictive value (PPV) = TP / (TP+FP) = 160 / 650 ≈ 24.6%.
  • Negative predictive value (NPV) = TN / (TN+FN) = 9,310 / 9,350 ≈ 99.6%.
Even with a "good" 80%/95% test, roughly 3 in 4 women who test positive do not actually have the lesion (low PPV, driven by low prevalence — Bayes' theorem / Porta's Dictionary of Epidemiology). This is a real perceived-barrier: fear of a frightening but likely-false-positive result, and the anxiety/cost of follow-up biopsy, can suppress uptake even though the programme is technically sound. An HBM-informed fix is to pre-counsel women on what a positive result does and doesn't mean, lowering perceived severity of a false alarm and reducing the barrier.
🎯 Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB)

Intentions are the most proximal predictor of behaviour; intention is in turn shaped by three belief-based constructs:

  • Attitude toward the behaviour — the person's own evaluation of performing it (favourable/unfavourable).
  • Subjective norm — perceived social pressure from people who matter (family, peers, religious leaders) to perform or not perform the behaviour.
  • Perceived behavioural control (PBC) — perceived ease or difficulty of performing the behaviour; acts both as a direct predictor of behaviour (a proxy for actual control, when accurate) and as a mediator of intention.
Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA, Fishbein & Ajzen 1975)Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB, Ajzen 1991)
Predictors of intentionAttitude + subjective normAttitude + subjective norm + perceived behavioural control
Best suited toBehaviours fully under volitional controlBehaviours partly constrained by skills, resources, or opportunity
Worked scenario: predicting exclusive breastfeeding intention in a new mother. Attitude ("breastfeeding is best for my baby") and subjective norm ("my mother and husband expect me to breastfeed") may both be positive, yet low PBC ("I don't think I can manage it once I'm back at work with no lactation room") can still suppress intention and behaviour — this is exactly the gap HBM's "barriers" construct and TPB's PBC both try to capture, which is why workplace lactation rooms (a supportive-environment, Ottawa action area 2) directly raises PBC.
🔄 Stages of Change & Social Cognitive Theory
  • Transtheoretical Model / Stages of Change (Prochaska & DiClemente, 1983): precontemplation (not intending to change within 6 months) → contemplation (intending within 6 months, ambivalent) → preparation (intending within 30 days, some steps taken) → action (changed behaviour <6 months) → maintenance (sustained >6 months); relapse is a normal, expected part of the cycle, not a failure endpoint.
  • Two supporting constructs: decisional balance (weighing pros vs cons of changing — pros must exceed cons before someone moves past contemplation) and processes of change — 10 cognitive/experiential processes (e.g. consciousness raising, self-re-evaluation) used more in earlier stages, and behavioural processes (e.g. stimulus control, reinforcement management, helping relationships) used more in later stages.
  • Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura, 1986): behaviour, personal factors and environment interact bidirectionally — reciprocal determinism. Central construct is self-efficacy — belief in one's capability to execute the behaviours needed to produce a given outcome; built via mastery experience, vicarious experience (modelling), verbal persuasion, and interpreting one's own physiological/affective state.
  • Social-ecological model (McLeroy et al., 1988): behaviour is shaped at nested levels — intrapersonal (knowledge, attitudes, self-efficacy) → interpersonal (family, peers) → organisational (workplace/school rules) → community (norms, networks) → public policy (laws, taxes).
Ecological levelExample for smoking
IntrapersonalNicotine dependence, self-efficacy to quit
InterpersonalPeer smoking, family support for quitting
OrganisationalWorkplace smoke-free policy, quitline referral
CommunityLocal social norms, availability of cheap cigarettes
PolicyCigarette tax, advertising ban, plain packaging
Match the theory to the target and level — there is no single "best" theory for every problem; many real programmes explicitly blend constructs from several theories (a common exam trap is to force-fit one theory onto a multi-level problem when a socio-ecological answer scores better).
Deep dive: Diffusion of Innovations (Rogers) ▼

Explains how a new idea, product or practice (e.g. a new vaccine, a mobile health app, a clinical guideline) spreads through a population/organisation over time, following an S-shaped adoption curve segmented into five adopter categories:

Adopter categoryApprox. % of populationCharacter
Innovators2.5%Risk-tolerant, resourced, seek novelty
Early adopters13.5%Opinion leaders, respected locally
Early majority34%Deliberate, adopt just before the average
Late majority34%Skeptical, adopt after majority pressure
Laggards16%Traditional, last to adopt, resource-constrained

Five perceived attributes of the innovation itself predict adoption speed: relative advantage (is it better than what it replaces?), compatibility (fits existing values/needs), complexity (ease of understanding/use), trialability (can it be tried on a limited basis?) and observability (are the results visible to others?).

Practical use: to spread a new practice (e.g. HPV self-sampling kits), engage respected early adopters first as local champions, make the innovation trialable (a free pilot batch) and observable (share early success stories) rather than mass-launching to everyone at once.
3. Strategies, Settings & Social Marketing
Sources: WHO Healthy Settings guidance; Kotler & Zaltman (social marketing, 1971); WHO MPOWER (2008); WHO Health in All Policies (Helsinki, 2013)
🏘️ Healthy Settings Approach

Settings = "the places or social contexts in which people engage in daily activities, and in which environmental, organisational and personal factors interact to affect health and wellbeing" (WHO). Rather than targeting individuals one at a time, the settings approach changes the context so the healthy choice becomes the easy or default choice.

SettingFrameworkExample components
Health-Promoting SchoolsWHO's 6-component modelSchool health policy; physical environment; social environment; community links; personal health skills/curriculum; health services
Healthy WorkplacesWHO Healthy Workplace ModelPhysical work environment; psychosocial work environment (stress, bullying); personal health resources (screening, gym access); enterprise community involvement
Health-Promoting HospitalsWHO-Europe HPH networkPatient/staff health promotion embedded in routine clinical care, not just treatment
Age-Friendly CitiesWHO Age-Friendly Cities FrameworkOutdoor spaces, transport, housing, social participation, respect, communication, civic participation, community support
The settings approach is practical, sustainable and reaches whole populations passively (people don't need to opt in), which is exactly why it complements individually-targeted education — it lowers the "cost/barrier" side of the HBM/TPB equation for everyone in that setting at once.
📣 Social Marketing

Social marketing applies commercial marketing principles and techniques to influence a target audience to voluntarily accept, reject, modify or abandon a behaviour for the benefit of individuals, groups or society as a whole (Kotler & Zaltman, 1971; Andreasen). The classic 4 Ps are extended:

ElementIn social marketing
ProductThe behaviour/benefit offered — not a physical good, but sometimes bundled with one (e.g. a free bed-net alongside "sleep protected from malaria")
PriceCosts of adopting the behaviour: time, effort, money, social stigma, loss of pleasure
PlaceWhere and when the target audience can access/perform the behaviour
PromotionPersuasive communication tailored to the segmented audience
+ Public / Partnership / PolicyThe wider stakeholder and policy environment that enables or blocks the behaviour
Exchange theory underlies the whole model: ask what the individual gives up (price) and what they get in return (product/benefit) — an effective campaign makes that exchange feel worthwhile. Effective campaigns segment the audience (e.g. by stage of change, not just demographics) and use formative research (pre-testing messages with the actual target group) before launch.
Worked example: segmenting a "quit smoking" campaign by stage of change ▼

A national quit-smoking campaign has a limited budget. Rather than one generic message, it segments smokers using the Transtheoretical Model:

Precontemplators (~40% of smokers)

Message emphasises consciousness raising — factual harms, secondhand-smoke risk to children — not a "quit now" call to action, which they are not ready for and would tune out (poor fit = wasted "price" of attention).

Contemplators (~35%)

Message shifts to decisional balance — testimonials that tip the pros-of-quitting/cons-of-smoking balance, plus signposting to a free quitline (lowering price/barrier).

Preparation/Action (~25%)

Message offers concrete behavioural tools — nicotine replacement therapy subsidy, quit-buddy apps, relapse-prevention tips (stimulus control, reinforcement).

This is exchange theory in action: for each segment the "price" the campaign asks them to pay, and the "product" it offers in return, are calibrated to what that segment is actually ready for — a single undifferentiated message would over-price the exchange for two of the three groups.
🤝 Policy & Intersectoral Action

Sustained, population-wide impact needs intersectoral action — the health sector alone cannot control the determinants of health, so it must liaise with education, transport, urban planning, agriculture, and finance ministries (this is the essence of Health in All Policies, HiAP, formalised at the Helsinki conference, 2013).

  • Structural interventions (tax, law, built environment) often outperform individual-level education alone, because they change the default for an entire population without requiring individual motivation or knowledge.
  • Classic example: tobacco control combines price (tax), smoke-free laws, cessation support, and counter-marketing — summarised by WHO's MPOWER package: Monitor tobacco use; Protect people from smoke; Offer help to quit; Warn about the dangers (health warnings); Enforce bans on advertising/promotion/sponsorship; Raise taxes on tobacco.
Health promotion without policy change tends to widen inequities — the already-advantaged (higher income, more health literacy) adopt voluntary behaviour changes first, so education-only strategies can paradoxically increase the health gap unless paired with structural measures that reach everyone regardless of individual motivation (Marmot's "inverse care law"-adjacent logic; WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health).
Worked example: price elasticity and cigarette taxation ▼

Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures how much consumption changes for a given percentage change in price: PED = %Δ quantity demanded / %Δ price. WHO reports overall PED for cigarettes in low- and middle-income countries of roughly −0.4 (i.e. demand is inelastic — people keep smoking somewhat even as price rises — but not perfectly so).

Applying it

If a government raises cigarette excise tax such that retail price rises by 20%: %Δ quantity = PED × %Δ price = −0.4 × 20% = −8%. So overall consumption is expected to fall by about 8%.

Why this is an equity-relevant policy tool

Youth and lower-income smokers tend to have higher elasticity (often cited around −0.6 to −1.0), meaning the same 20% price rise produces a proportionally larger drop in consumption in exactly the groups a health promotion programme most wants to protect — this is why taxation is rated one of the most cost-effective tobacco-control measures (WHO "best buys").

Contrast this with a pure education campaign, whose effect size is much harder to quantify and typically smaller and slower — illustrating the earlier point that structural/fiscal policy tends to outperform individual education alone for population-level behaviour change.
Deep dive: Health Impact Assessment (HIA) as a HiAP tool ▼

A Health Impact Assessment is a structured method used to judge the potential (positive and negative) health effects of a policy, plan or project outside the health sector — e.g. a new highway, a mining licence, a free-trade agreement — before it is implemented. Typical steps: screening (is an HIA needed?) → scoping (which health determinants/populations?) → appraisal/assessment → recommendations → decision-making and monitoring.

HIA operationalises action area 1 of the Ottawa Charter ("build healthy public policy") by giving non-health sectors a concrete, evidence-based mechanism to consider health consequences before decisions are locked in.
4. Health Education & Health Literacy
Sources: WHO Health Promotion Glossary; Nutbeam (2000, health literacy levels); Michie, van Stralen & West (COM-B / Behaviour Change Wheel, 2011)
🎓 Health Education

Health education = "any combination of learning experiences designed to help individuals and communities improve their health, by increasing their knowledge or influencing their attitudes" — more specifically, a "communication activity that aims to improve health literacy and develop life skills which are conducive to individual and community health" (WHO). It is one component of health promotion, not a synonym for it.

  • Aims: inform, motivate, build skills, change social norms, and (ultimately) support informed decision-making.
  • Methods: lectures/talks, small-group education, mass media, digital/social media, peer education, one-to-one counselling.
MethodStrengthLimitation
Didactic lecture/talkEfficient for large numbers, low cost per headPassive, low retention, poor for skills or attitude change
Small-group / participatory (role-play, demonstration)Builds skills & self-efficacy, allows tailoring & questionsResource- and time-intensive, reaches fewer people
Mass media campaignWide reach, can shift social normsWeak on skill-building, effect often small & hard to attribute
Peer educationHigh credibility/trust, culturally attunedMessage consistency and quality-control harder
Digital / mHealth (apps, SMS reminders)Scalable, can be personalised, low marginal costDigital divide can exclude the most vulnerable
📖 Health Literacy

Health literacy = "the cognitive and social skills which determine the motivation and ability of individuals to gain access to, understand and use information in ways which promote and maintain good health" (WHO). Nutbeam (2000) distinguishes three progressively deeper levels:

LevelSkillExample outcome
FunctionalBasic reading/numeracy to understand health informationCan read a medicine label correctly
InteractiveMore advanced skills to extract meaning, communicate, apply new information as circumstances changeCan ask a nurse the right follow-up questions
CriticalAbility to critically analyse information and use it to exert greater control over life events/situationsCan evaluate conflicting online health claims and participate in community health advocacy
  • Low health literacy is associated with poorer medication adherence, more emergency visits and hospitalisation, worse chronic-disease control, and higher mortality in multiple studies across health systems.
  • Universal precautions approach: because low health literacy is often invisible and under-disclosed, assume all patients may struggle; use plain language, short sentences, and the teach-back method.
Health-literate organisations make the system easy to navigate (clear signage, simplified forms, interpreter services), rather than placing the entire burden of "getting smarter" on the patient — this mirrors the settings-approach logic in Section 3: change the environment, not just the individual.
Worked example: the teach-back method in practice ▼

Teach-back is a simple check for understanding, not a test of the patient: after explaining, the provider asks the patient to explain the information back in their own words.

Poor practice

Nurse: "Take one tablet twice a day with food. Do you understand?" Patient: "Yes." (Yes/no questions do not verify comprehension — patients often say "yes" out of politeness or embarrassment.)

Teach-back practice

Nurse: "I want to make sure I explained this clearly — can you tell me how you'll take this medicine at home?" Patient: "I'll take two tablets once a day..." Nurse (catching the error, without blame): "Let me go over that part again — it's actually one tablet, twice a day, with food."

Teach-back shifts the "burden of proof" of understanding from the patient onto the communication itself — directly operationalising Nutbeam's functional health-literacy level in a routine clinical encounter.
🛠️ Education Methods & Fidelity
  • Tailor content, language, examples and delivery channel to the audience's literacy level, language, culture and stage of change.
  • Prefer participatory methods (role-play, demonstration, discussion) over pure didactic lecturing wherever skill-building or attitude change (not just knowledge) is the goal — adult-learning theory (andragogy) holds that adults learn best through relevant, problem-centred, experiential methods.
  • Assess implementation quality using the same lens as any programme: reach (who was actually exposed), dose (how much of the intended content was delivered/received), and fidelity (was it delivered as designed).
Deep dive: COM-B and the Behaviour Change Wheel (Michie et al.) ▼

COM-B proposes that any behaviour (B) requires the interaction of three conditions: Capability (physical/psychological ability, incl. knowledge and skill), Opportunity (physical and social environment that enables the behaviour) and Motivation (reflective and automatic processes that direct behaviour). If any one is missing, the behaviour won't happen regardless of the others.

The Behaviour Change Wheel surrounds COM-B with nine intervention functions — education, persuasion, incentivisation, coercion, training, restriction, environmental restructuring, modelling, enablement — and seven policy categories (e.g. legislation, fiscal measures, regulation) that can deliver them, giving a systematic way to design an intervention once you've diagnosed which of Capability/Opportunity/Motivation is the actual bottleneck.

Diagnostic use: a hand-hygiene programme that only posts motivational posters (targeting Motivation) will fail if the real bottleneck is Opportunity (no functioning sinks/alcohol gel at the point of care) — a COM-B analysis prevents this mismatch, and is a strong way to structure an exam answer that asks you to "design an intervention".
5. Programme Planning & Evaluation
Sources: Green & Kreuter, Health Program Planning: An Educational and Ecological Approach (PRECEDE-PROCEED); Glasgow, Vogt & Boles (1999, RE-AIM); Kirkpatrick (1994); CDC Framework for Program Evaluation (1999); Rothman, Epidemiology (sample size & hazard ratios)
🗺️ PRECEDE-PROCEED

A planning model (Green & Kreuter) that deliberately works backwards from the desired quality-of-life/health outcome to the causes, and then forwards through implementation and evaluation — nine phases in total, linking diagnosis to implementation & evaluation.

PhaseNameKey question
1Social assessmentWhat is the community's quality-of-life concern, in their own terms?
2Epidemiological assessmentWhich health problems/behaviours/environmental factors underlie that concern? (Rank by prevalence & changeability)
3Educational & ecological assessmentWhat predisposing (knowledge, attitudes), enabling (skills, resources, accessibility) and reinforcing (social support, peer/family influence) factors drive the behaviour? (the "PEF" analysis)
4Administrative & policy assessmentWhat organisational resources, policies and regulations will help or hinder implementation?
5ImplementationDeliver the programme
6Process evaluationWas it delivered as planned? (fidelity, reach, dose)
7Impact evaluationDid predisposing/enabling/reinforcing factors and the target behaviour change in the short term?
8Outcome evaluationDid health status and quality of life change in the long term?
The mnemonic: PRECEDE = Predisposing, Reinforcing and Enabling Constructs in Educational/Ecological Diagnosis and Evaluation (phases 1–4, the diagnostic/planning half); PROCEED = Policy, Regulatory, and Organizational Constructs in Educational and Environmental Development (phase 5 implementation, phases 6–8 evaluation). Working backwards from quality-of-life to causes (rather than starting with "what intervention do we like") is the model's signature strength — it keeps the programme anchored to what the community actually values.
📊 RE-AIM

A framework (Glasgow, Vogt & Boles, 1999) for judging whether an efficacious intervention will actually translate into real-world, population-level impact:

DimensionQuestionLevel
ReachWho participated, as a proportion of the eligible target population — and were they representative?Individual
EffectivenessWhat was the effect on outcomes (and quality of life / any negative effects) under real-world conditions?Individual
AdoptionWhat proportion and representativeness of settings/agents (clinics, schools, staff) adopted the programme?Setting
ImplementationWas it delivered consistently, at what cost, and with what fidelity across those settings?Setting
MaintenanceAre effects sustained long-term at both the individual level (habit) and setting level (institutionalised)?Both
An efficacious programme with poor reach or maintenance has limited public-health value — the overall population impact is often approximated as Reach × Effectiveness (sometimes extended to Reach × Effectiveness × Adoption × Implementation for a full "public health impact" estimate).
Worked example: RE-AIM arithmetic for a workplace smoking-cessation programme ▼

A factory of 500 employees runs a voluntary stop-smoking programme. 300 employees are current smokers (the eligible population). 120 of them enrol; of those, 30 are smoke-free at 6 months. Among smokers who did not enrol, a background/secular quit rate of 5% is observed over the same period (people quit "on their own" even without the programme).

Reach

Reach = enrolled / eligible = 120 / 300 = 40%.

Effectiveness (within participants)

Quit rate (participants) = 30 / 120 = 25%.

Comparative effect size
  • Risk ratio = 25% / 5% = 5.0 (participants were five times as likely to quit as non-participants).
  • Risk difference (absolute effect attributable to the programme) = 25% − 5% = 20 percentage points.
  • Number needed to treat (NNT) = 1 / 0.20 = 5 — about 5 smokers need to go through the programme to produce one additional quitter beyond what would have happened anyway.
Population ("public health") impact

Impact ≈ Reach × (Effectiveness − secular rate) = 0.40 × 0.20 = 8% of all eligible smokers in the factory quit because of the programme — a modest slice of the total 300 smokers, even though the within-participant effect looked impressive (25% quit rate, RR = 5.0).

This is precisely why a headline "25% quit rate!" is an incomplete evaluation claim — without Reach, Adoption and Maintenance data, you cannot judge population-level value, and self-selection (motivated smokers volunteering) likely inflates the apparent effectiveness above what a mandatory, whole-workforce programme would achieve.
🔍 Types of Evaluation
  • Process evaluation: was the programme delivered as planned? (fidelity, dose, reach, participant satisfaction) — answers "did we do what we said we'd do?"
  • Impact evaluation: immediate/short-term changes in knowledge, attitudes, behaviour, or intermediate risk factors (e.g. blood pressure, quit rate at 6 months).
  • Outcome evaluation: long-term changes in health/social outcomes — morbidity, mortality, quality of life, equity — that usually require longer follow-up and larger samples to detect.
Kirkpatrick's four levels (reaction → learning → behaviour → results) map closely onto process→impact→outcome and are widely used for training/education evaluation specifically. The CDC's six-step Framework for Program Evaluation (engage stakeholders → describe the programme → focus the evaluation design → gather credible evidence → justify conclusions → ensure use and share lessons learned) is a commonly cited practical checklist for planning any evaluation.
Worked example: is my evaluation's sample size big enough? ▼

Suppose you plan to evaluate whether a new cessation programme raises the 6-month quit rate above the background rate of p₁ = 5% to a hoped-for p₂ = 15%, using a two-arm comparison (programme vs control), two-sided α = 0.05 (Zα/2 = 1.96) and 80% power (Zβ = 0.84).

Formula (comparing two proportions)

n per group = (Z_(α/2) + Z_β)² × [p1(1−p1) + p2(1−p2)] / (p1−p2)²

Substituting the numbers

(1.96+0.84)² = 2.80² = 7.84
p1(1−p1) + p2(1−p2) = 0.05×0.95 + 0.15×0.85 = 0.0475 + 0.1275 = 0.175
(p1−p2)² = (0.10)² = 0.01
n = 7.84 × 0.175 / 0.01 = 137.2 → round up to 138 per group, i.e. about 276 participants total.

This is why a pilot evaluation with only, say, 40 participants per arm is usually under-powered to detect this size of effect — a common flaw examiners look for when a question presents a "disappointing" or "inconclusive" small evaluation and asks you to critique it.
Worked example: interpreting a hazard ratio in a tertiary-prevention evaluation ▼

A 5-year outcome evaluation of a cardiac-rehabilitation programme (tertiary prevention) reports a hazard ratio (HR) for all-cause mortality of 0.75 (95% CI 0.62–0.90) comparing attendees vs non-attendees.

What the HR means

At any given instant during follow-up, attendees have 25% lower instantaneous risk (hazard) of death than non-attendees (1 − 0.75 = 0.25), assuming the hazards are proportional over time (an assumption that itself should be checked — Rothman). The 95% CI excludes 1.0, so the reduction is statistically significant at the conventional α = 0.05 threshold.

Turning it into an absolute, decision-relevant number

An HR alone does not tell you the absolute benefit — you also need the baseline risk. If 5-year mortality among non-attendees is 20%, a hazard ratio of 0.75 corresponds approximately to about 15–16% mortality among attendees (exact conversion requires the full survival curve, but this is a reasonable illustrative approximation for teaching purposes): ARR ≈ 20% − 15% = 5 percentage points, giving NNT ≈ 1/0.05 = 20 — about 20 patients need to attend cardiac rehab for 5 years to prevent one additional death.

Exam-relevant point: a relative measure (HR, RR) alone can make an intervention look uniformly impressive, but the absolute benefit (and therefore the NNT and cost-effectiveness) depends heavily on the baseline risk of the population being evaluated — always ask "compared to what baseline?" before judging an evaluation's real-world significance.

Q1 Ottawa Charter 15 marks
Outline the five action areas of the Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion and give a Malaysian (or generic public-health) example for each.
Marks guide: 15 marks: 5 areas named & briefly explained (1 each = 5) · one relevant example per area (2 each = 10)
Five action areas
  1. Build healthy public policy
  2. Create supportive environments
  3. Strengthen community action
  4. Develop personal skills
  5. Reorient health services
Worked examples, one per action area
1. Healthy public policy

A sugar-sweetened beverage tax and mandatory front-of-pack nutrition labelling, aimed at shifting the whole food environment rather than relying on individuals to read fine print.

2. Supportive environments

Smoke-free public spaces and workplaces, and urban planning that provides safe walking/cycling paths, making the healthy option the physically easier one.

3. Strengthen community action

A village or residents' committee (e.g. a community "Healthy Kampung/Community" initiative) that identifies and plans its own dengue-control or exercise activities, with government facilitating rather than dictating.

4. Develop personal skills

Life-skills-based health education in schools covering nutrition literacy, refusal skills against tobacco/vaping, and basic first aid.

5. Reorient health services

Primary-care clinics expanding promotive/preventive services — maternal & child health, non-communicable-disease screening, community health kiosks — rather than functioning only as curative, illness-treatment centres.

Common mistake to avoid

Do not simply restate the five headings without a concrete example for each — examiners specifically credit application, and a generic "education campaign" example repeated for every action area (rather than one genuinely matched to each) usually loses marks.

Q2 Theory 15 marks
Using the Health Belief Model, explain why an apparently effective screening programme may have low uptake, and how to improve it. Illustrate your answer with reference to how the test's own statistical performance can itself act as a perceived barrier.
Marks guide: 15 marks: HBM constructs applied to the scenario (6) · worked screening-statistics illustration (4) · improvement strategies (5)
Low uptake explained via HBM

Low perceived susceptibility ("it won't happen to me"), low perceived severity, high perceived barriers (cost, time, fear, pain, stigma of a positive result) and weak cues to action all suppress participation, even when the test itself performs well analytically.

Worked illustration — the test's own numbers can be a barrier
Scenario

Sensitivity 80%, specificity 95%, disease prevalence 2%. Screening 10,000 people gives 200 truly diseased and 9,800 truly disease-free. True positives = 0.80×200=160; false positives = 0.05×9,800=490. PPV = 160/(160+490) = 160/650 ≈ 24.6%.

Interpretation

About three-quarters of people who test positive do not actually have the condition. If this is well known or experienced in the community, fear of a frightening-but-probably-false positive result (and the cost/anxiety of confirmatory testing) becomes a rational, HBM-recognised barrier to attending screening at all — this is a subtler mechanism than "people are simply uninformed."

Improvement strategies

Raise perceived susceptibility/severity via targeted, locally-relevant risk messaging; reduce barriers by making screening free, accessible and convenient (mobile clinics, flexible hours); add cues to action (SMS reminders, community champions); build self-efficacy with clear step-by-step instructions; and — specific to the PPV issue above — pre-counsel participants honestly about what a positive result does and doesn't mean, so a false alarm is met with informed reassurance rather than panic or programme distrust.

Q3 Evaluation 20 marks
A workplace health programme reduced smoking in its participants. Using RE-AIM, explain why this alone is insufficient evidence of population impact, show a worked calculation of the programme's true population-level effect, and state what else you would assess.
Marks guide: 20 marks: RE-AIM critique (8) · worked calculation (6) · additional assessment needed (6)
RE-AIM critique

Efficacy observed only in enrolled participants says little about Reach (what fraction of all eligible workers actually enrolled — likely the more motivated ones, a self-selection/volunteer bias), Adoption (whether other worksites took up the programme), Implementation (fidelity and cost of delivery across sites), or Maintenance (whether quitting persisted at 12+ months, and whether the programme itself was sustained institutionally). A small, self-selected reach can still yield a trivial population impact even with an impressive within-participant effect size.

Worked calculation
Given

300 eligible smokers; 120 enrolled (Reach = 40%); 30 quit at 6 months among participants (Effectiveness = 25%); background/secular quit rate among non-participants = 5%.

Effect size

Risk ratio = 25%/5% = 5.0; risk difference = 20 percentage points; NNT = 1/0.20 = 5.

Population impact

Reach × (Effectiveness − secular rate) = 0.40 × 0.20 = 8% of all 300 eligible smokers quit because of the programme — a modest population effect despite the headline 25% participant quit rate.

What else to assess

Report Reach (% of all eligible employees enrolled, and whether they were representative by age/job grade/baseline dependence), Adoption across other worksites, Implementation fidelity and cost per quitter, and 12-month Maintenance of quitting at both individual and organisational level; formally compare quit rates in participants vs a comparable non-participant group (not just before/after in participants, to avoid conflating programme effect with secular trend or regression to the mean); and quantify the absolute risk reduction across the whole eligible workforce, not just among those who opted in.
