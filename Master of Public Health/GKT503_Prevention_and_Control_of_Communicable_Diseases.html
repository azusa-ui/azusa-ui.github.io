1. Epidemiological Concepts & Chain of Transmission
Source: MPH GKT503 lecture "Epidemiological Concepts & Principles of Prevention & Control of CD" — compiled directly from the lecture deck.
Historical perspectives ▼

Hippocrates (c. 400 BC) was first to use the terms "endemic" and "epidemic"; his theory of noxious vapours became the miasma theory of disease, which held that diseases such as cholera, chlamydia, or the Black Death were caused by "bad air" from rotting organic matter. Miasma theory was eventually replaced by the germ theory of disease after 1880, though it had already motivated sanitary reform (clean-up of human waste, well-ventilated buildings) in the mid-19th century.

During the Middle Ages (11th–14th century), Muslim physicians discovered the contagious nature of TB, STDs and plague, the spread of disease through water and soil, quarantine as a control method, risk factor analysis, and the idea of a syndrome. Ibn Sina (Avicenna, 11th century) discovered the contagious nature of infectious diseases and introduced quarantine; Ibn Khatima and Ibn al-Khatib (14th century) hypothesised that plagues were caused by "contagious entities/minute bodies", later popularised in Renaissance Europe by Girolamo Fracastoro.

Anton van Leeuwenhoek (1632–1723) was first to observe microorganisms (bacteria). John Snow (1813–1858, "Father of Epidemiology") suggested cholera was transmitted by the faecal-oral route and applied epidemiological investigation/prevention concepts to outbreak control. Louis Pasteur (1822–1895) discovered the principles of vaccination and pasteurisation. Robert Koch (1843–1910) proved the germ theory of disease via his work on anthrax, isolated the cholera bacterium, and provided the scientific basis known as Koch's postulates. Edward Jenner, Jonas Salk and Albert Sabin developed vaccines for smallpox and polio that led to eradication/near-eradication of these diseases. Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin, the world's first antibiotic.

Historical pandemics: Hippocrates documented the first influenza pandemic (412 BC). The Black Death (plague) killed 25 million in Europe over 5 years (1347–1352). Smallpox, measles and typhus caused pandemics among native inhabitants of the Americas after European contact in the 15th–16th centuries; smallpox alone killed an estimated 60 million Europeans in the 18th century, before the world became free of the disease in 1980 due to vaccination. The 1918 influenza pandemic (Spanish Flu) killed 25–50 million people (~2% of the 1.7 billion world population at the time). Currently, influenza kills up to 500,000 worldwide annually; HIV/AIDS and TB together kill nearly 2 million; and there was the COVID-19 pandemic.

Communicable diseases in Malaysia ▼

Most CDs in Malaysia have been on the decline since post-war independence, due to socio-economic development and advances in vaccination, giving way to diseases more related to development and modernisation (CVS disease, cancers, injuries). Malaysia was declared polio-free in 2000, until a recent case in 2019 after 27 years (the last outbreak was in 1977). Leprosy, malaria, and vaccine-preventable diseases (neonatal tetanus, whooping cough, diphtheria, measles) have declined to very low levels, except for recent small outbreaks. Significant decreases have occurred in food, water and vector-borne diseases such as typhoid, cholera, dysentery, hepatitis A, filariasis, and HIV/AIDS. However, dengue, TB, leptospirosis, food poisoning, viral hepatitis, HFMD and COVID-19 continue to pose threats.

Ministry of Health Disease Control Division incidence/mortality data, Malaysia 2022 (per 100,000 population): food poisoning 43.77 (mortality 0.01); dengue fever 202.09; dengue haemorrhagic fever 0.35 (mortality 0.17); malaria 8.60 (mortality 0.03); measles 0.64; pertussis 0.31; filariasis 0.82; hepatitis B 16.02 (mortality 0.33); hepatitis C 14.17 (mortality 0.29); tuberculosis 77.76 (mortality 7.88); leprosy 0.51; AIDS 3.71 (mortality 2.16); HIV 9.73 (mortality 0.74); gonorrhoea 5.57; syphilis 14.30; HFMD 458.10; leptospirosis 9.12 (mortality 0.13); COVID-19 6,937.50 (mortality 16.27).

Emerging and re-emerging diseases that have periodically threatened Malaysia: Nipah encephalitis (1998–1999); anthrax scare (2001); conjunctivitis C-24 variant and meningitis (2002); SARS (2003); avian influenza (late 2003); chikungunya (2006); HFMD outbreaks (Sarawak 1997, national 2018); Zika, MERS-CoV, COVID-19; leptospirosis (rising over the past 10–15 years); tuberculosis (persistent for almost 3 decades); and neglected tropical diseases including lymphatic filariasis and rabies.

Core definitions and classification ▼

Infectious disease: a clinically evident disease resulting from the presence of pathogenic or infectious agents, including viruses, bacteria, fungi, protozoa or parasites. Communicable disease: illnesses due to specific infectious agents or their toxic products, which arise through transmission of that agent from an infected person, animal, or inanimate reservoir to a susceptible host, either directly or indirectly, through an intermediate plant or animal host, vector, or inanimate environment.

Classification of CD: (i) Microbiologic — by type of agent (bacterial, viral, parasitic infections, etc.); (ii) Clinical — by clinical symptoms or systems affected (diarrhoeal disease, respiratory disease, CNS disease, sepsis, etc.); (iii) Epidemiological (public health) — by mode of transmission, reservoir, or preventive aspect (arthropod-borne, food-water borne, airborne infections, vaccine-preventable disease, etc.).

Epidemiological triad and agent properties ▼

The epidemiological triad consists of: Agent — a biological, chemical, or physical factor whose presence or absence is essential for a disease/condition to occur; Host — a human or other living organism that can be infected under natural conditions; Environment — external conditions (other than the agent) that favour disease transmission, including biological (food, water, air, vectors), physical (humidity, radiation, sunlight), or socio-cultural factors (crowding, sanitation, healthcare accessibility).

Factors influencing transmission: Host intrinsic factors — susceptibility, immune response, resistance, portal(s) of entry. Agent intrinsic factors — virulence, toxigenicity, infectivity, resistance, pathogenicity, antigenicity, dose. Extrinsic/environmental factors — prevalence of vectors, herd immunity, opportunity for exposure (crowding, humidity, sunlight, etc.).

Chain of infection: reservoirs, portals, transmission ▼

The chain of infection requires: an infectious agent → a source or reservoir → a portal of exit → a mode of transmission → a portal of entry → a susceptible host. Reservoirs may be human, other vertebrate animals (zoonosis), or the environment.

Human reservoirs include symptomatic/asymptomatic cases and carriers (infected persons without symptoms who harbour the agent). Carrier types: inapparent (remain disease-free but contagious, e.g. 95% of poliomyelitis, 50% of Hepatitis A); incubatory (contagious before clinical disease, e.g. measles, mumps, chickenpox); convalescent (recovered but still transmit, e.g. typhoid, diphtheria, Hep B); chronic (shed pathogen for months/years, e.g. typhoid — convalescent carriers shed for 3 weeks to 3 months, chronic carriers over one year). The classic example is "Typhoid Mary", a chronic S. typhi carrier who worked as a cook in different NYC households, causing 10 typhoid outbreaks (51 cases, 3 deaths).

Zoonotic reservoirs (pigs, horses, birds, bats, dogs, rodents) include Nipah encephalitis, avian flu, rabies, plague, brucellosis, anthrax, typhus, leptospirosis, Q fever, and hantavirus. Environmental reservoirs include soil, water, food and air (melioidosis, histoplasmosis, anthrax, tetanus, cholera); Legionella pneumophila is often traced to cooling towers/evaporative condensers, and healthcare-associated organisms (MRSA, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter) can persist in healthcare equipment.

Modes of transmission: Direct — vertical (mother to fetus/newborn, e.g. syphilis, HIV transplacental transmission), direct contact (skin-to-skin, sexual contact, animal bites, blood transfusion), droplet spread (short range, ~1–2m, e.g. influenza, measles, pneumonia, whooping cough, TB, COVID-19). Indirect — vector-borne (biological, e.g. mosquitoes transmitting malaria/dengue/filariasis; or mechanical, e.g. flies/cockroaches transmitting salmonellosis/shigellosis), vehicle-borne (fomites, food, water, body fluids — e.g. cholera, salmonellosis, hepatitis A via shellfish), and airborne (droplet nuclei <5 microns that remain suspended, e.g. TB, measles, influenza, SARS).

Natural history, incubation period & herd immunity ▼

Natural history of disease is the progression of disease in an individual over time absent intervention, passing through: stage of susceptibility → subclinical disease → clinical disease → recovery/disability/death (the "spectrum of disease").

Incubation period is the interval between contact/entry of the agent and onset of symptoms — the time needed for the microorganism population to multiply to a threshold sufficient to produce symptoms. It can be as short as days (cholera, influenza) or as long as years (HIV, Creutzfeldt-Jakob), depending on dose, portal of entry, and host immune response. Latent period is the interval from infection to becoming infectious; infectious period is the time during which the agent is shed and can infect others. For measles: incubation 14 days (7–18), but infectiousness begins ~4 days before rash onset and continues 2–4 days after. For HIV: incubation/clinical latency 10–12 years before AIDS, but infectiousness may be high soon after infection.

The "iceberg" concept illustrates that clinical disease is only the visible tip of total infections — most infections may be subclinical/asymptomatic (e.g. >90% of poliomyelitis infections show no clinical symptoms, vs. ~90% of measles and 66% of mumps infections that do, and almost 100% of rabies infections).

Herd immunity is the resistance of a group to a disease when a large proportion is immune, reducing contact between infected and susceptible persons; the entire population need not be immunised to prevent an epidemic (e.g. smallpox needs ~95% immunised, poliomyelitis ~70% — the more infectious the disease, the higher the required herd immunity level). Requirements for herd immunity: the agent must be restricted to a single host species; transmission must be relatively direct between members of that species; there must be no reservoir outside the human host (e.g. as with smallpox); and there must be random mixing of the population.

Levels of disease occurrence: Endemic — habitual presence of a disease in a given area; epidemic — occurrence of a group of illnesses in excess of normal expectancy (an "outbreak" is a limited/localised epidemic); pandemic — a widespread epidemic affecting several countries/continents. An epidemic may result from a recent increase in agent amount/virulence, introduction of the agent into a new setting, enhanced transmission exposing more susceptible persons, or changes in host susceptibility.

Principles of prevention and control: applying primary, secondary & tertiary prevention to the epidemiological triad ▼

Because communicable disease results from the interaction of agent, host and environment, prevention and control measures can be organised either by which point in the triad/chain of infection they target, or by the classic three levels of prevention (primary, secondary, tertiary) used across all of public health. The two framings map onto each other directly for CD, since interrupting transmission before infection occurs is primary prevention, detecting and treating infection early is secondary prevention, and managing established/complicated disease is tertiary prevention.

Primary prevention (before infection/exposure occurs — reduces incidence by acting on the susceptible population): this is where most agent-, host- and environment-directed measures sit. Host-directed: increasing host resistance and reducing susceptibility via immunobiologicals (passive/active immunisation), pre-exposure antibiotic/antimalarial chemoprophylaxis (e.g. antimalarials before entering an endemic area, Bactrim for PCP prophylaxis in HIV patients), and improvement of general host resistance (nutrition, personal hygiene, exercise). Agent-directed: inactivating the infectious agent before it reaches a host, using physical methods (heat/pasteurisation, cold, UV radiation) or chemical methods (chlorination of water supply/sewage, disinfection of materials). Environment-directed: vector control, measures against animal/environmental reservoirs (animal immunisation, herd testing/culling), environmental control of air/dust/dirt, general sanitation (food, water, sewage, waste disposal), and personal protective measures (hygiene, PPE, insect repellents) that reduce opportunity for exposure in the first place.

Secondary prevention (after infection has occurred but before, or at, clinical onset — reduces prevalence/severity by shortening the disease course and interrupting onward transmission): rapid case detection through active surveillance and screening; contact tracing to identify exposed/infected contacts before they become symptomatic or infectious to others; antibiotic/antiviral treatment initiated early in the clinical course; post-exposure chemoprophylaxis; and isolation or quarantine of detected cases/contacts, graded by intensity as segregation, personal surveillance, modified quarantine, or complete quarantine depending on the disease's incubation period, mode/vehicle of transmission, and degree of contact — all of which act on the "portal of exit → transmission → portal of entry" links of the chain of infection to prevent onward spread while a case is still early in its course. Standard precautions in healthcare settings (which protect both patients and staff from transmission during the window when a case is infectious but potentially still undiagnosed) also function as secondary prevention in this framework.

Tertiary prevention (after disease is clinically established — reduces complications, disability and mortality, and supports recovery): definitive treatment of diagnosed disease to cure infection and prevent progression to severe/complicated forms (e.g. completing a full antibiotic course rather than stopping at symptom resolution, to prevent relapse or resistance); management of complications once they occur (e.g. rehydration and organ support in severe dengue, ICU management of severe malaria, directly observed therapy and management of drug-resistant TB); and rehabilitation and long-term support for sequelae of infection (e.g. post-polio disability support, management of chronic Hepatitis B/C-related liver disease, TB-related lung damage) — recognising that some communicable diseases, despite being "acute" in name, can leave lasting disability requiring the same rehabilitation-focused tertiary prevention seen in chronic/non-communicable disease.

Mathematical modelling: SIR model and R₀ ▼

Mathematical, statistical and computational models help understand transmission dynamics, identify key epidemic determinants, guide data collection, interpret trends, forecast epidemic direction, and evaluate cost-effectiveness of interventions. The concept of R₀ (basic reproductive number) is the average number of secondary cases generated by a single infectious case in a completely susceptible population.

The Susceptible-Infectious-Recovered (SIR) model (Kermack-McKendrick) is a simple model of dynamic person-to-person transmission, constructed from sub-population sizes (susceptible S, infectious I, recovered/immune R) and rates of change between them (birth rate, incidence of infection, recovery rate), described by ordinary differential equations with parameters: a = per-capita birth rate, b = natural death rate, β = transmission rate (transmission probability × contact rate), σ = rate of recovery.

To control an epidemic, interventions must reduce transmission so that the effective R falls below 1, meaning a fraction (R₀ − 1)/R₀ of transmission must be eliminated (e.g. 50% for R₀ = 2; 75% for R₀ = 4; 90% for R₀ = 10). This can be achieved by reducing contacts (quarantine, social distancing), reducing susceptibility (vaccination, prophylaxis), or reducing infectiousness (treatment).

Core issue: Every topic in this module (Sections 2–9) is an application of this same triad/chain framework to a specific disease category. When studying each disease below, explicitly identify its reservoir, transmission route, and which link of the chain the recommended control measure targets.
2. Surveillance of Communicable Disease
Source: MPH GKT503 lecture "Communicable Disease Surveillance" — compiled directly from the lecture deck.
Definition and purpose of surveillance ▼

Definition: the ongoing, systematic collection, analysis and interpretation of health data, essential to the planning, implementation and evaluation of public health practice; closely integrated with the timely dissemination of these data to those who need to know, and the application of the data to prevention and control.

Purposes of surveillance: to estimate the magnitude of a health problem in the population at risk; to understand the natural history of a disease or injury; to detect outbreaks or epidemics; to document the distribution and spread of a health event; to generate hypotheses about aetiology; to evaluate control strategies; to monitor changes in infectious agents; to monitor isolation activities; to detect changes in health practice/assess quality of care; to assess the safety of drugs, devices, diagnostics or procedures; to identify research needs; and to facilitate planning.

Detection of outbreaks through surveillance enables rapid investigation, pathogen identification and response. Surveillance data can also garner government attention to a previously unrecognised or underappreciated illness, keeping civic/political leaders aware of health burdens and costs. It is used to establish baseline disease rates, identify new/emerging infections, and monitor the impact of public health interventions.

Types of surveillance ▼

Four principal types: active surveillance (health authorities proactively seek out cases), passive surveillance (routine reporting by providers), sentinel surveillance (selected reporting sites represent a wider population), and syndromic surveillance (monitoring symptom clusters/patterns rather than confirmed diagnoses, useful for early signal detection).

Malaysia's CD surveillance system architecture ▼

Malaysia's communicable disease surveillance system comprises five components: mandatory notifiable diseases surveillance (notification of listed diseases, systems such as TBIS, e-dengue, e-measles, e-notification, HIV surveillance); laboratory-based surveillance (via Microbiology, IMR/PHL); clinical-based surveillance (sentinel selected clinics, national syndromic surveillance in A&E/wards/clinics); community-based surveillance (community/media/international sources); and disease surveillance by other agencies — notably the Department of Veterinary Services (zoonotic diseases) and FOMEMA Sdn Bhd (health screening of all foreign workers, including TB, cancer, HIV, drug abuse, and pregnancy).

Data flow: notifications from public health centres/hospitals and private GP clinics/hospitals, plus laboratory and sentinel/community data, are reported to the District Health Office, then the State Health Department, and finally the national Division of Disease Control at the Ministry of Health — with laboratory isolate confirmation flowing in parallel via IMR/PHL.

For each mandatory notifiable disease surveillance system, evaluation should cover: the type of disease/system, a description, its usage, strengths and weaknesses, barriers to its operation, and how the current system could be improved.

3. Outbreak Investigation & Response
Source: MPH GKT503 lecture "Outbreak Investigation & Management" — compiled directly from the lecture deck.
Definitions ▼

Epidemic: occurrence of disease cases in excess of what is normally expected in a specific community, geographical area, or time period (excess = two or more linked cases to the same source, or a single case of a rare disease). Outbreak: synonymous with epidemic, sometimes preferred to avoid the sensationalism of "epidemic"; alternatively, a localised (as opposed to generalised) epidemic. Nosocomial outbreak: illnesses of common aetiology in patients of a medical care facility, acquired by exposure to the disease agent while confined in that facility. Cluster: an aggregation of cases in a place/time suspected to be greater than expected — may be a preliminary observation before an outbreak is confirmed. Pseudo-outbreak: a perceived increase in cases due to new/enhanced surveillance, different laboratory methods, or seasonal variation.

Why do outbreaks occur? agent and susceptible hosts present in sufficient number; a recent increase in the amount or virulence of the agent; recent introduction of the agent into a new setting; an enhanced mode of transmission exposing more susceptible people; or factors that increase host exposure/introduce new portals of entry. Who recognises outbreaks? practitioners (clinicians, infection control staff, lab workers), patients or their families, public health surveillance data (uncommon), or local media.

Goals and rationale for investigating ▼

Goals: (1) rapidly identify the source and reservoir of the outbreak; (2) implement interventions to control and eliminate the outbreak; (3) develop policies to prevent future outbreaks; (4) identify new agents (not in every situation). It is important to maintain an open mind — natural biologic toxins, heavy metals and chemical poisoning may mimic signs and symptoms of infectious agents.

Rationale to investigate: control of further spread (interrupting transmission, post-exposure prophylaxis); uncovering new infectious agents/diseases; identifying spread of a known infection to a new area; improving epidemiological understanding of transmission and natural history; informing public health regulations/policy; reassuring the public; and minimising economic and social disruption. Priority for investigation vs. control depends on whether the causative agent and source/mode of transmission are known or unknown (Goodman et al, 1990) — investigation priority is highest when either is unknown, while control priority is highest when the agent is known regardless of source certainty.

The 10 steps (CDC, 1992) and the 7Cs framework ▼

The steps differ from outbreak to outbreak, have no fixed order (can run in parallel), and are dynamic (case definition, line listings, descriptive epidemiology, hypotheses, and communication are continuously updated) — but control measures should be implemented as early as possible for known/common outbreaks.

CDC's 10 steps: (1) prepare for field work; (2) establish existence of an outbreak; (3) verify the diagnosis; (4) define and identify cases; (5) perform descriptive epidemiology; (6) develop hypotheses; (7) evaluate hypotheses; (8) refine hypotheses/additional studies; (9) implement control & prevention; (10) communicate findings.

These map onto a 7Cs conceptual approach (UC Berkeley School of Public Health): Case investigation (steps 2–5), Cause investigation (6–8), Control measures — do early! (9), Conduct analytic study (7–8), Conclusions/Inference (5–8), Continue surveillance (9), Communicate findings (10).

Preparation covers three areas: investigation (scientific knowledge — literature review, expert consultation, sample questionnaires; supplies — lab consultation, equipment); administration (personnel/funding, Rapid Response Team, travel arrangements); and consultation (clarifying your role — lead investigator or support, and who to contact on arrival).

Establishing the outbreak & verifying diagnosis ▼

Establish existence of an outbreak: determine whether it is a true outbreak, a cluster/sporadic/unrelated cases of the same disease, unrelated cases of a similar disease, or a seasonal variation/surveillance bias — by determining the expected number of cases before deciding whether the observed number exceeds it. Comparisons use surveillance records for notifiable diseases, hospital discharge data, registries, mortality statistics, data from other facilities/states, or community/social/mass media reports.

Verify the diagnosis: ensure proper diagnosis and rule out laboratory error, by reviewing clinical findings/lab results, summarising with frequency distributions, characterising the spectrum of disease, and developing a case definition. Visiting and talking with patients directly (source of exposure, what they think caused illness, knowledge of others similarly ill, common denominators) is valuable for generating hypotheses.

Case definition, case counting & line listing ▼

A case definition is a standard set of criteria (clinical criteria plus restrictions by time, place, person) for classifying an individual as having the condition of interest; it must be applied consistently and without bias, and must not contain the exposure/risk factor being tested. Classifications are confirmed (laboratory confirmed), probable (typical clinical features, no lab confirmation), and possible/suspected (fewer typical features) — e.g. for E. coli O157 at a restaurant: confirmed = culture-positive with the outbreak PFGE pattern and exposure; probable = bloody diarrhoea and exposure; possible = diarrhoea (≥3 loose stools/day) and exposure. Early in an investigation, a "loose" case definition is used to capture the full extent of the problem and generate hypotheses; the definition is later "tightened" as hypotheses sharpen (e.g. the geographic exposure criteria for SARS case definitions were expanded over Feb–May 2003 as more affected locations were identified).

Identifying and counting cases uses enhanced passive surveillance (e.g. a letter to facilities requesting reports), active surveillance (phoning/visiting facilities), and public media alerts. Where a restricted population may have many mild/asymptomatic cases (e.g. cruise ship, school), the entire population may be surveyed with questionnaires and lab specimens. Information collected per case includes identifying information (for re-contact, duplicate-checking, mapping), demographics, clinical findings (verifying case definition, charting time course), risk factor information, and reporter information. A line listing — one line per case, spreadsheet format, updated as the investigation develops — abstracts key variables from case report forms, questionnaires and data abstraction forms.

Descriptive epidemiology: time, place, person ▼

After data collection, the outbreak is characterised by time, place and person. Time: the epidemic curve is a histogram of case counts by date of onset, showing outbreak magnitude and time trend, where the investigation stands in the time course, probable future course, and the probable exposure period (useful for focusing questionnaire design). Types: common source (point, continuous, or intermittent exposure), propagated/progressive (person-to-person spread, with successive peaks roughly one incubation period apart), and mixed patterns. The probable period of exposure can be estimated by counting back one incubation period from the peak, or by counting back the minimum incubation period from the first case and the maximum incubation period from the last case.

Place: geographic extent of the problem; clusters or patterns (e.g. spot maps showing where cases live, work, or were exposed) can provide important aetiologic clues — e.g. mapping TB cases by residence area within a prison to identify a localised outbreak zone. Person: determine the population at risk by age, sex, ethnicity etc.; usually defined by host characteristics or a common exposure/event (e.g. E. coli O157 and a school lunch, salmonellosis and a festival); rates (numerator = number of cases, denominator = number of people at risk) are used to identify high-risk groups.

Developing and testing hypotheses ▼

Hypotheses should address the source of the agent, the mode of transmission (vector or vehicle), and the specific exposure that caused disease — generated by considering what is known about the disease (reservoir, transmission, common vehicles, known risk factors), talking to case-patients with open-ended questions, consulting local health department staff, and using descriptive epidemiology (e.g. the epidemic curve).

Testing hypotheses compares them against established facts (clinical, lab, environmental, epidemiological data) or uses analytic epidemiology (cohort or case-control studies) to quantify relationships and explore the role of chance. In a "closed" population (e.g. hospital, school), a retrospective cohort study may be used; in general, the case-control design is especially suited to outbreak investigation because multiple exposure hypotheses can be tested concurrently, it does not require enumerating the whole cohort, and it makes efficient use of time/resources — though recall bias is especially likely if the investigation occurs long after the outbreak.

Retrospective cohort example (church supper): of 75 attendees, 46 became ill (attack rate 61.3%). Comparing those who ate vanilla ice cream (54 people, 43 ill, AR 79.6%) against those who did not (18 people, 3 ill, AR 16.7%) gives a relative risk of 4.77, implicating the ice cream. Case-control example (college cafeteria GI outbreak): of those who ate macaroni salad, 12 were cases vs. 4 controls; of those who did not eat it, 6 were cases vs. 14 controls, giving an odds ratio of (12/6)/(4/14) = 7.0.

Key epidemiological measures used in outbreak analysis: Attack rate (AR) = number of cases ÷ total population at risk over the observation period, × 100. Secondary attack rate (SAR) = (cases in group − initial cases) ÷ (susceptible persons in group − initial cases) × 100 — used to assess infectivity of the agent and evaluate prophylaxis efficacy (initial cases = index case(s) + co-primaries). Case fatality rate (CFR) = number of deaths due to disease X ÷ number of cases of disease X — reflects fatal outcome as affected by treatment efficacy.

Implementing control measures & communicating findings ▼

Control measures should be implemented immediately — to interrupt transmission from the suspected reservoir (e.g. recalling contaminated food, culling pigs as in the Nipah outbreak) — aimed at the agent, source, or reservoir, and should not be delayed to await completion of the causal investigation; control continues in parallel with ongoing investigation, followed by follow-up studies to assess whether incidence has been reduced, potentially prompting policy changes.

The underlying logic: transmission mechanisms drive transmission dynamics; transmission dynamics have critical control points; five critical control points (contact rate, probability the source is infectious, transmission probability, duration of infectiousness, fraction susceptible) map to six control strategies — reduce contact rate; reduce the proportion of infectious sources; reduce infectiousness; reduce susceptibility; interrupt transmission; and increase herd immunity.

Communicating findings (which may also happen early, depending on the outbreak stage) includes preliminary assessments and recommendations (letter, memo, press release), oral communication within the facility/media/community as part of risk communication, and interim/final written reports covering the extent of the outbreak, investigation results, control measures implemented, and their effectiveness (with reports potentially considered for publication as a record of performance, for legal purposes, and as a knowledge base for future investigations).

The overall framework of outbreak response runs: notification (from regular surveillance, instant notification, press reports, directives from higher levels, or rumours) → rapid assessment → analysis → outbreak confirmation → operation room activation → parallel investigation and control activities → documentation → report writing. An outbreak investigation team requires combined skills in epidemiology, questionnaire design, interview techniques, biostatistics, data management, and microbiology (including laboratory access).

Constraints of outbreak management ▼

Real-world outbreak investigations rarely proceed as cleanly as the textbook steps suggest: field investigations are frequently not initiated with a clear hypothesis, and investigators often collect descriptive data before a hypothesis is developed, meaning data quality is often not ideal — decisions must often be made based on small numbers and second-hand data (collected from health providers or family members), under high-visibility pressure (from superiors, politicians, media and the public) that risks biasing interviewees or investigator judgement. Additional constraints include limited statistical power, legal and financial liability pressures, delays that reduce the availability of human/environmental samples for testing, the unexpected/"beyond normal" nature of some events, and the need for multi-agency collaboration with or without bureaucratic friction.

Core issue: Control measures should be implemented as soon as a plausible hypothesis is supported by preliminary evidence, not deferred until definitive laboratory confirmation — mirroring the field-epidemiology principle established by John Snow (Section 1). A dissertation critiquing an outbreak response should assess timeliness of action relative to the point at which sufficient evidence existed, not merely relative to final confirmation, and should distinguish attack rate, secondary attack rate, and case fatality rate precisely when reporting outbreak metrics.
4. Vaccine-Preventable Diseases: Diphtheria, Pertussis & Measles
Source: GKT503 student seminars on Diphtheria, Pertussis and Measles — compiled directly from the seminar decks.
Diphtheria ▼

Agent: Corynebacterium diphtheriae, a Gram-positive, slender, club-shaped bacillus; toxigenic strains produce an exotoxin with two subunits (A — inhibits cellular protein synthesis; B — binding). Humans are the only reservoir. Transmission is person-to-person via respiratory droplets or direct contact with nasopharyngeal secretions, or via fomites. Incubation period averages 2–5 days; period of communicability is usually under 2 weeks (rarely over 1 month) and can be reduced to under 4 days with effective antibiotic therapy, though chronic carriers may shed the organism for 6 months or more.

Clinical features: disease can occur in immunised, partially immunised, or unimmunised individuals, though severity is markedly lower in the fully immunised. Presentation may be an asymptomatic carrier state, respiratory disease (pharynx/nose/tonsils/larynx, with insidious onset, low-grade fever, sore throat, an exudate progressing to a pseudomembrane that can extend to cause "bull neck" swelling, and in children under 4 laryngeal diphtheria with stridor and airway obstruction), or cutaneous disease (shallow ulcers, usually mild, caused by either toxigenic or non-toxigenic strains). Major complications are myocarditis (arrhythmias, cardiac failure, sudden death) and neuritis (paralysis of soft palate, diaphragm, ocular muscles or limbs, usually with spontaneous resolution).

Diagnosis and treatment: the gold-standard test is culture isolation of C. diphtheriae from a nose/throat swab (transported in Stuart's/Amies medium), with PCR sent to the Institute for Medical Research (IMR) to confirm toxin production; treatment (antitoxin plus antibiotics — penicillin or erythromycin) must not be delayed while awaiting laboratory confirmation, since diphtheria antitoxin (equine-sourced) only neutralises circulating, unbound toxin and cannot reverse toxin already fixed to tissue.

Outbreak/contact management (Malaysia): a close contact is defined as someone in contact with a laboratory-confirmed case for 8 hours and in close proximity within the last 14 days (Surat Pekeliling KPK Bil 16/2016), including household members, regular visitors, kissing/sexual contacts, and those sharing a room at school/work/shelter. Asymptomatic close contacts require a throat swab, 10 days of prophylactic antibiotics, symptom monitoring for 7 days post-exposure, and assessment of vaccination status; a culture-positive asymptomatic contact is managed as a carrier (not a case), treated with 14 days of antibiotics, with repeat cultures taken twice, 24 hours apart.

Malaysian epidemiology: diphtheria incidence rose from 2 cases (2014) to 4 cases/1 death (2015) to 31 cases/5 deaths (2016), predominantly among children, attributed to waning vaccine-induced immunity (a booster every 10 years is not current MOH policy) and sporadic under-immunised subgroup pockets. A documented Johor Bahru outbreak involved an index case (a 2-year-old in a welfare home) who died of multi-organ failure; contact tracing (54 contacts) identified 7 culture-positive samples, 4 confirmed toxigenic by PCR/Elek's test, of whom 3 had incomplete vaccination histories — illustrating that outbreaks can persist even under generally good national coverage where local immunisation gaps exist.

Pertussis (whooping cough) ▼

Agent: Bordetella pertussis, a small, strictly aerobic Gram-negative coccobacillus (first identified 1900, Bordet & Gengou), producing four toxins (pertussis toxin, adenylate cyclase/haemolysin, dermonecrotic toxin, tracheal toxin) that cause localised tissue damage and systemic spread. Incubation period is 5–10 days (range 4–21); the infectious period runs from the start of the catarrhal stage up to 21 days after cough onset, though early antibiotics shorten infectivity. Humans are the only host/reservoir; disease is most severe in neonates and unimmunised infants (maternal antibodies are insufficient for protection), while adolescents/adults may have milder, common-cold-like presentations that facilitate silent transmission.

Clinical course has three stages: catarrhal, paroxysmal, and convalescent. Case definition (clinical): cough ≥2 weeks plus at least one of paroxysmal coughing, inspiratory "whoop", or post-tussive vomiting; (laboratory): isolation of B. pertussis, positive PCR, or positive paired sera. A confirmed case meets the clinical definition plus culture/PCR/serology (four-fold antibody rise) confirmation, or is epidemiologically linked to a confirmed case. An outbreak is defined as two or more epidemiologically linked cases within 42 days in one house/childcare centre/class.

Diagnosis: sputum culture is the gold standard but has low sensitivity; PCR is more sensitive and rapid, is not affected by antibiotic treatment, and should be used for all suspected cases, with nasopharyngeal swab specimens placed in Stuart's transport medium, stored/transported at 4°C, reaching the laboratory within 3 days. Suspected cases must be notified to the District Health Office within 7 days of diagnosis, with case investigation within 48 hours of notification.

Control measures: a close contact is defined as direct face-to-face contact with a symptomatic case, sharing a confined space for over 1 hour, or direct contact with respiratory/oral/nasal secretions. Inadequately immunised household contacts under 7 years should be excluded from school/childcare/public gatherings for 21 days after last exposure, or until the case/contact has completed at least 5 days of a 14-day antibiotic course.

Malaysian epidemiology and issues: a pertussis resurgence occurred in Malaysia (2018–2019), attributed to waning community immunity, transmission from asymptomatic individuals (rising cases among older children/adults/adolescents), vaccine refusal/delay, and a "window of vulnerability" in infants under 2 months who are too young for vaccination — this youngest, unvaccinated group bears the highest case-fatality burden. Maternal Tdap vaccination during pregnancy (best given 27–36 weeks gestation), already adopted in Malaysia's private healthcare sector, has been shown to reduce infant infection risk by over 90% and infant pertussis death by 95%, bridging the protection gap from birth to completion of the primary vaccination series.

Measles ▼

Measles is caused by measles virus (genus Morbillivirus), transmitted by respiratory droplets and airborne spread, with humans as the only reservoir. As established in Section 1 (Epidemiological Concepts), the incubation period is approximately 14 days (range 7–18), and infectiousness begins around 4 days before rash onset and continues 2–4 days afterward — meaning transmission occurs before the classic rash makes the case clinically obvious, complicating contact tracing. Measles is exceptionally transmissible (among the highest R₀ values of any human pathogen), which is why the herd immunity threshold required to interrupt transmission is very high (approximately 95%, as noted in Section 1) — coverage even slightly below this threshold, or clustered pockets of under-vaccination, can sustain outbreaks despite an apparently adequate national average.

Measles remains a notifiable disease under Malaysia's mandatory surveillance systems (e-measles, referenced in Section 2), and Malaysian notification data (Section 1) recorded an incidence of 0.64 per 100,000 population in 2022 — low relative to the pre-vaccination era, but not zero, reflecting the ongoing challenge of sustaining sufficiently high and evenly distributed vaccination coverage discussed throughout this section.

Core issue: The diphtheria and pertussis case studies illustrate the same structural vulnerability from two different angles: diphtheria shows that a well-vaccinated country can still sustain outbreaks through localised immunisation gaps and waning adult immunity (no routine adult booster policy), while pertussis shows that even full population-level coverage cannot protect the youngest infants who are too young to be vaccinated, making maternal/cocooning strategies (Tdap in pregnancy) structurally necessary rather than optional. A dissertation on any vaccine-preventable disease in Malaysia should distinguish these two distinct failure modes (coverage gaps vs. age-window gaps) rather than treating "low vaccination coverage" as a single undifferentiated explanation.
5. Food & Water-Borne Diseases
Source: GKT503 Course Notes, "Food Water Borne Diseases" folder — "Typhoid" (lecturer/presenter attribution omitted), "Cholera", "Food Poisoning"; Case Definitions for Infectious Diseases in Malaysia, 3rd Edition (MOH, 2017); Garis Panduan Pengurusan Wabak Kolera di Malaysia, Jilid 3 (MOH, 2006); WHO fact sheets on typhoid, cholera and food safety.
🦠 Typhoid fever: epidemiology and agent

Typhoid (enteric) fever is caused by Salmonella Typhi, a gram-negative, non-spore-forming bacillus of the Enterobacteriaceae family that primarily infects the human gastrointestinal tract; humans are its only reservoir and natural host. Global estimates range from 9–21 million cases and roughly 110,000–161,000 deaths annually, concentrated in South Asia, Southeast Asia and sub-Saharan Africa where WASH (water, sanitation, hygiene) infrastructure is inadequate — the WHO South-East Asian, Eastern Mediterranean and African regions show the highest incidence (306, 187 and 111 per 100,000 respectively). Untreated mortality can reach 10%, falling to under 1% with appropriate antibiotics. In Malaysia, East Malaysia and the East Coast of Peninsular Malaysia are endemic areas; national incidence has shown a decreasing trend from 2000–2024 (around 1 per 100,000 nationally), though Kelantan-specific surveillance shows a historically Malay-predominant (98.1%), Kota Bharu-concentrated (71.8%) case distribution with median age around 16 years and school-age/adolescent groups (5–19 years) most susceptible.

Typhoid: case definitions, classification, and carrier states ▼

Clinical case definition: insidious onset of prolonged fever, constitutional symptoms (malaise, headache, anorexia), non-productive cough early in illness, constipation more often than diarrhoea, hepatosplenomegaly, and rose spots in fair-skinned patients. Case classification: suspected (meets clinical definition) → probable (positive serology/antigen test without organism isolation) → confirmed (isolation of S. Typhi/paratyphi from blood, stool or other specimen). Relapse is recurrence of symptoms with a positive culture within 3 months of treatment (having previously cleared); re-infection is recurrence beyond 3 months. A carrier harbours the organism without symptoms and can transmit via faeces; a chronic carrier is a confirmed case whose stool remains culture-positive beyond 3 months post-treatment completion despite being asymptomatic — the epidemiologically critical group for outbreak source-tracing, especially among food handlers. Incubation is 3 days to 1 month (average 8–14 days); the infectious period runs from about 1 week post-infection for as long as the organism is excreted in faeces. An outbreak is 2 or more epidemiologically linked cases within a similar incubation-period timeframe in a shared locality, declared over after 42 days (two incubation periods) without a new case.

Typhoid: diagnosis and treatment ▼

Blood culture (recommended volume: adults 5–10ml, children 0.5–3.0ml) can be taken regardless of illness stage, with ~90% positivity in week 1 falling to ~50% by week 3; stool culture/high rectal swab has lower yield (60–70%) and may be negative in week 1 but positive by week 3 in untreated patients; bone-marrow culture is the gold standard (85–90% positive even after antibiotics have started) but rarely used given its invasiveness; urine culture is not recommended (low, inconsistent yield). Antibiotic sensitivity testing (AST) and PFGE support resistance monitoring and outbreak-linkage analysis respectively; PCR is preferred for detecting carriers (including food handlers) given low/intermittent bacterial shedding in stool; the Widal serology test is no longer used in Malaysian government facilities due to poor specificity. Treatment is supportive plus antibiotics (ampicillin/ciprofloxacin/ceftriaxone for uncomplicated cases; ceftriaxone/ciprofloxacin for moderate-severe disease; carrier-state treatment uses ciprofloxacin 500mg BD for 4 weeks in adults, or paediatric oral amoxicillin dosing over 3 months), guided by AST results where drug resistance is suspected — a genuine concern given rising resistance to ciprofloxacin, azithromycin and third-generation cephalosporins, including extensively drug-resistant (XDR) lineages such as genotype 4.3.1 (H58) reported in South Asia. Discharge requires completed treatment plus 48 hours symptom-free, with the district health office (DHO) informed for stool-clearance follow-up (3 consecutive negative stool cultures for hospital discharge; follow-up stool cultures at 1, 2, 3, 6 and 12 months post-discharge for cases, and biweekly-then-monthly protocols for carriers, who must not handle food during this period).

Typhoid: surveillance, prevention and control ▼

Malaysia's eNotifikasi system (launched 2011, operating under the Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases Act 1988) mandates notification of suspected/probable/confirmed typhoid within 7 days of diagnosis, enabling real-time surveillance across DHOs, state health departments and MOH — though the seminar identifies real operational weaknesses: limited field/mobile accessibility, manual-entry error risk, private-sector underreporting, delayed lab-data integration, and rural connectivity/training gaps. Applying the epidemiological triad, control targets all three nodes: agent (disinfection with Lysol of contaminated surfaces, proper sewage disposal via SPAL projects, well-water chlorination, outbreak chemoprophylaxis); host (health education targeted at food handlers and the public, mandatory Food Hygiene Regulations 2009 training/certification and health screening for food handlers, active case-finding and stool-clearance follow-up for cases/carriers, and typhoid immunisation — Typhim Vi injectable for ages >2 with 3-yearly boosters, or Ty21a oral live-attenuated for ages >6 — targeted at food handlers, sanitation/sewerage workers, lab personnel handling S. Typhi, and close contacts of carriers, contraindicated in pregnancy, infancy under 2, and immunosuppression); and environment (food-premise inspection/closure under the CDC Act 1988/Food Act 1983, safe water supply with chlorine ≥2ppm at treatment plants/0.5ppm at endpoints, the BAKAS rural water/sanitary-toilet assistance programme, and structured sewage/drainage systems). The 2018 Segamat, Johor outbreak investigation illustrated this in practice: of 544 stool samples from contacts/food handlers/suppliers, 4 were Salmonella-positive; PFGE linked isolates from several cases as epidemiologically related; and Typhidot-C serology flagged a fried-chicken seller and a mother-daughter Nasi Kerabu stall pair at a pasar malam as probable carriers — food and environmental sampling additionally identified positive Salmonella in food and environmental specimens, demonstrating how laboratory triangulation (culture, PFGE, serology, food/environmental sampling) converges on a source in a real Malaysian outbreak.

💧 Cholera: epidemiology, agent and clinical features

Cholera is an acute diarrhoeal disease caused by Vibrio cholerae infecting the intestinal tract, with 1.3–4.0 million cases and 21,000–143,000 deaths estimated worldwide annually (likely underestimated, as WHO suggests only 5–10% of true cases are captured by reporting) — one of the oldest recognised diseases, with 7 pandemics recorded between 1817 and 1967, and a potentially notifiable event under WHO's International Health Regulations. Most infections are mild/asymptomatic and respond to oral rehydration; severe cases require IV fluids and antibiotics, with complications including acidosis, hypoglycaemia and acute renal failure. Case-fatality falls from over 50% untreated to under 1% with prompt treatment. Infective dose depends on individual susceptibility, notably gastric acidity (pH under 4.5 can kill the organism); incubation is a few hours to 5 days (mean 2–3 days), with communicability lasting as long as stool culture remains positive. A documented Sabah outbreak (Kudat) affected 18 villages and Kudat town, with cases predominantly Ubian-ethnicity fishermen residing near the coast; despite 70.5% of cases having a potable water supply, 52.6% lacked a sanitary toilet — illustrating that water supply alone is insufficient without adequate sanitation.

Cholera: outbreak management and public health response ▼

An outbreak is declared with one or more cases in a locality within a defined period, and declared over after no new cases for 2 incubation periods (10 days) from the last case's symptom onset. Case notification under the CDC Act 1988 requires immediate phone notification to the DHO followed by a written notification form within 24 hours, with investigation exploring travel and food-intake history from 5 days before symptom onset. Clinical management follows a severity-based pathway: oral rehydration solution for mild cases; IV fluids plus antibiotics (doxycycline/tetracycline in adults; erythromycin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole or tetracycline in children) for moderate/severe disease, with rectal swabs taken on 3 occasions 24 hours apart (the first sample no less than 24 hours after antibiotics are completed). Contact management includes symptom screening, hospitalisation of symptomatic contacts, rectal swabs for all contacts, and selective (not mass) chemoprophylaxis for close contacts/food handlers — mass chemoprophylaxis is explicitly discouraged due to antimicrobial resistance risk. Case detection combines passive surveillance (cholera alerts at clinics/hospitals, randomised rectal swabs among acute-gastroenteritis patients) with active surveillance (home visits and targeted case-finding in endemic localities).

Cholera: prevention, control strategy and global initiatives ▼

A multifaceted strategy combines effective surveillance (local feedback loops, global information-sharing, and early-diagnosis capacity, with WHO encouraging neighbouring countries of affected areas to strengthen preparedness), water sanitation and hygiene (the long-term solution — treated piped water, household-level filtration/disinfection/safe storage, safe waste disposal — though requiring sustained investment that is often difficult to fund), early diagnosis and treatment (reducing both rehydration-fluid volume needed and duration/quantity of V. cholerae shedding in stool, while cautioning against mass antibiotic use due to resistance), hygiene/social mobilisation campaigns (handwashing, safe food storage, safe faeces disposal, and — in some African contexts — promotion of breastfeeding as protective), and oral cholera vaccine (OCV) — 3 WHO-prequalified vaccines (Dukoral, Shanchol, Euvichol) given as 2 doses 7–14 days apart, offering roughly 65% protection for 2–5 years and contributing herd protection in endemic areas, always used as an adjunct rather than a stand-alone tool. The Global Task Force on Cholera Control (GTFCC, established 2014) coordinates global technical exchange and research, and in October 2017 launched "Ending Cholera: A Global Roadmap to 2030," targeting a 90% reduction in cholera deaths and elimination in up to 20 countries by 2030; standardised "cholera kits" support rapid deployment of outbreak-investigation and treatment materials.

🍲 Food poisoning: classification and burden

Foodborne disease (foodborne illness/food poisoning) is any illness resulting from consuming food or water contaminated with disease-producing agents — bacteria, parasites, viruses, fungi and their products, or non-microbial toxic substances — presenting mainly as gastrointestinal but occasionally neurological, gynaecological or immunological illness. Three mechanisms are distinguished: food infection (ingestion of living pathogens that invade host tissue — e.g., cholera, salmonellosis, typhoid, shigellosis, E. coli, campylobacteriosis, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, listeriosis, plus mycotic and viral causes such as hepatitis A, norovirus and poliovirus); toxin-mediated infection/toxico-infection (ingestion of large numbers of live pathogens that then produce toxin within the GI tract — e.g., Bacillus cereus, Clostridium perfringens, enterotoxigenic/enterohaemorrhagic E. coli, some Campylobacter jejuni, Vibrio cholerae); and food intoxication (ingestion of pre-formed toxins/chemicals already present in the food, from bacteria such as S. aureus/B. cereus/C. perfringens/C. botulinum, fungal aflatoxin, chemical contaminants, plant toxicants, or poisonous animals such as puffer fish, triggerfish and shellfish — characterised by short incubation of minutes to hours and typically no fever). Globally, over 250 recognised sources of foodborne disease exist; foodborne causes account for roughly 70% of diarrhoeal disease; over 2 million annual deaths occur in developing countries; and in the US alone an estimated 1 in 6 people (48 million) fall ill, 128,000 are hospitalised, and 3,000 die from foodborne disease yearly.

Food poisoning: risk factors, WASH-attributable mortality, and Malaysian surveillance ▼

Risk/contributing factors span host susceptibility (malnutrition, micronutrient deficiency, genetic factors, immunity, hygiene knowledge/practice, low socioeconomic status, reduced access to quality food, climate change, WASH conditions) and agent characteristics (pathogenicity, infective dose, virulence). SDG indicator 3.9.2 — the mortality rate attributable to unsafe water, sanitation and lack of hygiene (unsafe WASH exposure) — is defined as deaths per 100,000 population from diarrhoea, acute respiratory infection, intestinal nematode infection, and protein-energy malnutrition attributable to WASH deficiencies (WHO custodian agency). In Malaysia, WASH-related mortality data for 2021 recorded 253 government-hospital deaths, of which 96.0% were coded as other gastroenteritis/colitis of infectious or unspecified origin (ICD-10 A09), 2.4% protein-energy malnutrition, 1.2% typhoid/paratyphoid, and 0.8% amoebiasis, with zero deaths from intestinal nematode infection — though this reflects aggregated hospital reporting rather than individual case-level analysis. Malaysian food-poisoning surveillance draws on public reports, media, clinical-case reports from healthcare providers, laboratory/notification/syndromic surveillance data, and food-service-facility inspection, with MOH sampling over 10,000 food items annually across food premises to verify compliance with food safety standards.

Food poisoning: outbreak investigation framework ▼

The five objectives of a foodborne outbreak investigation are to identify the aetiologic agent, persons at risk, the mode of transmission and vehicle, the source of contamination, and the contributing factors — pursued through three components: epidemiological investigation, environmental/food investigation, and laboratory investigation. The classic "5 Ws" framework structures this work: what (clinical picture — symptoms, signs, complications, laboratory findings, generating hypotheses); who (person — sociodemographic profile, attack rates, population at risk); where (place — exposure location/transmission source, mapped via spot/area/density maps); when (time — trend, pattern, and epidemic curve revealing incubation period and exposure window); and why (causes, risk factors, mode of transmission) — the first four constituting descriptive epidemiology (distribution) and the last constituting analytical epidemiology (determinants). Preliminary assessment requires verifying report validity and rapidly interviewing an initial 5–10 cases (to minimise recall bias), gathering demographic, clinical (onset date/duration/severity, healthcare visits, lab results), risk-factor (contact history, food-consumption history, exposure dates, the respondent's own theory of cause, awareness of similarly ill contacts, shared exposures) and reporter information.

6. Vector-Borne Diseases: Dengue Surveillance & Control
Sources: GKT503 course-notes seminar "Dengue Surveillance Malaysia"; HTA Report "Integrated Vector Management for Aedes Control" (MaHTAS, MOH, 2019); National Strategic Plan for Elimination of Malaria 2010–2020; Guidelines for Malaria Vector Control in Malaysia (MOH, 2022); Management Guidelines of Malaria in Malaysia (MOH, 2013).
Epidemiology and clinical features ▼

Globally, WHO had recorded over 7.6 million dengue cases as of mid-2024, including 3.4 million confirmed cases, over 16,000 severe cases, and over 3,000 deaths. In Malaysia, 2024 recorded 122,423 cases and 117 deaths (a 17% increase), with periodic outbreaks recurring every 4–5 years and Selangor consistently the most affected state.

Clinical spectrum: dengue fever presents with high fever, headache, rash, abdominal pain and joint pain; severe dengue involves plasma leakage, bleeding, and organ failure. Warning signs of severe dengue include abdominal pain/tenderness, persistent vomiting, clinical fluid accumulation, mucosal bleeding, lethargy/restlessness, liver enlargement over 2cm, and a laboratory finding of rising haematocrit with a concurrent rapid platelet decline. Diagnosis uses NS1 antigen and IgM/IgG serology; management is supportive with fluid management being critical in severe cases. Differentiating primary from secondary infection uses detection of a significant IgG rise by ELISA — clinically important because secondary infection with a different serotype carries higher risk of severe disease.

A live attenuated dengue vaccine (Qdenga) is available for individuals aged 4 years and above, given subcutaneously; in an 8-country trial across Latin America and the Asia-Pacific, it reduced symptomatic dengue fever cases by 80% and dengue-related hospitalisation by 90%.

Epidemiological triad for dengue ▼

Agent: dengue virus, genus Flavivirus (same genus as yellow fever, Zika and West Nile viruses), with 4 serotypes; infected symptomatic and asymptomatic humans serve as the main carrier/source for uninfected mosquitoes. Host: approximately 75% of human cases are asymptomatic; the infectious period is 4–5 days (up to 12 days) until first symptoms appear; a person can be infected multiple times, with lifelong immunity only to the serotype of initial infection, while secondary infection with a different serotype increases risk of severe dengue. Clinical course has three phases: febrile, critical, and recovery. Vector: female Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitoes, which bite for blood to develop eggs, become able to transmit for life after a 4–10 day viral incubation period, breed predominantly in man-made containers in urban habitats, and are day-time feeders peaking in early morning and before dusk (Ae. albopictus has additionally spread to North America and Europe via the international used-tyre trade and is well-adapted to cooler temperate climates). Environment: temperature, rainfall and humidity directly affect the Aedes life cycle — higher temperatures accelerate larval development, shorten time to adult emergence, increase biting rate, and reduce the time needed for viral replication within the mosquito, though excessively high temperatures can shorten mosquito lifespan; container cleanliness and water-holding habitats are the key environmental determinant of breeding.

Case definitions and outbreak terminology ▼

Clinical case definitions: Dengue Fever — acute high-grade fever for 2–5 days with two or more of headache, retro-orbital pain, myalgia, arthralgia, rash, or mild haemorrhagic manifestation; Dengue Haemorrhagic Fever (DHF) — dengue fever with haemorrhagic tendencies (positive tourniquet test, petechiae, bleeding, thrombocytopenia, evidence of plasma leakage, rising haematocrit); Dengue Shock Syndrome (DSS) — DHF plus circulatory failure. An alternative warning-sign-based classification distinguishes dengue without warning signs, dengue with warning signs, and severe dengue (severe plasma leakage/DSS, severe bleeding, or organ failure).

Case classification: suspected (clinically compatible) vs. confirmed (suspected case with a positive laboratory test); all suspected cases must be notified to the nearest district health office (PKD) within 24 hours of diagnosis, followed by a notification form. Outbreak terminology: a controlled outbreak ("Wabak Terkawal") is two or more cases in the same locality within 14 days; an uncontrolled outbreak ("Wabak Tak Terkawal") is a continued increase in cases after day 14 from the second case; a hotspot is a locality with an active outbreak persisting more than 30 days from when the second case was reported.

Surveillance systems and entomological indices ▼

Dengue surveillance serves multiple purposes: early detection and outbreak response, monitoring disease trends, guiding prevention/control programmes, evaluating intervention impact, detecting serotype shifts, informing national/global reporting, improving clinical management, and supporting public risk communication. Malaysia's system requires mandatory case reporting within 24 hours, supported by laboratory surveillance (the Dengue Virus Surveillance System, DVSS) and digital platforms (SPWD, iDengue, eDengueV2).

Entomological surveillance covers risk assessment/entomological investigation, virus detection in mosquitoes, effectiveness screening for space-spraying, Aedes insecticide-susceptibility monitoring, sentinel stations, and information management through the MyEntoPest system, using three standard indices: the Aedes Index (% of houses with Aedes larvae/pupae; threshold >1% signals high risk), the Breteau Index (positive containers per 100 houses inspected; more sensitive than the Aedes Index, threshold >5 indicates increased transmission risk), and the Container Index (% of water-holding containers positive for larvae/pupae, ideally under 10%).

Prevention, control strategy, and challenges ▼

Malaysia's MOH Strategic Plan (2022–2026) targets a 5% case reduction per year and case fatality rate below 0.2%, through enhanced surveillance, integrated vector control, and community education. Core control pillars: (1) early detection and management — point-of-care rapid testing, diagnosis and registration within 3 days of symptom onset, home-based monitoring cards for outpatients, referral systems, "Dengue Champions"/hospital Dengue Management Teams, and regularly updated Clinical Practice Guidelines; (2) vector control — source reduction (Pemusnahan Tempat Pembiakan, PTP), space spraying (ULV/thermal fogging), larviciding, release of Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes, outdoor residual spraying, all under an Integrated Vector Management (IVM) framework with five elements (integrated approach; evidence-based decision-making; advocacy/social mobilisation/legislation; cross-sectoral collaboration; capacity building); (3) community engagement — school/media campaigns, gotong-royong clean-ups, COMBI volunteers, dengue-free premises initiatives, and anti-dengue community kiosks ("Pondok Panas"); and (4) legal enforcement — periodic Operasi Gempur Aedes (OGA) under the Destruction of Disease-Bearing Insects Act 1975 and the Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases Act 1988.

Challenges identified include urbanisation creating more breeding sites, climate change effects on vector ecology, community non-compliance, and insecticide resistance, alongside surveillance-specific problems of inaccurate case classification in e-notification and poor sentinel sampling. A real district-level outbreak response example documented in the seminar (Taman Maju, Perak Tengah) showed entomological indices falling across two control rounds (Aedes/Breteau Index from 5.1 to 3.64, Container Index from 0.83 to 0.24) alongside active case detection, enforcement notices (compounds under Section 13(1) APSPP), and integrated gotong-royong activities — illustrating the practical translation of the IVM framework into a real district response cycle.

Dengue IVM: evidence on effectiveness and cost (Source: HTA Report "Integrated Vector Management for Aedes Control", MaHTAS, MOH, 2019) ▼

Effectiveness by intervention type: this MaHTAS Health Technology Assessment synthesised systematic reviews and RCTs evaluating IVM components against entomological indices (Breteau Index, BI; Container Index, CI; House Index, HI; Pupae-per-Person Index, PPI) as proxy outcomes, given the impracticality of powering trials on clinical dengue incidence alone. Chemical interventions (larviciding, space-spraying/fogging, insecticide-treated materials) showed inconsistent and generally short-lived reductions in entomological indices across the cited trials, with effect sizes often diminishing within weeks of application and mixed results attributed to insecticide resistance and incomplete coverage. Biological control (e.g., Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis, copepods, Wolbachia-based approaches) and environmental management (source reduction, container removal/covering) showed more sustained reductions in immature-stage indices in several of the reviewed studies, though heterogeneity in study design and outcome reporting across the included primary trials limited pooled certainty. Interventions that combined community mobilisation with environmental management (e.g., the cited school- and household-based participatory approaches) tended to show larger and more sustained index reductions than single-component chemical interventions alone, though the report also noted that most included studies were of moderate-to-low methodological quality (small sample sizes, short follow-up, risk of detection bias), meaning the evidence should be interpreted as suggestive of relative effectiveness rather than definitive.

Cost-effectiveness: the report compiled incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (cost per DALY averted) for various vector-control strategies from the included economic evaluations, generally finding community-based/environmental management approaches to be more cost-effective per DALY averted than routine chemical fogging programmes, though absolute ICER values varied substantially by country/setting and were not always directly transferable to the Malaysian context.

Malaysia-specific costing: Malaysia's national dengue vector-control programme was estimated to cost approximately US$73.5 million in 2010 (about 0.03% of GDP that year), of which human resources (staff time for fogging, source reduction enforcement, surveillance) accounted for 64.8% of total cost — indicating that labour-intensive activities, not insecticide/equipment procurement, are the dominant cost driver. A subsequent Malaysia-specific financial burden analysis covering 2016–2018 estimated national dengue vector-control expenditure at approximately MYR 772 million against approximately MYR 101 million in averted costs from cases prevented (a cost-saving ratio of roughly 13%), implying that, on these figures, current vector-control spending exceeds the direct costs it offsets and that the programme's justification rests substantially on morbidity/mortality averted rather than pure cost-recovery.

Social, organisational, ethical and legal dimensions (Malaysia-specific): the report discussed enforcement-based components of Malaysia's dengue control (compounds and prosecution for breeding-site offences under the Destruction of Disease-Bearing Insects Act 1975, Act 154) alongside the Prevention and Control of Infectious Disease Act 1988 (Act 342) and the Local Government Act 1976 (Act 171), which together provide the legal basis for inspection, enforcement and local-authority involvement in vector control; the report noted debate around the ethics and practical effectiveness of punitive enforcement (fines/compounds) versus purely voluntary community engagement, and organisational challenges in inter-agency coordination between health authorities and local government for premises inspection.

Note on evidence strength: as an HTA report synthesising heterogeneous primary studies (varying designs, settings, and outcome measures), these findings should be read as a rapid evidence synthesis rather than a formal meta-analysis with pooled effect estimates; a dissertation citing specific effect sizes from the primary studies referenced within this report (e.g., the named systematic reviews and RCTs) should ideally trace back to and verify those original sources rather than citing the HTA report's summary alone.

Malaria: elimination programme (Sources: National Strategic Plan for Elimination of Malaria 2010–2020; Guidelines for Malaria Vector Control in Malaysia, MOH, 2022, 1st Edition) ▼

Note on sources: this sub-section draws on two MOH course documents of different vintage. The National Strategic Plan for Elimination of Malaria 2010–2020 (October 2010) supplies the historical epidemiology and the original elimination targets (data current to 2009). The Guidelines for Malaria Vector Control in Malaysia (2022, 1st Edition) is the more recent document and reflects the programme's current (2018 onwards) operating system; where the two differ, the 2022 guideline is treated as authoritative for current practice, and the 2010 plan is used only for historical background.

Milestones of malaria vector control in Malaysia (per the 2022 guideline): an eradication phase (1960–1980) used indoor residual spraying (IRS) with DDT, starting in Sabah/Sarawak (1961) and Peninsular Malaysia (1967). A control phase (1980–2010) added insecticide-treated nets (ITN) as a second core intervention from 1995 (following research in Sabah, Kelantan and Pahang), introduced locality prioritisation by incidence rate (malarious ≥10/10,000; potentially malarious 1–10/10,000; malaria-free <1/10,000), and replaced DDT with synthetic pyrethroids for IRS in 1997. A pre-elimination phase (2011–2015) retained IRS/ITN as core interventions under a red (incidence >1/1,000)/yellow (<1/1,000)/green (receptive and vulnerable, no local infection) locality classification, with red localities receiving 8 spray/net cycles at 6-monthly intervals and yellow/green localities a minimum of 2 cycles; this phase also introduced now-discontinued "focal" and "special" spraying categories. The current elimination phase (2016–2020, and continuing in practice past its nominal end date) shifted from incidence-based stratification to a foci-based classification system (Guidelines for Prevention of Malaria Re-Introduction, 2016).

Current (foci-based) stratification system: under the 2022 guideline, localities are classified by foci type rather than raw incidence: Active Foci (locally acquired case(s) detected within the current calendar year); Residual Non-Active Foci (local transmission interrupted 1–3 years ago); and Cleared Foci (no local transmission for more than 3 years, further stratified into high, medium, or low receptivity-and-vulnerability (RV) index, where RV index reflects the risk of malaria re-establishment). Vector control (IRS or ITN/long-lasting insecticidal net, LLIN) is mandatory in all Active Foci, Residual Non-Active Foci, and Cleared Foci with high or medium RV index, for a minimum of 6 cycles (3 years) in Active/Residual Non-Active Foci or 2 cycles (1 year) in high/medium-RV Cleared Foci, at 6-monthly intervals (shortened to 3-monthly on surfaces with poor insecticide retention, e.g. concrete or metal-sheet walls). "Focal" and "special" spraying are explicitly discontinued in this phase. The insecticide currently used is K-Othrine WG250 (deltamethrin 25% w/w) for IRS at 20–25 mg a.i./m², and Icon 2.5CS (lambda-cyhalothrin 25 g/L) for ITN treatment at 10–15 mg a.i./m².

Operational quality benchmark ("TOCOSURE"): the guideline frames IRS quality around four criteria: Total (every house/structure in the locality sprayed), Complete (every sprayable surface within each house sprayed), Sufficient (adequate insecticide deposit achieved), and Regular (cycles maintained strictly at 6-monthly intervals). Performance indicators include IRS/ITN implementation coverage (target 100%), proportion of premises covered (target 90%), and proportion of population protected (target 90% for IRS, 95% for ITN/LLIN); wall bioassay is conducted in Active Foci two weeks after the first spray/net cycle to confirm bio-efficacy. ITN/LLIN fabric integrity is graded using a Proportionate Hole Index (weighting hole sizes from 1 for small holes to 578 for holes over 25 cm), with nets scoring 643 or above classified as torn and requiring replacement regardless of age; LLIN are also replaced after 3 years of field use regardless of condition.

Historical epidemiology (2006–2009 data, from the 2010 plan): national incidence had fallen below 1 per 1,000 by the mid-2000s, but Sabah alone exceeded this threshold throughout 2007–2009 (1.04–1.32 per 1,000) and, with Sarawak, accounted for the large majority of cases. Roughly 80% of cases occurred in men; high-risk occupational groups were agriculture/farming workers (18%) and forestry/logging workers (15%). By species, roughly half of cases were Plasmodium vivax, a third P. falciparum, and the remainder P. malariae (~9%) or mixed infection (~4%) — this 2010-era breakdown does not yet reflect P. knowlesi as a distinct major category, consistent with knowlesi malaria's more recent recognition as a dominant residual infection in Malaysia; this specific shift should be verified against post-2020 MOH surveillance data rather than either course document, since neither covers it directly.

Historical trend and elimination definition: Malaysia's malaria caseload fell from 243,470 in 1961 to 7,010 in 2009, meeting its Millennium Development Goal target (a 45% case reduction and 26% mortality reduction by 2009 versus 2000). WHO's elimination definition is reduction of local (indigenous) case incidence to zero in a given geographic area; a country may still have competent vectors and report imported cases via travel/migration. WHO's staged pathway runs control phase (slide positivity rate, SPR, above 5%) → pre-elimination phase (SPR under 5%) → elimination phase (incidence under 1 case per 1,000 population, working toward zero indigenous cases) → a 3-year period of zero local transmission before WHO certification is possible, with "re-orientation" transition programmes at the start of the elimination phase and after certification.

Epidemiology (2006–2008 data cited in the plan): national incidence was already below 1 per 1,000 (0.2–0.3), but Sabah alone exceeded this threshold throughout 2007–2009 (1.04–1.32 per 1,000) and, together with Sarawak, accounted for the large majority of national cases (over half from Sabah, about 25% from Sarawak). Roughly 80% of cases occurred in men; in Peninsular Malaysia over half of cases were in foreigners, versus about 40% in Sabah and 10% in Sarawak. High-risk occupational groups were agriculture/farming workers (18%) and forestry/logging workers (15%). By species, roughly half of cases were Plasmodium vivax, a third P. falciparum, and the remainder P. malariae (~9%) or mixed infection (~4%) — notably, the plan's 2010-era data do not yet reflect P. knowlesi as a distinct major category, consistent with knowlesi malaria's more recent recognition as a dominant residual infection in Malaysia.

Feasibility assessment and area stratification: WHO's Malaria Elimination Group framework assesses technical (incidence under 1/1,000), operational (health system capacity, vector control coverage, political stability, multi-sector collaboration), and financial feasibility before a country enters the elimination phase. Malaysia's SWOT analysis (2006–2008) identified strengths including a legal notification mandate (malaria notifiable within 7 days under the Control of Communicable Diseases Act 1988), near-universal free blood-film-for-malaria-parasite (BFMP) diagnostic access, and ~98% of cases investigated within 7 days; weaknesses included microscope shortages in parts of Sarawak, slide-reading error rates of 3–7%, incomplete case investigation forms, inconsistent P. falciparum treatment regimens, incomplete follow-up (especially migrant workers), absent malaria outbreak management guidelines, and unsatisfactory residual spray coverage in several states; threats included illegal migration reintroducing malaria to receptive areas, employer non-compliance with insecticide-treated net provision, treatment-seeking delay, and both drug and insecticide resistance. Localities were stratified as red (incidence ≥1/1,000), yellow (<1/1,000), or green (no local infection); of 656 sub-districts assessed, 115 (largely in Sabah and Sarawak) were red zones covering over 1.2 million at-risk population.

National objective: the plan's main objective was a Malaysia free of locally-acquired (indigenous) malaria by 2020, with Peninsular Malaysia targeted for elimination by 2015 and Sabah/Sarawak by 2020, via a 20% annual reduction in local cases (baseline 2009).

Seven strategies underpinned the plan: (1) Surveillance — improving the e-Notis/e-Vekpro databases (which the plan documented as having significant discrepancies between systems in 2009) and a foci registry tracking case prevalence, entomological surveillance and control activity per locality; (2) Integrated Vector Management (IVM) — requiring 100% coverage of insecticide-treated nets and indoor residual spraying in the elimination phase (versus 85%/80% in the preceding control programme), with locality-stratified spraying/net cycles (e.g., red localities: 100% residual spray coverage every 6 months for 8 cycles); (3) Early detection and treatment — combining passive case detection (BFMP testing of outpatient fever cases, with the required screening proportion varying by locality colour), active case detection (house-to-house canvassing, escalating to weekly or monthly ACD depending on trigger criteria), and mass blood surveys (e.g., of boarding-school students after semester breaks in red/yellow localities), with strict turnaround-time targets (8 hours for passive case detection in clinics, 24 hours for active case detection/mass blood survey) and quality-control targets (slide rejection rate under 0.5% for health-personnel-collected samples, under 5% for volunteer-collected samples); (4) Preparedness and response to outbreaks — a target of controlling any malaria outbreak within 6 weeks of declaration; (5) Communication and social mobilisation — health education tailored to high-risk groups (including translated materials for migrant workers) and COMBI (Communication for Behavioural Impact) community activities; (6) Capacity building — addressing substantial staffing vacancies, particularly in Sarawak (only 50% of PKA U17 community health assistant posts filled) and Sabah; and (7) Malaria research — operational research to resolve implementation issues as they arose.

Key performance indicators included: 20% annual reduction in local cases; zero case fatality rate; 100% insecticide-treated net and indoor residual spray coverage in malarious areas; 100% case investigation and 100% completed radical treatment; slide examination error rate under 1%; same-day BFMP slide examination in over 95% of cases; control of outbreaks within 6 weeks in 85% of instances; and a 10% increase in community-involvement activities in malarious areas.

Malaria: clinical diagnosis, classification and treatment (Source: Management Guidelines of Malaria in Malaysia, MOH, 2013) ▼

Historical epidemiology and the P. knowlesi shift: this course document, published in 2013, records the transition described qualitatively above with real Malaysian figures: national malaria cases fell from 12,705 (2000) to 4,725 (2012), with incidence declining from 0.55 to 0.16 per 1,000 population over the same period, and the case fatality rate stabilising around 0.3–0.5 per 100,000 population since 2006. Critically, by 2012, 38.4% of all reported cases were zoonotic P. knowlesi infection, the single largest species category, ahead of P. vivax (50.2% of the human-only total), P. falciparum (30.7%) and P. malariae (16.7%). This confirms, using an actual course source, the shift toward zoonotic knowlesi malaria flagged (but not yet evidenced) earlier in this section. P. knowlesi was first reported in Malaysia in 1965, but its diagnostic and clinical importance was only recognised more recently, driven partly by the fact that it is morphologically indistinguishable from P. malariae under microscopy; the guideline states that in Sabah and Sarawak, P. malariae is now considered rare, and that microscopy-reported "P. malariae" cases should be presumed P. knowlesi and confirmed by PCR at the National Public Health Laboratory.

Diagnosis: blood film for malaria parasite (BFMP), examining both thick (more sensitive, screening) and thin (species identification) films, remains the diagnostic gold standard, with a laboratory turnaround target of within 1 hour of the slide reaching a laboratory with a technician. Quantitative parasite density is calculated by counting asexual and gametocyte forms against a fixed number of white blood cells (conventionally 200, or 8,000 WBC/µl assumed), then reported as parasites per µl of blood. PCR (nested, 7 working days; real-time qPCR, 3 working days) is indicated specifically when clinical symptoms are present but BFMP is negative, in mortality cases, and whenever microscopy reports P. malariae (to rule in/out P. knowlesi). Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) based on histidine-rich protein 2 detect P. falciparum only; pLDH-based RDTs cover multiple species but no commercially available RDT is sufficiently sensitive for P. knowlesi, so a negative RDT does not exclude knowlesi infection.

Clinical classification: uncomplicated malaria is symptomatic infection without evidence of vital organ dysfunction. Severe malaria is defined by any of: impaired consciousness, prostration, failure to feed, convulsions, respiratory distress, circulatory collapse, jaundice with organ dysfunction, haemoglobinuria, abnormal bleeding, pulmonary oedema, hypoglycaemia (<3.0 mmol/l), metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate <18 mmol/l), severe anaemia (Hb <8 g/dl), or hyperparasitaemia — notably, the hyperparasitaemia threshold for P. knowlesi is markedly lower (>20,000/µl) than for other species (>100,000/µl), reflecting knowlesi's 24-hour erythrocytic cycle (versus 48 hours for falciparum/vivax/ovale and 72 hours for malariae) and correspondingly faster potential for severe disease progression. Case fatality among treated severe malaria is quoted at 10–20%, rising sharply if untreated.

Treatment: the guideline mandates Artemisinin Combination Therapy (ACT) as first-line treatment for all species, using either Riamet (artemether-lumefantrine, weight-banded 3-day, 6-dose regimen) or ASMQ (artesunate-mefloquine fixed-dose combination, 3-day once-daily regimen), with sulphadoxine-pyrimethamine explicitly proscribed for field/presumptive treatment given high resistance. A single dose of primaquine (0.75 mg/kg) is added on day 1 for confirmed P. falciparum to clear gametocytes (after G6PD screening where feasible), while radical cure with 14-day primaquine is required only for P. vivax (to eliminate hypnozoites); P. knowlesi and P. malariae do not form hypnozoites and so do not need radical-cure primaquine. Severe malaria of any species is treated as severe falciparum malaria: IV artesunate (2.4 mg/kg at 0, 12, 24 hours then daily) is first-line, with IV quinine plus doxycycline as the alternative, managed in an ICU/HDU with close monitoring for pulmonary oedema, hypoglycaemia and renal impairment. Malaria is a notifiable disease under the Communicable Diseases Control Act 1988 (7-day statutory limit), though the guideline instructs practitioners to notify within 24 hours to enable prompt investigation and control.

Chemoprophylaxis for travellers: two prophylaxis types are distinguished — suppressive (chloroquine, proguanil, mefloquine, doxycycline; must continue 4 weeks after leaving a risk area since they act only on the blood stage) and causal (atovaquone-proguanil, primaquine; can stop 7 days after departure since they also target the liver stage). Malaysia is itself listed as a low-relative-risk destination in the guideline's country table, with chloroquine resistance noted and atovaquone-proguanil, doxycycline or mefloquine recommended for travellers to at-risk rural areas of Sabah, Sarawak and Peninsular Malaysia.

Dengue and malaria prevention mapped to the epidemiological triad and primary/secondary/tertiary prevention ▼

Dengue — primary prevention (before infection occurs, acting on the vector/environment rather than the human host, since no widely-deployed pre-exposure prophylactic drug exists for dengue): source reduction (Pemusnahan Tempat Pembiakan, PTP) eliminating Aedes breeding sites; environmental management and community "gotong-royong" clean-ups; larviciding; release of Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes; and the Qdenga vaccine for those aged 4+ (a genuinely host-directed primary-prevention tool, though coverage is not yet universal). Secondary prevention (after transmission risk has risen locally, aimed at early case/outbreak detection and interrupting further spread): entomological surveillance using the Aedes/Breteau/Container Index to trigger action before human cases cluster; case notification within 24 hours and active case detection (ACD) once an outbreak is suspected; and space-spraying/fogging deployed reactively around confirmed case clusters (though, per the HTA evidence above, fogging is a comparatively weak secondary-prevention tool relative to environmental/community approaches). Tertiary prevention (managing established dengue infection to prevent progression to severe disease/death): supportive management guided by warning signs (fluid management, monitoring for plasma leakage), and case-fatality reduction targets under Malaysia's Strategic Plan (2022–2026), which is explicitly a tertiary-prevention metric distinct from the case-incidence-reduction target that instead reflects primary/secondary prevention performance.

Malaria — primary prevention: vector control via IRS/ITN-LLIN under the current foci-based stratification system (mandatory in Active/Residual Non-Active/high-medium-RV Cleared Foci), and chemoprophylaxis for travellers entering endemic areas (a rare example of drug-based primary prevention against a vector-borne disease, since it acts before infection occurs rather than after). Secondary prevention: passive case detection (BFMP testing of outpatient fever cases) and active case detection (house-to-house canvassing, mass blood surveys), both aimed at finding infection early — including subclinical/asymptomatic carriage — before onward transmission via mosquito bite can occur; PCR confirmation specifically for microscopy-reported "P. malariae" results, to correctly identify P. knowlesi early given its faster progression to severe disease. Tertiary prevention: ACT treatment of confirmed cases to achieve parasitological cure and prevent progression to severe malaria; radical cure with 14-day primaquine specifically for P. vivax to eliminate hepatic hypnozoites and prevent future relapse (a tertiary-prevention measure aimed at a specific, species-dependent relapse mechanism not shared by P. knowlesi/malariae); and IV artesunate/ICU management of already-severe malaria to prevent death. Malaria elimination is unusual among the primary/secondary/tertiary framework in that its ultimate goal (zero local transmission for 3 years, then WHO certification) is a population-level outcome achieved only through sustained, simultaneous primary and secondary prevention across every locality — a single locality's tertiary-prevention success (treating its cases well) does not, by itself, advance elimination if primary/secondary prevention (vector control, case detection) lapses elsewhere.

Core issue: Dengue control in Malaysia depends on translating entomological indices (Aedes/Breteau/Container Index) into triggered actions (source reduction, fogging, ACD) at the district level — the real outbreak example above shows this cycle in practice, though the HTA evidence above suggests routine chemical fogging is among the weakest-performing IVM components and that Malaysia's ~US$73.5 million programme cost is dominated by labour rather than insecticide, and current spending (MYR 772 million, 2016–2018) still exceeds the direct costs it offsets — a gap between programme design (fogging-heavy, enforcement-heavy) and the comparative-effectiveness evidence (favouring environmental/community-based approaches) worth interrogating in any dissertation evaluating Malaysia's dengue control strategy. Malaria elimination, by contrast, depends on locality-level stratification (red/yellow/green, later foci-based classification) driving differentiated, resource-intensive vector-control and case-detection intensities rather than a uniform national approach — and the source documents themselves identify persistent implementation gaps (data discrepancies between e-Notis and e-Vekpro, incomplete follow-up of migrant workers, understaffing in Sabah/Sarawak) that a dissertation evaluating Malaysia's progress toward elimination should explicitly assess, rather than assuming the strategy's existence equates to its achievement. The 2013 management guideline confirms, with real Malaysian case data, that the residual malaria burden has shifted toward zoonotic P. knowlesi — a shift that changes the clinical urgency (lower hyperparasitaemia threshold for severe disease, 24-hour erythrocytic cycle) as much as the vector-control logic, since knowlesi transmission from a macaque reservoir cannot be interrupted by human-to-human strategies such as mass screening and treatment.
7. Zoonotic Diseases: Brucellosis (plus Nipah/Leptospirosis from Section 1)
Source: GKT503 student seminar "Brucellosis" — compiled directly from the seminar deck; Nipah virus and leptospirosis content carried from the Epidemiological Concepts lecture (Section 1).
Brucellosis: agent, hosts and transmission ▼

Brucellosis (also known as Malta fever/Mediterranean fever) is a zoonotic bacterial disease in which the organism multiplies in the reproductive organs and mammary glands of infected animals, with animals most contagious during parturition or abortion. Major food-producing host species (cattle, sheep, goats, pigs) are the key reservoirs; other animals (bison, buffalo, camels, dogs, horses, reindeer, yaks) can be locally significant. The causative species differ by host: B. abortus (cattle and other bovidae/cervidae), B. canis (dogs), B. melitensis (goats, sheep, camels), B. suis (swine), B. ovis (sheep), B. neotomae (wood rat).

Human transmission occurs via: ingestion of unpasteurised milk/dairy products (food-borne, regulated under Regulations 82, 85, 87 of the Food Regulations 1985); direct contact with infected animals or their secretions through skin cuts/abrasions or conjunctival splashes (occupational exposure); or inhalation of contaminated aerosols, particularly in laboratory, abattoir and farm settings. Human-to-human transmission is extremely rare. Incubation is highly variable (5–60 days, commonly 1–2 months, occasionally up to 5 months).

Clinical features, case definition and diagnosis ▼

Brucellosis is a systemic bacterial disease with acute or insidious onset, characterised by continued/intermittent/irregular fever, headache, profuse sweating, weakness, generalised aching, and arthralgia, with both acute and chronic manifestations possible. Case definition: clinical (fever plus one or more of night sweats, fatigue, anorexia, myalgia, weight loss, headache, arthralgia, arthritis/spondylitis, meningitis, or focal organ involvement, with history of exposure to a probable source); probable (clinically compatible plus presumptive positive IgM/IgG titre plus epidemiological link to a confirmed case); confirmed (clinically compatible plus definitive lab evidence — culture isolation, a four-fold or greater rise in antibody titre between acute and convalescent sera taken ≥2 weeks apart, or a positive PCR).

Diagnosis: blood culture using BacT/Alert or BACTEC systems under BSL-2 facility with Level 3 practices, with all isolates sent to IMR for species identification and DNA fingerprinting; serology (3–5mL serum); PCR (for acute cases only, 5mL EDTA blood). Suspected food-source outbreaks require food sampling under Regulation 5 of the Food Act 1983/Food Regulations 1985 (minimum 250mL/250g, transported at 0–4.4°C, reaching the laboratory within 24 hours), and coordination with District Veterinary Offices for animal sampling.

Notification, treatment and outbreak management ▼

Brucellosis is not notifiable under the Prevention and Control of Infectious Diseases Act 1988, so notification (of probable and confirmed cases only, within 1 week of laboratory diagnosis) is made by administrative order rather than statutory requirement — a structural difference from most diseases covered elsewhere in this module. Treatment regimens combine antibiotics for prolonged courses (e.g., adult regime: IM streptomycin plus doxycycline, or doxycycline plus rifampicin, for 6 weeks); no vaccine is generally available for human brucellosis.

An outbreak is defined as more than one probable/confirmed case with an epidemiological link within 60 days; common-source outbreaks typically follow ingestion of unpasteurised dairy, while aerosol infections occur in laboratory/abattoir settings. Outbreak response requires identifying the common vehicle (recalling/destroying implicated food products if food-borne), notifying the National Crisis Preparedness and Response Centre (CPRC) by phone/SMS, registering the outbreak in the eWabak system within 24 hours, and producing a final report within 1 month of outbreak resolution. Occupational prevention includes PPE, immediate washing after exposure, antiseptic treatment and dressing of cuts, and — for high-risk laboratory exposures — post-exposure prophylaxis (doxycycline plus rifampicin for 3 weeks) with baseline serology and 6 months of active surveillance for febrile illness. Intersectoral collaboration with the Veterinary Department (6-monthly animal surveillance, vaccination, test-and-slaughter, cull and compensation) is structurally built into the control programme — a concrete Malaysian example of the "One Health" approach.

Nipah virus and leptospirosis (from Section 1 lecture material) ▼

As established in the Epidemiological Concepts lecture (Section 1), the 1998–99 Nipah virus outbreak remains Malaysia's most significant zoonotic emerging-disease event, illustrating an amplifying-host effect: fruit bats served as the ultimate wildlife reservoir, while pigs served as an intermediate amplifying host, enabling sufficient viral load and human contact opportunity for spillover into intensively farmed pig-rearing communities. Leptospirosis (an environmental/rodent-associated zoonosis) showed a rising notification trend in Malaysia over roughly the past decade (9.12 per 100,000, mortality 0.13 per 100,000, 2022 MOH data cited in Section 1), associated with recreational water exposure, flooding, and urban rodent populations.

Core issue: Brucellosis illustrates a distinctive administrative feature relevant to any zoonotic-disease dissertation: because it sits outside the statutory notifiable disease list (unlike TB, HIV or dengue), its surveillance depends entirely on an administrative order and voluntary/clinical reporting pathways, which likely produces greater under-ascertainment than statutorily notifiable zoonoses — a methodological caveat worth stating explicitly if brucellosis notification data are used as an outcome measure. More broadly, all three zoonoses discussed here (brucellosis, Nipah, leptospirosis) require an explicit animal- or environment-sector intervention component (One Health framing) because their reservoirs cannot be eliminated by human-directed measures alone.
8. HIV/AIDS & Sexually Transmitted Infections
Source: MPH GKT503 lecture "HIV/AIDS: Epidemiology, Prevention & Control" — compiled directly from the lecture deck.
Global HIV/AIDS estimates (UNAIDS 2023/2024) ▼

Global adult (15–49) HIV prevalence has levelled off since 2001. In 2023: 39.9 million [36.1–44.6 million] people were living with HIV globally; 1.3 million [1.0–1.7 million] were newly infected; 53% of people living with HIV were women and girls; 630,000 died of AIDS-related illnesses. AIDS-related mortality has declined by 57% among women/girls and 47% among men/boys since 2010. Of people living with HIV, 86% knew their status, of whom 77% were accessing antiretroviral therapy and 72% were virally suppressed. UNAIDS reports a 40% reduction in new HIV infections globally since 2010, though progress varies by region (~5% decline in eastern/southern Africa, more gradual declines in Asia-Pacific and western/central Africa), and a 51% reduction in HIV-associated deaths since 2010, attributed partly to expanded treatment access. The United Nations Political Declaration on HIV/AIDS targets fewer than 350,000 new infections globally by 2030.

Key population risk multiples (compared with general adult populations): men who have sex with men (MSM) 23 times higher risk than other men; sex workers 9 times higher risk than adult women; transgender people 20 times higher risk than adult women; people who inject drugs (PWID) 14 times higher than people who do not inject; prisoners and other incarcerated people 7 times higher. Median global adult HIV prevalence was 0.8%, but markedly higher among specific groups: 2.3% among young women/girls (15–24) in eastern/southern Africa, 7.7% among gay men and other MSM, 3% among sex workers, 5% among PWID, 9.2% among transgender people, and 1.3% among people in prisons.

Vulnerable populations requiring targeted programming beyond key populations/partners include young people (ages 15–24 account for roughly a third of new infections and face particular barriers to accessing HIV and sexual/reproductive health services), women and girls (HIV/AIDS is a leading cause of death among women of reproductive age, with gender inequality, differential service access, and sexual violence increasing vulnerability, and younger women being biologically more susceptible), migrants, ethnic/cultural minorities and other marginalised populations, and people living with HIV who also have TB.

HIV and TB co-infection ▼

TB is a leading cause of death among people living with HIV worldwide, and HIV has driven a resurgence of TB, particularly in Africa. People living with HIV are approximately 18 times more likely to develop active TB disease than people without HIV. In 2021, 7% of new TB cases globally occurred in people living with HIV; TB deaths among people living with HIV declined between 2000 and 2020, largely due to scale-up of joint HIV/TB services, though only around 50% of those eligible for TB preventive treatment and ART reach care.

Prevention strategies (UNAIDS combination prevention) ▼

Combination prevention approaches include: health education programmes and outreach; behaviour-change programmes on safe sexual practices, including correct and consistent use of male and female condoms; prevention and elimination of mother-to-child transmission (MTCT); harm reduction among people who inject drugs (clean needles/syringes via needle-syringe exchange programmes, opiate substitution therapy such as methadone maintenance therapy); voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC), which reduces female-to-male sexual transmission of HIV by an estimated 60% and has been recommended by WHO/UNAIDS since 2007 in high-prevalence countries; antiretroviral treatment to reduce viral load and prevent onward transmission; and ARV medicines as pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP).

Behavioural approaches are commonly summarised under the ABC approach (Abstinence or delay of sexual activity especially for youth; Being faithful, especially in committed relationships; Condom use for those engaging in risky behaviour) or the CNN approach, which emphasises condom use more heavily (Condom use; Needles — use clean ones; Negotiating skills, including empowering women to negotiate safer sex). Comprehensive sex education incorporating life skills (negotiating healthy sexual relationships) alongside accurate, explicit safer-sex information has been shown in the cited literature to be more effective at preventing STIs than abstinence-only education. Thailand's 100% condom use programme, which de-stigmatised condom promotion for sex workers and their clients, is cited as a landmark example, with a consistent inverse relationship observed between rising condom use and declining STI/AIDS case numbers from 1985–2003; similar success has been described in Uganda, Cambodia and Brazil.

Preventing mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) follows a four-tier framework, from most to least preferred: (1) prevention of HIV in women of reproductive age and (2) prevention of unintended pregnancy in HIV-positive women (both considered the "best" interventions); (3) prevention of MTCT through antiretroviral therapy during pregnancy, safer delivery practices, and infant feeding counselling ("next best"); and (4) care, treatment and support for HIV-infected parents, infants and families. WHO's Global Health Sector Strategy on HIV and STIs 2016–2021 set a target of zero new HIV infections among infants by 2020; Malaysia was among the first countries in the Asia-Pacific region (alongside Thailand, the Maldives and Sri Lanka) validated by WHO for elimination of mother-to-child transmission of HIV and syphilis by the end of 2020, having reduced HIV vertical transmission to below 2% by 2016.

Testing, ARV treatment and universal precautions ▼

Three types of HIV test exist, differing in window period: nucleic acid tests (NAT), antigen/antibody tests, and antibody tests (including most rapid tests and self-tests). A single rapid diagnostic test is not by itself sufficient for a positive diagnosis. HIV self-testing allows a person to collect their own specimen (oral fluid or blood), perform the test, and interpret the result (typically within 20 minutes) in a private setting.

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) improves general health and prolongs life and reduces transmission risk, but does not cure HIV infection; a key goal is reducing viral load to an undetectable level, which nearly eliminates sexual transmission risk. Highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART), introduced in 1996, combines at least three ARV drugs from different classes and may prolong survival by 4–12 years or longer with complete adherence, while preventing many opportunistic infections. Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is used for individuals exposed to HIV through occupational hazard or sexual assault; pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is used for individuals with regular ongoing exposure risk.

Universal precautions for healthcare workers include wearing latex gloves, not re-capping used needles, using masks with goggles in theatre, using long ("veterinary") gloves when handling delivery of HIV-infected mothers, and using disposable/reusable aprons, gowns and shoe covers as appropriate — standard infection-control measures that protect against occupational HIV transmission regardless of a patient's known status.

HIV/AIDS in Malaysia: epidemiology ▼

Malaysia's HIV notification rate has declined by more than half, from 28 per 100,000 population in 2002 to 9.7 per 100,000 in 2023, with new infections having been relatively stable since 2008. Malaysia is classified by UNAIDS as a middle-income country with a "concentrated" HIV/AIDS epidemic (general population prevalence below 1%, with much higher prevalence in specific sub-populations — historical estimates cited in the lecture include roughly 19% among people who inject drugs, 11.5% among sex workers, and 9.7% among transgender people, versus under 1% in antenatal, pre-marital and blood-donor screening used as proxies for general-population prevalence).

Cumulative reported HIV cases in Malaysia reached 135,035 by 2023, with 31,456 cumulative AIDS cases and 63,108 cumulative HIV/AIDS-related deaths; an estimated 85,283 people were living with HIV (Spectrum 2023 estimate), of whom 71,927 were captured in surveillance data and 48,765 were receiving ART as of December 2023. Reported new HIV infections in 2023 numbered 3,220. The mode of transmission has shifted markedly over time: sharing of injecting equipment accounted for the large majority of cases in the early 2000s, but heterosexual and, increasingly, male-same-sex transmission have become dominant, with the PWID-to-sexual-transmission ratio falling from roughly 4–5 in the early 2000s to near zero by the 2010s–2020s. Among men, roughly 23.7% of infections are attributed to injecting drug use versus 74.6% to sexual transmission; among women, the large majority (92.4%) are attributed to heterosexual transmission. The male-to-female case ratio has declined gradually, from 9.6 in 2000 to 8.8 in 2023, reflecting a rising (though still minority) share of infections among women.

By 2023, progress toward the UNAIDS 95-95-95 testing-and-treatment cascade in Malaysia stood at 84% of people living with HIV knowing their status, 68% of those aware of their status receiving ART, and 89% of those on ART being virally suppressed — with the largest remaining gap at the second "95" (linkage to and retention in treatment). Late HIV diagnosis (CD4 count below 350 at diagnosis) has remained persistently high among Malaysian adults, around 65–70% in recent years, indicating continued need for earlier case detection.

Malaysia's national strategy and screening programmes ▼

The National Strategic Plan for Ending AIDS (NSPEA) 2016–2030 adopts UNAIDS's vision of "3 Zeros" (zero new infections, zero discrimination, zero AIDS-related deaths), targeting 90-90-90 by 2020 and 95-95-95 by 2030, aligned with the Sustainable Development Goals. It places testing and treatment at the centre of the national response, supported by intensified prevention strategies targeting 80–95% coverage of key populations. Programme priorities include: intensifying testing and treatment; scaling up harm reduction (opiate substitution therapy and needle-syringe exchange programmes); reducing sexual transmission among key populations and their partners while working toward elimination of mother-to-child transmission; and reducing stigma and discrimination while providing social protection as a cross-cutting concern for all key populations.

Malaysia operates both routinely screened and mandatory HIV testing programmes. Routinely screened groups include antenatal mothers at government clinics, inmates in drug rehabilitation centres and prisons (at admission, 6 months, and pre-release), TB and STD patients, patients receiving regular blood transfusion (e.g., thalassaemia or haemophilia patients), sex workers, and contacts of HIV-positive persons. Mandatory HIV testing applies to blood donors (a government policy since 1986) and a proportion (around 10%) of blue-collar migrant workers. Voluntary counselling and testing (VCT) is integrated into antenatal care, TB and STI services in government facilities, alongside anonymous testing, provider-initiated testing and counselling (PITC), and NGO-based community testing for high-risk and marginalised groups. Pre-marital HIV screening, piloted in Johor in 2001 among Muslim couples and now available in nearly all states in collaboration with Islamic authorities and State Health Departments, is now accessible to couples of any faith who wish to undergo it, ideally 2–3 months before marriage, with medical and (where relevant) religious counselling on the result.

Newer initiatives include a differentiated HIV services for key populations programme (DHSKP), implemented through health clinics in collaboration with the Malaysian AIDS Council (MAC) and originally supported by the Global Fund, integrating NGO-led case management/outreach with government clinic-based treatment; a PrEP pilot programme for MSM launched in 2023 across 18 health clinics in high-prevalence states (Klang Valley, Johor, Penang, Sabah), which by 2023 had recruited around 3,500 clients and showed declining syphilis co-infection and increasing condom use and single-partner behaviour over 6–12 months of follow-up, alongside proactive engagement with Islamic religious authorities (JAKIM National Fatwa Committee); and an HIV self-testing programme (HIVST) launched in December 2023 targeting young people and key populations, built around 5C principles (Consent, Confidentiality, Counselling, Correct results, Connection to care), accessible via a dedicated web portal and the MySejahtera application.

Malaysia has been eligible for Global Fund (GFATM) grants for HIV/AIDS since 2008 as a middle-income country with a concentrated epidemic; a Country Coordinating Mechanism (CCM), a multi-sectoral body of government, civil society and community representatives, was mandated in 2009 to oversee GF-supported programmes, with MAC serving as Principal Recipient. Internalised stigma remains prevalent: a 2021 study cited in the lecture found 70.1% of respondents living with HIV reported feelings of shame due to their status, while discriminatory attitudes (e.g., unwillingness to buy food from a vendor known to have HIV, or objection to HIV-positive children attending mainstream school) were reported by roughly 40–45% of the general population surveyed.

HIV/AIDS prevention and control mapped to primary/secondary/tertiary prevention ▼

The combination-prevention and treatment measures described above map onto the classic three levels of prevention as follows. Primary prevention (preventing infection before exposure/transmission occurs): behaviour-change programmes (ABC, CNN approaches), condom promotion, comprehensive sex education, harm reduction for people who inject drugs (needle-syringe exchange, opiate substitution therapy), voluntary medical male circumcision, and pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) for those with ongoing exposure risk — all host- or behaviour-directed measures that reduce the probability of transmission occurring at all, plus prevention of unintended pregnancy in HIV-positive women and prevention of HIV acquisition in women of reproductive age (the two "best" tiers of the PMTCT framework, since they prevent the transmission opportunity from arising in the first place rather than managing it after conception).

Secondary prevention (early detection of infection to interrupt transmission and initiate treatment before advanced immunosuppression): the three tiers of HIV testing (NAT, antigen/antibody, antibody-only/self-testing), Malaysia's routinely screened and mandatory testing programmes (antenatal, prison/rehabilitation-centre, blood donor, pre-marital screening), provider-initiated testing and counselling, HIV self-testing (HIVST) built around the 5C principles, and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) following a known exposure — all aimed at identifying infection (or preventing its establishment immediately post-exposure) as early as possible, since Malaysia's persistently high rate of late diagnosis (CD4 <350 at diagnosis, ~65–70%) indicates this secondary-prevention stage is where Malaysia's cascade currently underperforms relative to the first "95" and especially the second "95" of the 95-95-95 target.

Tertiary prevention (managing established infection to prevent progression, complications, and further transmission): antiretroviral therapy (ART)/HAART to suppress viral load, prevent opportunistic infections, and prolong survival; the third and fourth tiers of the PMTCT framework (ART during pregnancy, safer delivery, infant-feeding counselling, and ongoing care/treatment/support for HIV-infected parents, infants and families); universal precautions in healthcare settings (protecting others from onward transmission during care of an already-infected patient); and management of TB/HIV co-infection specifically, since TB is a leading cause of death among people already living with HIV and joint HIV/TB service scale-up is explicitly a tertiary-prevention intervention (managing a complication of established HIV infection) rather than a primary- or secondary-prevention one. Reframed this way, the fact that 89% of Malaysians on ART are virally suppressed shows tertiary prevention functioning reasonably well once patients are on treatment — reinforcing that Malaysia's main HIV cascade gap sits upstream, at secondary prevention (testing/linkage), not at the tertiary-prevention (treatment-efficacy) stage.

Core issue: Because Malaysia's HIV epidemic profile has shifted from injection-drug-use-dominated to sexual-transmission-dominated (illustrated concretely by the declining PWID/sexual transmission ratio shown in national data), prevention strategies designed around the earlier epidemic profile (harm reduction alone) are insufficient on their own; effective coverage of key populations facing stigma and, in places, legal risk (male-male sex remains illegal in Malaysia) remains a persistent structural barrier worth discussing explicitly in any Malaysia-focused HIV dissertation. Any epidemiological figures cited (prevalence, risk multiples, cascade percentages) should be tied to a specific UNAIDS/MOH reporting year, since these change from year to year.
9. CD Emergency Preparedness, Risk Communication & Disaster-Associated CD

Definitions and background: MySED and APSED III ▼

Emerging disease (per the MySED II Workplan, 2017–2021): infections that newly appear in a population, or have existed but are rapidly increasing in incidence or geographic range, including new diseases and re-emerging/resurging known and epidemic-prone diseases. Public health emergency: an occurrence or imminent threat of illness caused by bioterrorism, epidemic/pandemic disease, novel/highly fatal infectious agents or biological toxins, posing substantial risk of significant fatalities or long-term disability — for this course's purposes, mainly referring to emergencies from emerging diseases and/or acute public health events such as food safety incidents, which if unmanaged may cross national borders and become a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (e.g., an influenza pandemic).

The Asia Pacific region, including Malaysia, faces continual threats from emerging/re-emerging infectious disease outbreaks (Nipah virus, SARS, avian influenza, Influenza A(H1N1), Ebola, MERS-CoV, Zika, rabies), natural disasters, and bioterrorism. Malaysia's MySED Workplan (2012–2015) provided Malaysia's first framework for implementing and strengthening WHO's International Health Regulations (IHR, 2005); a 2016 evaluation confirmed its continued relevance, leading to the MySED II Workplan (2017–2021), developed through extensive multi-level consultation and aligned with the Asia Pacific Strategy for Emerging Diseases III (APSED III), the Joint External Evaluation (JEE), and the Global Health Security Agenda (GHSA). MySED II adopts an all-hazards approach and emphasises learning from past experience.

APSED III (a bi-regional WHO tool, originally 2005, updated 2010 and again as APSED III in 2016) exists to help WHO's South-East Asia and Western Pacific regions meet IHR core capacity requirements; it identified continued progress (rapid response team deployment, Field Epidemiology Training Programmes, improved diagnostic proficiency) alongside persistent gaps (systematic risk assessment, infection prevention and control, multisectoral engagement). APSED III's vision is prevention, detection and response to public health emergencies through collective regional responsibility, organised around eight essential public health functional areas.

MySED II's eight focus areas ▼

MySED II's scope is to strengthen public health security systems for all-hazard Public Health Emergency Preparedness (PHEP) and response, provide a common high-level framework for hazard-specific strategies, and strengthen core public health functions and health-system building blocks (workforce, service delivery, information/technology, leadership/governance). Its guiding principles include being country-focused, an all-hazards approach, a staged/step-by-step approach, continuous learning from past events, connecting national systems to regional/international levels, partnership for collective action, a forward-looking orientation, and financial sustainability for preparedness investment. Implementing agencies span well beyond the Ministry of Health: the National Disaster Management Agency (NADMA), National Security Council, Department of Veterinary Services, Ministry of Defence, Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Royal Malaysian Police, Fire and Rescue Department, the Atomic Energy Licensing Board, and others.

The eight focus areas, each with key elements and strategic actions: (1) Public Health Emergency Preparedness — developing/testing an all-hazards national response plan, an Incident Management System (IMS) using "4C" principles, strengthened IHR National Focal Points with 24/7 communication capability, and tested Points-of-Entry contingency plans; (2) Surveillance, Risk Assessment and Response — Malaysia's indicator-based surveillance (IBS) and event-based surveillance (EBS) systems, including the web-based e-Wabak outbreak reporting system, using multiple information sources (health and non-health sectors) for timely risk assessment, supported by a skilled FETP-trained workforce; (3) Laboratories — a network of reference, public health, pathology and research laboratories with capacity for priority disease diagnosis, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, and national/international/cross-sector referral linkage; (4) Zoonoses — multi-sectoral, multi-stakeholder surveillance information sharing, coordinated response, risk-reduction education, and standardised guidelines; (5) Prevention through Healthcare — infection prevention and control (IPC) culture, clinical management competency, and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) policy (Malaysia's National Surveillance of Antimicrobial Resistance reported rising hospital AMR from 2010–2015); (6) Risk Communication — treated as a core preparedness/response/recovery element in its own right (detailed below); (7) Regional Preparedness, Alert and Response — regional risk assessment systems, operational hubs, rapid response mechanisms and information sharing, reflecting the "borderless" nature of emerging disease threats; (8) Monitoring and Evaluation — systematic M&E across the planning/implementation cycle, measuring actual system functionality (not just the existence of capacities), and transparency/accountability through annual reporting, after-action reviews and JEE exercises.

Risk communication in disease outbreaks (WHO Outbreak Communication Planning Guide, 2008) ▼

Effective risk communication is an essential outbreak management tool, particularly when treatment options are limited, direct interventions take time to organise, and resources are scarce; pro-active communication encourages protective behaviour, facilitates surveillance, reduces confusion, and enables better resource use. Core principles: trust, announcing early, transparency, listening, and planning.

The WHO guide's seven planning steps: (1) Assessment — review existing plans and identify existing public communication capacity (language/translation, information networks, ability to reach hard-to-reach populations, existing listening mechanisms, relevant legal/policy constraints on information release); (2) Coordination — identify likely communication partners (public health and non-public-health organisations alike) and establish a coordination mechanism, illustrated by Uganda's National Task Force model chaired by a senior Ministry of Health official with a dedicated communication/social mobilisation sub-committee; (3) Transparency — establish a public communication policy/guideline and an emergency clearance process, since a lack of transparency increases rumour and misinformation and makes disease control harder; information on incidence/spread/containment, protective actions, risk assessments used in decision-making, and what remains unknown should all be proactively released, with clearance procedures tied to roles rather than specific individuals, and provisional/interim findings released with the caveat that recommendations may change (China's 2007 National Law for Emergency Response, categorising emergencies into four severity levels, is cited as one example policy framework); (4) Listening — gather information via community advisory panels, home visits, telephone hotlines, public opinion research, and media monitoring, to develop culturally appropriate communication strategies, identify barriers to interventions, and evaluate communication effectiveness; (5) Communication evaluation — both during the outbreak (assessing behaviour change, risk perception, message reach/comprehension) and after it (identifying areas for improvement); (6) Constructing an emergency communication plan — built from the previous steps, addressing distinct objectives across the outbreak's pre-event, introduction, rapid-increase/peak, and decline/resolution phases; (7) Training — simulation exercises (tabletop drills), spokesperson media experience, periodic plan review, and senior management endorsement.

Prevention of communicable disease after natural disasters ▼

Different disasters carry characteristic CD risks: cyclones (infectious diarrhoea, leptospirosis, dengue, malaria, cholera, acute respiratory infections, pulmonary TB); floods (viral infections, water-borne, vector-borne and airborne infections); earthquakes (cholera, tetanus, trauma/wounds, respiratory infections, malaria, dengue); tsunamis (waterborne diseases including diarrhoea/cholera/measles, and vector-borne diseases including malaria/dengue). The top five causes of death in emergencies/disasters are diarrhoea, acute respiratory infections, measles, malnutrition, and malaria (in endemic zones); the most common infectious diseases in disasters are food/water-borne disease, vector-borne disease, respiratory infection, and wound-related infection.

Priority actions to reduce post-disaster CD impact: (1) Site planning and shelter — appropriate shelter/site planning (sufficient space per person, access to water/fuel/transport, solid waste management, food store security, with GIS tools helpful for site selection) reduces CD incidence and is considered the first phase of disaster management; (2) Safe water and sanitation — chlorination (broadly available, low-cost, effective against nearly all waterborne pathogens) plus soap/hand-washing education against faecal-oral disease, meeting minimum standards for water access, quality, and safe consumption facilities; (3) Food safety ("5 keys to food safety" post-disaster); (4) Vector control — indoor residual spraying, insecticide-treated nets, and other vector-specific interventions (e.g., tsetse fly traps) mandatory in anticipation of vector-borne disease; (5) Basic laboratory facilities — useful for confirming suspected epidemics (e.g., guiding mass immunisation decisions for meningococcal meningitis, or culture/sensitivity testing for dysentery); (6) Access to primary care — critical for early diagnosis/treatment and preventing excess mortality/morbidity; (7) Immunisation — mass measles immunisation plus vitamin A supplementation is an immediate priority in areas with pre-existing coverage below 90% among children under 15, and tetanus-diphtheria vaccine plus tetanus antitoxin is essential around earthquakes and emergency surgery; (8) Surveillance — to detect emerging outbreaks early within the disaster-affected population.

Core issue: Preparedness capacity is genuinely multi-domain (the eight MySED II focus areas span far beyond the health sector alone, into disaster management, security, veterinary, and foreign affairs agencies); evaluating "preparedness" by reference to a single component (e.g., existence of a written plan) without assessing surveillance sensitivity, laboratory capacity, risk communication practice, and cross-agency coordination in a genuine simulation or real event will overstate actual readiness. Likewise, effective outbreak risk communication depends on structural groundwork (coordination mechanisms, clearance processes, listening infrastructure) established before an outbreak begins, not improvised during one — a distinction worth making explicit in any dissertation evaluating a real outbreak response's communication component.
10. Tuberculosis (TB)
Source: MPH GKT503 lecture "Tuberculosis (TB): Epidemiology, Prevention & Control" — compiled directly from the lecture deck.
Causative agent and pathogenesis ▼

TB is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (with M. bovis and M. africanum together accounting for under 10% of cases), an acid-fast bacillus discovered by Robert Koch in 1882. It is a rod-shaped, highly aerobic organism with humans as the only known reservoir, a remarkably slow doubling time of 18–24 hours, and the ability to remain viable for years in dried sputum, though it is destroyed by sunlight (heat/UV). TB is a major and resurgent worldwide health problem, one of the top 10 leading causes of death globally, and the most common cause of infectious-disease-related mortality worldwide.

Transmission is airborne (the main route) via direct droplets to the respiratory tract. Following exposure, roughly 70% of exposed individuals remain uninfected; of those with primary infection (~30%), most (~90%) develop latent TB infection (LTBI) rather than primary disease (~10%). The course of disease is determined by the balance between host immunity and bacillary virulence: LTBI carries a lifetime reactivation risk of roughly 5–10% in healthy adults, but substantially higher in children under 5 years (~20%), people living with HIV (~30%), and children under 2 years (~40%). Risk factors for progression from infection to disease include advanced/untreated HIV infection (relative risk approximately 9–10, and up to 200 for AIDS specifically), silicosis (RR ~30), recent infection (RR ~20), close contact with an infectious case (RR ~6), radiographic evidence of untreated old healed TB (RR ~5), high-dose corticosteroid treatment (RR ~2.8), chronic renal failure (RR ~2.4), TNF-alpha inhibitor therapy (RR ~2.0), poorly controlled diabetes (RR ~1.7), underweight status (RR ~1.6), and smoking (RR ~1.5).

Risk of infection, infectiousness and high-risk groups ▼

The annual risk of TB infection has fallen below 0.05% in developed countries but remains around 3% in parts of Africa and the Indian subcontinent. Risk of infection depends on the incidence/prevalence of infectious TB in the community, degree and intensity of contact (household contacts have roughly 30% infection risk, with close contacts more affected than casual contacts), duration of exposure, and environmental factors (ventilation, shared air space). Risk also increases with age (levelling off around 50–60 years), varies by sex/ethnicity/socioeconomic status, and is higher with inadequate treatment of the index case, in institutional settings (e.g., prisons), and with undernutrition.

Left untreated, a person with active TB disease infects on average 10–15 people per year. Infectiousness is directly related to the number of droplet nuclei expelled and is higher with a productive cough, lung cavitation, acid-fast bacilli visible on sputum smear, laryngeal/airway TB, not covering the mouth when coughing, inadequate treatment or prolonged illness, cough-inducing procedures, and positive sputum cultures. High-risk groups include contacts of smear-positive pulmonary TB cases, people living with HIV, people with diabetes mellitus, immigrants from high-TB-burden countries, people in institutions such as prisons and drug rehabilitation centres, people with other immunosuppressive conditions (renal failure, silicosis, prolonged steroid therapy, blood disorders), and tobacco smokers.

Classification, case definitions, diagnosis and drug resistance ▼

TB is classified as pulmonary TB (70–80% of cases, further split into smear-positive and smear-negative), extrapulmonary TB (10–20%), and mixed pulmonary-with-extrapulmonary disease (5–10%). Case definitions (per national TB guidelines) include new case, relapse case (sputum-positive or sputum-negative relapse), treatment failure, treatment after interruption, transferred-in case, and chronic case.

Diagnosis relies on clinical, radiological and/or bacteriological evidence. Pulmonary TB symptoms are typically insidious in onset, with productive cough usually lasting over 2 weeks (occasionally blood-stained), loss of appetite and weight, fever, and less commonly dyspnoea, night sweats, pleuritic chest pain and hoarseness, alongside extrapulmonary symptoms depending on the site involved. Investigations include sputum smear microscopy for acid-fast bacilli (Ziehl-Neelsen/fluorescence/LED microscopy — which detects only about half of true TB cases and can miss up to 40% of active cases if used alone, hence chest X-ray is recommended alongside it for symptomatic patients), bacteriological culture (egg-based media such as Löwenstein-Jensen takes up to 8 weeks; radiometric methods such as BACTEC/MGIT/BacT/ALERT 3D give results within about 2 weeks), chest X-ray, the Mantoux (tuberculin skin) test, and immunological/molecular methods including PCR, interferon-gamma release assays (IGRA), and GeneXpert MTB/RIF, a DNA/RNA amplification rapid test recommended by WHO.

Drug resistance: multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) denotes resistance to at least both rifampicin and isoniazid; extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) denotes resistance to both isoniazid and rifampicin plus any fluoroquinolone and at least one of three injectable second-line drugs (amikacin, kanamycin or capreomycin); totally drug-resistant TB (TDR-TB) has been reported but is not formally defined by WHO. An estimated 6.2% of TB cases globally were XDR-TB as of a 2016 estimate cited in the lecture. Newer agents in the treatment pipeline include bedaquiline (approved by the US FDA, demonstrated efficacious/safe in MDR-TB in phase II trials), delamanid, and the nitroimidazole-oxazine PA-824, both active against drug-sensitive and drug-resistant strains; isoniazid-resistant isolates have also been shown to remain fully susceptible to the investigational drug pyridomycin.

Global and Malaysian TB burden ▼

Globally in 2022, TB was the second leading infectious-disease killer after COVID-19 and the leading cause of death among people living with HIV. An estimated 10.6 million people fell ill with TB (5.8 million men, 3.5 million women, 1.3 million children), with people living with HIV accounting for 6.3% of cases; TB incidence rose by 3.9% between 2020 and 2022, reversing roughly two decades of ~2%-per-year decline (an effect widely attributed to COVID-19-related disruption of case-finding and treatment services). An estimated 1.30 million TB deaths occurred in 2022, down from 1.4 million in 2020–2021 but still nearly back to 2019 levels. Eight countries accounted for more than two-thirds of the global total: India, Indonesia, China, the Philippines, Pakistan, Nigeria, Bangladesh and the Democratic Republic of the Congo.

In Malaysia, total TB cases (all forms) rose from around 15,000 in 2000 to a peak of roughly 26,000 around 2016–2019, before declining somewhat in the early 2020s (partly reflecting pandemic-related case-finding disruption), with 19,961 cases and a notification rate of 59.8 per 100,000 population reported for 2023 (data to September). Sabah (notification rate 119.2 per 100,000) and Selangor (4,131 cases) carried disproportionately high burdens among Malaysian states in this reporting period. WHO's 2022 estimates for Malaysia placed total TB incidence at 113 per 100,000 (38,000 cases, range 31,000–44,000), HIV-positive TB incidence at 5 per 100,000, MDR/RR-TB incidence at 1.8 per 100,000, HIV-negative TB mortality at 7.3 per 100,000, and HIV-positive TB mortality at 2 per 100,000; an estimated 0.92% of new cases and 19% of previously treated cases had MDR/RR-TB. Malaysia's TB treatment success rate for new/relapse cases registered in 2021 was 79%, below the WHO target of 85%, while HIV-positive TB cases had a notably lower success rate of 56%.

Contributing factors specific to Malaysia discussed in the lecture include: TB among migrant/immigrant populations (contributing an estimated 10–13% of cases annually, with migration linked to higher LTBI reactivation risk in the years after arrival, and to challenges of language, stigma and limited healthcare access); TB-HIV co-infection (contributing roughly 5–10% of total TB cases, with HIV co-infection contributing an estimated 30.3% of TB deaths); TB and diabetes mellitus (Malaysia's diabetes prevalence is around 15% nationally, rising to 20% in Kelantan; TB-DM prevalence among TB patients is around 16.4% nationally and 27% in Kelantan, the highest reported; diabetes roughly triples the risk of active TB and is estimated to cause about 15% of current TB burden, worsening treatment outcomes, increasing relapse risk, and increasing TB mortality risk); TB in prisons (burden roughly 10-fold higher than the general population, with an estimated 53% of TB among prisoners globally undetected); and diagnostic/treatment delay (both patient-level delay in seeking care and health-system-level delay in diagnosis and treatment initiation, which increase community infectiousness and risk of late-stage disease).

Global and national TB strategy ▼

Millennium Development Goal 6 (Target 8) called for halting and reversing TB incidence by 2015; the Stop TB Partnership's linked targets sought a 50% reduction in TB prevalence and death rates by 2015 (relative to 1990) and elimination of TB as a public health problem (1 case per million population) by 2050. WHO's End TB Strategy, endorsed by the 67th World Health Assembly in 2014, envisions a world free of tuberculosis (zero deaths, disease and suffering), with milestone reductions in TB deaths of 35% by 2020 and 75% by 2025 (relative to 2015), rising to 90% by the 2030 SDG target and 95% by the 2035 End TB target, alongside milestone reductions in TB incidence of 20% (2020), 50% (2025), 80% (2030) and 90% (2035), and a target of zero TB-affected families facing catastrophic costs at every milestone. Its three pillars are: (1) integrated, patient-centred care and prevention (early diagnosis including universal drug-susceptibility testing and systematic contact/high-risk-group screening; treatment of all people with TB including drug-resistant TB; collaborative TB/HIV activities; preventive treatment and vaccination); (2) bold policies and supportive systems (political commitment and resourcing; engagement of communities and providers; universal health coverage and regulatory frameworks; social protection and poverty alleviation); and (3) intensified research and innovation.

Malaysia's National TB Control Programme (an integrated programme since 1994/1995) sets objectives to increase TB case detection (target 113 per 100,000), reduce TB incidence by 50% and TB deaths by 75% by 2025, achieve a 90% treatment success rate (including MDR-TB), strengthen LTBI management (screening, diagnosis, preventive treatment), and promote TB awareness through collaboration with NGOs, local agencies and communities. National strategy is organised around a "Detect–Test–Treat–Prevent" cycle: early detection through systematic high-risk-group screening and outreach (including mobile X-ray/AI-assisted screening); rapid, high-quality diagnostic testing (LED microscopy/X-ray for drug-sensitive TB, rapid molecular testing such as Xpert Ultra for drug-resistant TB, IGRA for LTBI); early diagnosis and treatment of TB/LTBI/drug-resistant TB with improved follow-up and surveillance; and prevention via BCG immunisation, LTBI screening/diagnosis/treatment, infection control, and community engagement. Public health challenges highlighted in the lecture include the need to strengthen disease awareness, high default/dropout rates disproportionately affecting marginalised groups (remote/rural populations, urban poor), improving information management and research capacity, significant TB burden among foreign migrant workers, co-morbidities (diabetes, HIV/AIDS, smoking), drug resistance (MDR/XDR), and stigma and discrimination — with the lecture posing an open question of whether Malaysia is "anywhere close" to TB elimination given these persistent challenges.

Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course (DOTS) is referenced in the lecture as a core WHO-recommended treatment-support strategy, though the lecture material directs further independent reading on its specific principles, strategies, implementation and known issues rather than providing full detail in the slides themselves.

TB prevention and control mapped to the epidemiological triad and primary/secondary/tertiary prevention ▼

The measures already described above for TB can be organised explicitly by level of prevention, using the same triad/chain-of-infection logic established in Section 1. Primary prevention (before infection occurs): BCG immunisation (host-directed, increasing resistance, though its protective effect against adult pulmonary TB is only partial); reducing transmission risk through improved ventilation and reduced overcrowding in high-risk congregate settings (institutions, prisons); and community education/awareness campaigns to reduce stigma-driven care avoidance, which indirectly reduces the pool of untreated infectious cases in the community (environment-directed).

Secondary prevention (after infection but aimed at early detection before advanced disease, or at halting progression from latent to active disease): systematic screening of high-risk groups and household/close contacts (contact tracing, given household contacts carry roughly 30% infection risk); LTBI screening, diagnosis and preventive treatment (chemoprophylaxis) specifically to prevent progression from latent infection to active disease in those at elevated reactivation risk (children under 5, people living with HIV); rapid diagnostic testing (sputum smear, chest X-ray, GeneXpert MTB/RIF, IGRA) to shorten the interval between symptom onset and treatment initiation, since diagnostic/treatment delay both worsens individual prognosis and increases community infectiousness; and mobile/AI-assisted X-ray outreach screening under Malaysia's "Detect" pillar, which functions as active case-finding in a population that would not otherwise present passively.

Tertiary prevention (managing established active disease to prevent complications, disability, drug resistance and further transmission): standardised multi-drug treatment regimens (including DOTS-supported adherence) to achieve cure and prevent progression to drug-resistant disease; specific management of MDR-TB/XDR-TB with second-line and newer agents (bedaquiline, delamanid, PA-824) once resistance has emerged; medical removal/isolation of infectious cases during the most contagious period of treatment to prevent onward transmission (mirroring the isolation/quarantine principle from Section 1, applied here to an active rather than a merely exposed case); and management of post-TB lung damage and disability, which the End TB Strategy's "zero catastrophic costs" target implicitly recognises as an ongoing tertiary-prevention and rehabilitation burden rather than one that ends once treatment is completed. Framed this way, Malaysia's persistently below-target treatment success rate (79% overall, 56% in HIV-positive TB cases, against an 85% WHO target) is specifically a tertiary-prevention shortfall, distinct from the primary-prevention question of BCG coverage or the secondary-prevention question of case-detection speed — a useful distinction for a dissertation that wants to pinpoint which stage of the prevention cascade is driving Malaysia's gap to the End TB milestones.

Core issue: TB control in Malaysia is complicated by the convergence of an ageing population, co-morbidities (diabetes, HIV, smoking) that both increase individual risk of progression to active disease and complicate treatment, and cross-border migration dynamics that sustain transmission independent of purely domestic control measures — meaning TB elimination cannot be modelled as a single-disease intervention problem, and a dissertation on TB in Malaysia should explicitly address at least one of these intersecting drivers rather than treating TB in isolation.

Q1Outbreak Investigation
Why should control measures typically be implemented before an outbreak investigation is fully complete?

Because delaying action until definitive laboratory confirmation allows continued transmission; historically (John Snow's cholera investigation) and in current practice, control measures are implemented once the epidemiological evidence plausibly supports a hypothesis, balancing the cost of premature action against the cost of continued spread.

Q2Zoonotic Diseases
Why can't human herd immunity alone eliminate a zoonotic disease such as leptospirosis or rabies?

Because the pathogen persists in a non-human (animal or environmental) reservoir independent of human immunity levels; herd immunity theory requires no reservoir to exist outside the immunised host species, a condition zoonoses do not meet — control therefore requires animal- and environment-sector measures (One Health approach), not human vaccination/immunity alone.

Q3Tuberculosis
Why does diabetes mellitus matter so much for TB control in Malaysia specifically, beyond being just another risk factor?

Because Malaysia has a comparatively high national diabetes prevalence (~15%, and ~20% in Kelantan), diabetes roughly triples individual risk of active TB and is estimated to account for around 15% of the current TB burden, while also worsening treatment outcomes (lower drug concentrations, higher relapse and death risk) — meaning a large and rising share of Malaysia's TB burden is being sustained by a non-communicable disease risk factor rather than by transmission dynamics alone, which has direct implications for how TB control programmes should be resourced (bidirectional TB-DM screening) rather than treating TB purely as an infectious-disease problem.

Q4HIV/AIDS
Why is Malaysia's declining male-to-PWID HIV transmission ratio significant for prevention programme design?

Because Malaysia's HIV response was originally built around harm reduction (needle-syringe exchange, opiate substitution therapy) to address a PWID-dominated epidemic; as the mode of transmission has shifted toward sexual transmission (heterosexual and male-same-sex), harm reduction alone no longer addresses the dominant transmission route, requiring a parallel scale-up of condom promotion, PrEP, and outreach to key populations such as MSM — a mismatch between historical programme design and current epidemic profile that a dissertation should identify explicitly rather than assuming existing harm-reduction infrastructure is sufficient.
