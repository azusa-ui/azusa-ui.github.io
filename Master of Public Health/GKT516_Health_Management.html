1. Health Systems & Management
Source: WHO Health Systems Framework; Roemer's model; MOH Malaysia structure
🏥 What is a Health System?

WHO defines a health system as "all the organisations, people, and actions whose primary intent is to promote, restore, or maintain health." This is broader than "the health sector" — it includes home care, traditional/complementary medicine, community health volunteers, and inter-sectoral action on the social determinants of health (housing, water, sanitation, education), not just hospitals and clinics.

WHO's World Health Report 2000 frames three intrinsic goals any health system should be judged against:

  • Improved health — both the average level (e.g., life expectancy) and its distribution across the population (equity in health).
  • Responsiveness — non-health-improving aspects that matter to users: dignity, autonomy, confidentiality, prompt attention, choice of provider.
  • Fairness in financial contribution — households pay according to ability to pay, not according to how sick they are.

Underneath these sit instrumental (intermediate) objectives — access, coverage, quality, safety, and efficiency — the levers managers actually pull to reach the intrinsic goals.

Goal: better health, responsiveness, and fairness in financial contribution — not just more hospitals. A system can have excellent buildings and still fail on all three goals if financing is regressive and workforce is maldistributed.
Worked comparison: three classic health-system financing models ▼
ModelFunding sourceExample countriesKey strength / weakness
Beveridge (National Health Service)General taxationUK, Malaysia (public tier)Universal, equitable; can be under-funded, long queues
Bismarck (Social Health Insurance)Payroll-linked mandatory insurance, employer + employeeGermany, JapanStrong provider-payment link; can exclude informal-sector workers
Out-of-pocket / private-dominantDirect user payment at point of careMany LMICs' private tierNo pre-payment risk pooling → catastrophic-expenditure risk

Malaysia runs a hybrid: a tax-funded public system (Beveridge-like, heavily subsidised at point of use) alongside a fee-for-service private sector and employer/private insurance — this is why national health-financing reform debates keep resurfacing.

🧱 WHO Building Blocks

WHO's Everybody's Business (2007) framework decomposes a health system into six interdependent building blocks. Each has its own performance indicators, and a weakness in one block undermines the returns on investment in every other block.

Building blockWhat it coversTypical indicator
1. Service deliveryOrganisation and delivery of effective, safe, quality interventionsEffective coverage of a service (e.g., % hypertensives controlled)
2. Health workforceSufficient, competent, responsive, fairly distributed staffDensity per 1,000 population; % in rural posts
3. Health information systemProduction, analysis, dissemination, use of reliable dataCompleteness of vital registration; reporting timeliness
4. Access to essential medicinesEquitable access to safe, quality, affordable medicines & technologies% facilities with no stock-out of tracer medicines
5. FinancingRaising, pooling, purchasing (allocating) fundsOut-of-pocket % of total health expenditure
6. Leadership / governancePolicy frameworks, oversight, coalition-building, accountabilityExistence & implementation of a costed national health plan
A weakness in any block compromises the whole system — hence the 'building blocks' metaphor. Example: a new hospital wing (service delivery) with no additional nurses (workforce) and no essential-medicine restocking budget (financing) will not raise effective coverage — it may even worsen burnout in existing wards stretched to cover it.
Worked example: linking two building blocks ▼

Scenario: a district doubles its number of primary-care clinics (service delivery ↑) but workforce output from training institutions stays flat, and financing for locum/contract staff is not increased.

Consequence chain: existing staff are split across more sites → contact time per patient falls → process quality (block 1) falls → outcomes (e.g., diabetes control) may not improve despite more "access points" on paper. Building-block indicators should therefore always be read together, not in isolation.

🧭 Governance & Leadership

Stewardship is WHO's term for the careful, responsible management of population wellbeing — the ministry of health acts as the system's "steward" even for parts of the system (private providers, NGOs) it does not directly own or run.

  • Stewardship functions: generating intelligence (surveillance, evidence), formulating strategic policy direction, ensuring tools for implementation (powers, incentives, sanctions), building coalitions/partnerships, aligning policy objectives with organisational structure/culture, and holding actors accountable.
  • Good governance principles (UNDP/WHO): participation, rule of law, transparency, responsiveness, consensus-orientation, equity/inclusiveness, effectiveness/efficiency, accountability.
  • Leadership vs management (Kotter): management copes with complexity — planning, budgeting, organising, controlling, problem-solving to produce predictable results. Leadership copes with change — setting direction, aligning people, motivating/inspiring. Both are needed; a good hospital CEO does both roles at different moments.
  • Centralisation vs decentralisation: devolving authority (political, administrative, fiscal) to state/district level can improve local responsiveness but risks fragmenting standards and capacity if local management skills are weak.
"Doing the right things" (leadership/strategy) vs "doing things right" (management/operations) — a system needs both; excellent operational management of the wrong strategy still fails.
Case: decentralising hospital management — trade-offs ▼

Argument for: hospital boards/CEOs closer to local needs can respond faster, tailor services, and be held locally accountable.

Argument against: without strong central stewardship (minimum standards, pooled procurement, referral protocols), decentralisation can widen inter-facility quality gaps and lose economies of scale in purchasing (e.g., drugs, equipment).

Balance point: most systems keep policy/standard-setting and large capital investment centralised while devolving day-to-day operational and some budget-execution authority to hospital/state level — sometimes called "deconcentration."

2. Quality & Patient Safety
Source: Donabedian's SPO framework; WHO Global Patient Safety Action Plan 2021–2030; IOM Crossing the Quality Chasm
⭐ Dimensions of Quality (Donabedian)

Avedis Donabedian's Structure–Process–Outcome (SPO) framework remains the backbone of quality measurement:

  • Structure — the setting of care: facilities, staff-to-patient ratios, equipment, health information systems, accreditation status.
  • Process — what is actually done to and for the patient: adherence to clinical guidelines, correct diagnosis, communication, referral pathways.
  • Outcome — the result: mortality, morbidity, patient-reported outcomes, patient satisfaction.

The assumption behind SPO is a causal chain: good structure enables good process, which produces good outcome — but the links are probabilistic, not guaranteed (a well-staffed hospital can still run poor processes).

WHO/IOM commonly describe six dimensions of quality, remembered by the mnemonic STEEEP: Safe, Timely, Effective, Efficient, Equitable, Patient-centred.

DimensionMeaningExample indicator
SafeAvoiding harm from the care intended to helpRate of healthcare-associated infection
EffectiveEvidence-based care, avoiding under/over-use% diabetics with HbA1c at target
Patient-centredRespectful of, and responsive to, individual preferences/needsPatient experience survey score
TimelyReducing waits and harmful delaysDoor-to-needle time in stroke care
EfficientAvoiding waste of equipment, supplies, ideas, energyCost per case-mix-adjusted admission
EquitableQuality does not vary by gender, ethnicity, location, SESCoverage gap between richest & poorest quintile
Measuring only 'outcome' misses the structure/process levers that produce it — e.g., a rising maternal mortality ratio (outcome) is uninformative for action unless you also audit blood-bank availability (structure) and partograph use (process).
🔧 Quality Improvement Models
  • Plan–Do–Study–Act (PDSA) — a rapid-cycle model: Plan a small test of change → Do it on a small scale → Study the data against predictions → Act (adopt, adapt, or abandon) before scaling up. Multiple small PDSA "ramps" are chained rather than one big-bang rollout.
  • Six Sigma (DMAIC) — Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control — a statistically rigorous method aiming to reduce process variation to near-zero defects.
  • Lean — focuses on removing waste from the patient's value stream, summarised by the mnemonic TIMWOODS — Transport, Inventory, Motion, Waiting, Over-production, Over-processing, Defects, Skills (under-used staff talent).
  • Clinical audit cycle — set a standard/criterion → measure current practice against it → feed back results → implement change → re-audit ("closing the loop") to confirm improvement.
QI is continuous, team-based, and data-driven — distinct from research, which aims to generate generalisable new knowledge under controlled conditions. QI asks "did our unit get better?"; research asks "does this intervention work in general?"
Worked example: clinical audit of hand-hygiene compliance ▼

Standard set: WHO "5 Moments for Hand Hygiene" — target ≥80% compliance.

Baseline audit: 60 of 100 observed opportunities were compliant → 60/100 = 60% (below standard).

Intervention (PDSA cycle): alcohol-based handrub at every bedside + visual reminders + peer "hand-hygiene champion" feedback for 4 weeks.

Re-audit: 88 of 100 opportunities compliant → 88% (meets/exceeds standard) → the loop is "closed" and monitoring continues (e.g., monthly spot-checks) rather than the initiative being abandoned.

⚠️ Patient Safety & Error

An error is the failure of a planned action to be completed as intended (error of execution) or the use of a wrong plan to achieve an aim (error of planning) — James Reason's classic definition.

  • Slips & lapses — attentional/memory failures during a routine task (e.g., picking up the wrong pre-filled syringe). Mistakes — a flawed plan/rule is correctly executed (e.g., following an outdated protocol). Violations — deliberate deviation from a known safe procedure.
  • Active failures (frontline, immediate, "sharp end") vs latent conditions (systemic, "blunt end" — understaffing, poor rostering, confusing drug labelling, weak safety culture) that lie dormant until triggered.
  • Adverse event = unintended harm caused by medical management rather than the underlying disease. Near miss = an error that did not reach the patient/caused no harm — a free lesson. Sentinel event = an unexpected occurrence involving death or serious physical/psychological injury, signalling the need for immediate investigation.
  • Root Cause Analysis (RCA) — structured retrospective technique (e.g., "5 Whys", fishbone/Ishikawa diagram) tracing an adverse event back from the sharp-end action to underlying latent/systemic causes.
  • Reporting & learning systems must be non-punitive and confidential to surface latent conditions — a just culture distinguishes blameless human error, at-risk behaviour (coaching needed), and reckless behaviour (disciplinary action needed), often via a "substitution test" (would another competent professional in the same circumstances have done the same?).
Reason's Swiss-cheese model: each defence layer (design, training, procedures, supervision) has weaknesses ('holes') that shift over time. An accident occurs only when holes in successive layers momentarily line up, letting a hazard trajectory reach the patient — most bad outcomes therefore reflect a chain of small systemic failures, not one culpable individual.
Worked example: "5 Whys" root cause analysis ▼

Event: a patient received 10× the intended insulin dose.

  1. Why? — The nurse drew up 10 mL instead of 1 mL.
  2. Why? — The insulin vial and a look-alike diluent vial were stored side-by-side.
  3. Why? — Pharmacy shelving was not redesigned after a recent formulary change.
  4. Why? — There is no standard protocol requiring visual-alert separation of high-alert medications.
  5. Why? — The hospital has no high-alert medication policy aligned with national/WHO guidance.

Root cause: systemic — absence of a high-alert medication storage policy (latent condition), not simply "nurse error" (active failure). Corrective action targets the system (separate storage, independent double-check for insulin) rather than only re-training the individual nurse.

3. Human Resources for Health
Source: WHO Global Strategy on HRH 2030; Global Health Workforce Alliance; MOH Malaysia workforce data
👩‍⚕️ HRH — the Core of the System

Health workers are the single most important input to a health system — "no health workforce, no health services." The 2006 World Health Report labelled the global shortage a crisis, and WHO's Global Strategy on HRH projects a shortfall of roughly 10 million health workers by 2030, concentrated in low- and lower-middle-income countries.

  • Stock — absolute numbers/density (e.g., doctors, nurses per 1,000 population). Historic reference thresholds range from ~2.3 per 1,000 (Joint Learning Initiative, basic MCH coverage) to ~4.45 per 1,000 (WHO SDG index threshold).
  • Distribution — urban–rural and public–private maldistribution is usually worse than the national average suggests; a country can meet the national density threshold while rural districts remain critically understaffed.
  • Skill-mix — the balance of cadres (doctor, nurse, allied health, community health worker) suited to the burden of disease and service model.
  • Performance — availability, competence, responsiveness, productivity — quantity of staff does not guarantee quality of care.
Malaysia context: the country experienced the opposite problem in the 2010s — rapid expansion of medical schools produced a perceived oversupply of house officers relative to training/specialist posts, prompting the contract doctor/pharmacist scheme from 2016 — while rural and East Malaysia postings remained chronically hard to fill. "Shortage" and "maldistribution/oversupply" can therefore coexist within one country.
Worked example: calculating workforce density & gap ▼

Density formula: Density per 1,000 = (number of active health workers ÷ total population) × 1,000.

Example: a state has 900 doctors serving a population of 1,200,000. Density = (900 ÷ 1,200,000) × 1,000 = 0.75 per 1,000. If the policy target is 1.8 per 1,000, the state needs 1.8 × 1,200,000 ÷ 1,000 = 2,160 doctors — a gap of 1,260 doctors to close through training, recruitment, or retention.

💡 Motivation & Retention
  • Maslow's hierarchy — physiological (living wage) → safety (job security) → belonging (team support) → esteem (recognition) → self-actualisation (professional growth); explains why pay alone does not fix retention once basic needs are met.
  • Herzberg's two-factor theory — hygiene factors (salary, working conditions, job security, policies) prevent dissatisfaction but do not motivate if merely adequate; motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth) drive real engagement.
  • Vroom's expectancy theory — Motivation = Expectancy × Instrumentality × Valence: staff must believe effort leads to performance (expectancy), performance leads to a reward (instrumentality), and the reward is personally valued (valence). If any factor is zero, motivation collapses.
  • Task-shifting / task-sharing — delegating tasks to less specialised cadres where safe and competent (e.g., nurse-led hypertension titration, community volunteers doing TB DOTS follow-up) — extends coverage without waiting years for more doctors.
  • Retention levers: rural/hardship allowances, guaranteed career pathway and specialist training slots, adequate staff housing, supportive supervision, manageable workload, bonding schemes for scholarship recipients.
Performance = Competency × Motivation × Enabling environment — fix all three; a highly motivated, competent nurse still under-performs without functioning equipment and supply chains (the enabling environment).
Worked example: expectancy-theory scoring ▼

Score each 0–1: Expectancy (effort → performance) = 0.9; Instrumentality (performance → reward) = 0.3 (staff doubt the promotion board is fair); Valence (value of reward) = 0.9 (promotion highly valued).

Motivation index ≈ 0.9 × 0.3 × 0.9 = 0.24 (low) — despite staff being capable and wanting the reward, weak instrumentality (perceived unfair reward system) is the bottleneck management should fix first, not more training.

📋 HR Planning

Workforce planning matches future supply (existing stock + graduates + in-migration − attrition/emigration/retirement) to future demand (population growth & ageing, disease burden, service targets such as UHC benefit packages).

  • Needs-based method — starts from epidemiological need (disease burden, guideline staffing ratios) — normative but can be unaffordable.
  • Demand-based method — projects utilisation trends and what the health system can actually finance/absorb.
  • Supply projection / stock-and-flow modelling — models the pipeline: entrants from training institutions minus attrition (retirement, resignation, migration), over time.
  • WHO's Workload Indicators of Staffing Need (WISN) — a facility-level method: define workload components (e.g., outpatient consults, deliveries), assign standard time per activity, compute required staff = total annual workload time ÷ available working time per staff member.
Track density (workers per 1,000 population) and distribution (a maldistribution index) — a national average masks large sub-national gaps.
Worked example: simplified WISN calculation ▼

A primary care clinic sees 20,000 outpatient visits/year, averaging 15 minutes/visit → total workload = 20,000 × 15 = 300,000 minutes = 5,000 hours/year.

Available working time per full-time doctor ≈ 1,600 hours/year (after leave, training, admin time deducted from a nominal 2,000-hour year).

Required doctors = 5,000 ÷ 1,600 ≈ 3.1 → 3 to 4 doctors needed for outpatient workload alone (before adding inpatient/on-call duties).

4. Health Economics & Financing
Source: health economics fundamentals; WHO-CHOICE; World Bank UHC financing indicators
💰 Costs & Scarcity

Health economics starts from a simple premise: resources (money, staff, beds, time) are scarce relative to unlimited health needs/wants, so choices — and trade-offs — are unavoidable. Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative forgone by a choice (e.g., funding a new dialysis centre may mean forgoing an equivalent expansion of a childhood immunisation programme).

  • Fixed costs (rent, capital equipment — do not vary with output in the short run) vs variable costs (drugs, disposables — scale with activity).
  • Direct costs (medical resources used) vs indirect costs (productivity loss, caregiver time) vs intangible costs (pain, anxiety — hard to monetise).
  • Marginal cost — the cost of producing one more unit of output; relevant to "should we do one more" decisions, distinct from average cost.

Market failures specific to health justify government intervention in what would otherwise be left to a competitive market:

Market failureExplanationExample
Information asymmetryProvider knows more than patient about what care is neededSupplier-induced demand — unnecessary tests/procedures
ExternalitiesEffects on third parties not reflected in priceVaccination protects others (positive); untreated TB spreads (negative)
Moral hazardInsured people over-consume care since a third party paysOver-utilisation once insured/covered
Adverse selectionHigher-risk individuals more likely to seek insuranceVoluntary private insurance pools skew sicker/older → premiums rise
Health as a "merit good"Society values access beyond what individual demand alone would provideJustifies public subsidy for essential/preventive services
Economics informs allocation under scarcity, not just accounting — the central policy question is rarely "can we afford this?" in isolation but "what do we give up to afford this?" (opportunity cost). The equity–efficiency trade-off (maximise total health vs distribute it fairly) runs through almost every financing and priority-setting decision.
🎯 Efficacy vs Effectiveness vs Efficiency

These three terms are colloquially used as synonyms but carry distinct technical meanings in health management and health economics — a favourite short-answer/MCQ trap.

TermDefinitionStudy settingExample
EfficacyThe capacity of an intervention to produce benefit under ideal / controlled conditionsRandomised controlled trial (RCT) — strict eligibility criteria, protocol-driven adherence, close monitoringA malaria vaccine reduces clinical episodes by 60% in a tightly monitored RCT cohort
EffectivenessThe extent to which an intervention achieves its intended effect under real-world / routine practice conditionsObservational studies, pragmatic trials, programme/field evaluation — patients with comorbidities, variable adherence, resource constraintsThe same vaccine reduces clinical episodes by only 35% once rolled out nationally, because of cold-chain lapses and missed second doses
EfficiencyThe ratio of output/outcome achieved to input/resources consumed — "doing things in the most economical way"Cost analysis, economic evaluationProgramme A achieves the same coverage as Programme B at 70% of the unit cost per child immunised

A simple way to remember the distinction (adapted from health-economics teaching): efficacy = "can it work?" (best-case, ideal conditions), effectiveness = "does it work?" (usual conditions, real patients), efficiency = "is it worth it?" (value for money, given scarce resources). Evidence hierarchies traditionally rank RCTs (efficacy evidence) above observational/pragmatic studies, yet effectiveness studies are what decision-makers actually need to predict programme performance at scale — the "efficacy–effectiveness gap" is a recognised limitation of relying on trial data alone to plan roll-out.

A drug can be highly efficacious in a trial but poorly effective in the field (adherence, access, comorbidity), and even an effective drug can be inefficient if a cheaper alternative achieves a similar outcome — all three lenses are needed before a programme decision is sound.
Worked example: efficacy vs effectiveness vs efficiency for the same intervention ▼

Intervention: a new antihypertensive drug.

  • Efficacy finding: in an RCT with strict inclusion criteria and pill counts confirming ≥95% adherence, the drug lowers systolic BP by 15 mmHg vs placebo.
  • Effectiveness finding: once prescribed in routine primary care (patients with polypharmacy, variable adherence, no research-nurse follow-up), average population-level SBP reduction is only 8 mmHg.
  • Efficiency finding: the drug costs RM 45/month; an older generic achieves a 6 mmHg reduction at RM 8/month. Cost per mmHg reduced: new drug ≈ RM 5.6/mmHg; generic ≈ RM 1.3/mmHg — the generic is more efficient even though the new drug is more effective in absolute terms. The final funding decision requires weighing the extra effectiveness against the extra cost (i.e., an ICER), not efficacy or effectiveness alone.
⚖️ Economic Evaluation
TypeComparator / output
Cost-minimisation (CMA)Assumes equal outcomes → simply compares cost
Cost-effectiveness (CEA)Cost per natural unit of effect (e.g., per case detected, per DALY averted)
Cost-utility (CUA)Cost per QALY gained (incorporates quality-of-life utility weights)
Cost-benefit (CBA)All outcomes monetised → Net Present Value (NPV) / Benefit-Cost Ratio (BCR)

QALY (Quality-Adjusted Life Year) = years of life lived × utility weight (0 = dead, 1 = perfect health). DALY (Disability-Adjusted Life Year) = YLL (Years of Life Lost to premature death) + YLD (Years Lived with Disability) — DALYs measure burden (lower is better), QALYs measure health gained (higher is better), a common source of confusion.

ICER = (CostA − CostB) ÷ (EffectA − EffectB) — the extra cost per extra unit of effect of the new option (A) versus the comparator (B); it is only interpretable against a willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold (e.g., NICE historically used ~£20,000–30,000/QALY; WHO-CHOICE historically suggested 1–3× GDP per capita per DALY averted, a rule-of-thumb since widely critiqued and superseded by country-specific thresholds).
Worked example: computing an ICER and reading the cost-effectiveness plane ▼

Data: new drug A costs RM 5,000/patient/year and yields 6.2 QALYs. Standard drug B costs RM 3,000/patient/year and yields 5.8 QALYs.

ICER = (5,000 − 3,000) ÷ (6.2 − 5.8) = 2,000 ÷ 0.4 = RM 5,000 per additional QALY.

Interpretation: if the decision-maker's WTP threshold is, say, RM 30,000/QALY (an illustrative benchmark only), drug A is cost-effective and worth funding at the margin. If A had cost more and produced fewer QALYs than B (dominated), it would be rejected outright regardless of threshold — this is the "cost-effectiveness plane" logic (four quadrants: dominant, dominated, and two trade-off quadrants).

Applied example: economic evaluation of HIV PrEP and PEP ▼

Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) — daily oral antiretroviral medication taken by an HIV-negative person before potential exposure, to prevent acquisition. Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) — a short antiretroviral course started within 72 hours after a known or suspected exposure (occupational, e.g., needle-stick, or non-occupational, e.g., unprotected sex/assault) to prevent seroconversion.

Health-technology-assessment reviews of PrEP economic evaluations consistently report that cost-effectiveness (and sometimes cost-savings) is highly context-dependent on three drivers: (1) the baseline HIV incidence in the population targeted, (2) adherence to the daily regimen (efficacy in trials assumed near-perfect adherence; real-world effectiveness is lower), and (3) the drug price (generic tenofovir/emtricitabine substantially improves the cost-effectiveness ratio versus branded product). Targeting PrEP at those with the highest risk of HIV exposure (rather than the general population) markedly improves cost per infection averted, because fewer person-years of prophylaxis are "spent" per infection prevented.

Non-occupational PEP shows a similar pattern: it can be cost-effective or even cost-saving depending on the type/source of exposure (e.g., PEP after exposure to a known HIV-positive source with high viral load is far more favourable than "blanket" PEP for very-low-risk exposures), because the denominator of exposures needing treatment to avert one infection is smaller when risk-targeted.

Illustrative worked logic: suppose targeted PrEP in a high-incidence key population costs RM 2,000/person/year and prevents 1 HIV infection per 40 person-years of use (a plausible order of magnitude in high-incidence settings). Cost per infection averted ≈ RM 2,000 × 40 = RM 80,000. If the lifetime discounted cost of treating one HIV infection (lifelong antiretroviral therapy, monitoring, complications) exceeds RM 80,000 — which it typically does over a patient's lifetime — the intervention can be cost-saving, not merely cost-effective, illustrating why risk-targeting (rather than universal provision) is repeatedly highlighted as the single biggest lever on PrEP's economic case.

Key gap noted in the literature: evidence on how best to identify high-risk individuals for targeting, and the costs of that identification process itself (risk-screening, counselling), remains limited — a reminder that the numerator/denominator of any CEA depends on real-world implementation costs, not just drug price.

🌍 Universal Health Coverage & Financing
  • UHC = all people receive the promotive, preventive, curative, rehabilitative, and palliative services they need, of sufficient quality, without suffering financial hardship paying for them.
  • The classic "UHC cube" has three dimensions decision-makers must jointly expand and can trade off against each other: (1) population covered — who is covered; (2) services covered — breadth of the benefits package; (3) direct costs covered — proportion of cost paid by pooled/prepaid funds vs the patient (depth). Pushing outward on one face while holding a fixed budget usually means giving up ground on another.
  • Pre-payment & risk pooling (general taxation, mandatory social health insurance) spreads financial risk across a large population and across time (healthy subsidise sick, rich subsidise poor, working-age subsidise old/young) — voluntary private insurance and out-of-pocket payment do not pool risk broadly enough to deliver this protection.
  • Catastrophic health expenditure — commonly defined as out-of-pocket health spending exceeding 10% of total household consumption/income (or 25% for a stricter cut, and 40% of capacity-to-pay in WHO/World Bank reporting).
  • Impoverishing health expenditure — out-of-pocket payment that pushes a household below the poverty line, even if the absolute amount seems small.
Malaysia pursues UHC primarily via a tax-funded public system (Ministry of Health hospitals/clinics, heavily subsidised at the point of use) running alongside a fee-for-service private sector; long-running policy debate continues on introducing a broader national health financing/social health insurance scheme to diversify revenue sources as the population ages and non-communicable disease costs rise.
Worked example: is this household's spending "catastrophic"? ▼

A household has total monthly consumption expenditure of RM 3,000, of which RM 1,000 is spent on food (non-discretionary). Capacity to pay = RM 3,000 − RM 1,000 = RM 2,000. The household spends RM 900/month out-of-pocket on a family member's chronic dialysis-related costs.

Out-of-pocket as % of capacity to pay = 900 ÷ 2,000 = 45% — above the common 40% "catastrophic health expenditure" cut-off, meaning this household is at financial risk of poverty due to illness, illustrating why renal replacement therapy is prioritised for subsidy in many national UHC benefit packages.

5. Strategic & Risk Management
Source: GKT 516 Course Notes — planning, BSC; Risk
🗺️ Strategic Planning

Strategic management is commonly modelled (Wheelen & Hunger) as a continuous four-stage cycle: environmental scanning → strategy formulation → strategy implementation → evaluation & control, feeding back into the next scanning cycle.

  • Environmental scanning — external (opportunities/threats in the wider environment) and internal (organisational strengths/weaknesses) analysis, done before committing to a direction.
  • Strategy formulation — clarifying mission (why we exist, our core purpose), vision (what we aspire to become), objectives (specific measurable targets), and selecting a strategy (the broad approach chosen to achieve objectives) and supporting tactics/action plans (concrete short-term steps).
  • Strategy implementation — translating strategy into programmes, budgets, and procedures; requires structure, resources, and culture aligned to the chosen direction.
  • Evaluation & control — monitoring performance indicators against targets and feeding deviations back to revise strategy — strategic planning is iterative, not a one-off document.

Levels of strategy: corporate (which businesses/services should we be in — e.g., should a hospital open a new dialysis centre or expand maternity services?), business/competitive (how do we compete/position within a chosen service — e.g., differentiate on quality vs cost leadership), and functional (how do departments — HR, finance, clinical operations — support the chosen strategy day to day).

Strategy is about choices under uncertainty — deciding what not to do (which services, populations, or investments to forgo) is as strategically important as deciding what to pursue, because resources committed to one direction are opportunity costs against every other direction.
SWOT and PESTEL analysis — frameworks and worked example ▼

SWOT audits the organisation itself and its immediate environment:

HelpfulHarmful
Internal (organisation)Strengths — resources/capabilities that give an advantage (e.g., an experienced specialist team, strong reputation, modern equipment)Weaknesses — internal limitations (e.g., outdated IT systems, high staff turnover, limited bed capacity)
External (environment)Opportunities — favourable external trends to exploit (e.g., new government UHC funding stream, rising demand for a service)Threats — external risks (e.g., new competing private hospital, tightening reimbursement rates, workforce migration)

PESTEL scans the wider macro-environment feeding into Opportunities/Threats: Political (health policy priorities, election cycles), Economic (funding levels, currency/inflation affecting drug costs), Social (demographic ageing, health-seeking behaviour), Technological (telemedicine, AI diagnostics), Environmental (climate-related disease patterns, sustainability pressure), Legal/regulatory (licensing, data-protection law, accreditation standards).

Worked example — a public hospital considering a new outpatient dialysis unit:

  • Strength: existing nephrology specialists on staff.
  • Weakness: current facility has no spare floor space for new machines.
  • Opportunity: national UHC benefit package now covers renal replacement therapy more generously (per the financing discussion above).
  • Threat: a private provider nearby is already expanding dialysis capacity and may capture referrals first.

A TOWS matrix then converts each SWOT cell into an action: SO strategy (use strength to seize opportunity) — deploy existing nephrology expertise to fast-track a funding proposal under the expanded UHC package; WT strategy (minimise weakness and avoid threat) — if space cannot be found quickly, negotiate a shared-service/referral partnership with the private provider rather than competing head-on.

📊 Balanced Scorecard

Kaplan & Norton's Balanced Scorecard (BSC) was designed to correct the historical over-reliance on lagging financial indicators alone, by measuring performance across four linked perspectives, each answering a different strategic question:

PerspectiveQuestionExample measure (hospital)
FinancialAre we financially sustainable / creating value for funders?Cost per case-mix-adjusted admission; budget variance
Customer/PatientDo we meet the needs of patients & other key stakeholders?Patient satisfaction score; waiting-time targets met
Internal processWhich internal processes must we excel at to satisfy patients & funders?Hand-hygiene compliance rate; readmission rate; theatre utilisation
Learning & growthCan we continue to improve, innovate, and build capacity?Staff training hours completed; staff turnover rate; number of QI projects active

The four perspectives are causally linked in a "strategy map": investment in learning & growth (staff skills, systems) improves internal processes (safer, faster care), which improves customer/patient outcomes and satisfaction, which in turn secures financial sustainability (funding, reputation, referrals) — financial results are the lagging outcome of the other three, not the sole starting point.

The BSC deliberately prevents over-focus on finance at the expense of quality, patient experience, and staff development — a hospital that hits its budget target while hand-hygiene compliance and staff morale collapse has only appeared to succeed on one of four perspectives.

🛡️ Clinical / Organisational Risk Management

Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) frameworks (e.g., ISO 31000, COSO) describe risk management as a continuous, integrated cycle rather than a one-off checklist, applied across clinical, financial, legal, reputational, and strategic risk categories simultaneously — a weakness treated in isolation in one category (e.g., financial) can still expose the organisation through another (e.g., clinical/reputational) if risks are managed in silos.

  • Risk identification — scanning for emerging hazards (incident reports, complaints, audits, near-miss data, environmental scanning of external risks).
  • Risk analysis — estimating likelihood and severity/impact of each identified risk.
  • Risk evaluation — ranking risks (e.g., via a risk matrix/heat map) against the organisation's risk appetite to decide which need action first.
  • Risk response/treatment — four classic options: avoid (stop the risky activity altogether), reduce/mitigate (controls that lower likelihood or severity — e.g., checklists, double-checks), transfer (insurance, outsourcing, contractual indemnity), or accept (retain the residual risk consciously, usually when it is low-likelihood/low-impact or treatment cost exceeds the benefit).
  • Monitoring & review — tracking whether treatments actually reduced the risk, and re-scanning as the environment changes; communication & consultation with stakeholders runs throughout every stage of the cycle, not just at the end.

Clinical risk specifically refers to the risk of harm arising from clinical care itself (see also Section 2's Swiss-cheese model and root-cause analysis) and is managed through protocols/guidelines, checklists (e.g., WHO Surgical Safety Checklist), credentialing, and confidential incident-reporting systems feeding a just culture. Clinical errors are also a source of medico-legal risk: reviews of malpractice literature suggest only a minority of adverse events (roughly 1 in 7 by some estimates) ever result in a litigation claim, and the strongest predictors of a patient pursuing litigation are often relational — a poor prior patient–clinician relationship and a feeling of not being kept informed — rather than the objective severity of harm alone, which is why open disclosure and communication after an adverse event are core risk-management strategies, not just an ethical nicety.

Risk management is both proactive (preventing harm before it occurs — hazard identification, checklists, training) and reactive (learning from incidents that already happened — RCA, incident reporting, re-audit) — an organisation that only reacts after harm occurs is perpetually one step behind; a mature system runs both loops continuously and feeds lessons from the reactive loop back into proactive controls.
Worked example: scoring and treating risks on a 5×5 risk matrix ▼

📝 Sample Questions (with model answers)
Practice questions in the style of GKT 516 past-year papers
Q1 Quality & Safety 15 marks
Explain Reason's 'Swiss-cheese' model of system failure and how a just culture supports patient safety.
Marks guide: 15 marks: model 9 · just culture 6
Swiss-cheese model

Defences are layers with 'holes' (latent conditions + active failures). An accident happens only when holes in successive layers align, letting a hazard reach the patient. Most failures are systemic, not solely individual.

Just culture

A just culture distinguishes blameless human error from reckless violation; it encourages reporting and learning rather than punishing, so latent holes are found and fixed. This improves safety more than a blame culture, which suppresses reporting.

Q2 Health Economics 15 marks
Define cost-effectiveness analysis and the ICER, and state why a threshold is needed.
Marks guide: 15 marks: CEA 5 · ICER 5 · threshold 5
CEA

Cost-effectiveness analysis compares alternatives by cost per unit of health effect (e.g., cost per DALY/QALY averted), when outcomes are measured in natural/utility units and programmes differ in both cost and effect.

ICER

Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio = (CostA−CostB)/(EffectA−EffectB) — the extra cost per extra unit of effect of the newer vs comparator option.

Threshold

A willingness-to-pay threshold (e.g., per QALY) decides whether the ICER is 'worth it'; without it, ICERs cannot be judged affordable or equitable.

Q3 UHC 20 marks
Define Universal Health Coverage and discuss the three dimensions policy-makers must balance, with reference to financial risk protection.
Marks guide: 20 marks: definition 5 · dimensions 9 · financial protection 6
Definition

UHC = all people have access to the full range of quality promotive, preventive, curative, rehabilitative and palliative services they need, when and where they need them, without financial hardship.

Three dimensions

(1) Population covered (who); (2) Services covered (what basket); (3) Direct cost covered (how much protected). Expanding one dimension often strains the others.

Financial protection

Pre-payment and risk-pooling (tax/social insurance) replace out-of-pocket payments, preventing catastrophic expenditure and impoverishment — the equity heart of UHC. Malaysia relies on tax-funded public services toward this goal.
