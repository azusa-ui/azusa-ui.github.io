1. Measures of Disease Occurrence
Source: Gordis L. Epidemiology (6th ed.); Porta M. Dictionary of Epidemiology; GKT 502 Course Notes
📊 Introduction

Descriptive epidemiology quantifies when, where and who disease occurs. To compare occurrence across groups we need measures of frequency independent of population size. Two families: measures of frequency (how much disease exists) and measures of association / effect (how strongly an exposure relates to that disease — covered in Section 2).

Key distinction: Prevalence = existing cases (a proportion, a 'snapshot' — numerator not restricted to new events); Incidence = new cases (a rate/risk, requires a defined time interval). Confusing the two is a classic source of error — e.g., a chronic, non-fatal, incurable disease (like well-controlled diabetes) can have low incidence but high prevalence because cases accumulate.
Relationship between prevalence and incidence: in a steady state (stable population, constant incidence and duration), Prevalence ≈ Incidence rate × average Duration of disease (P ≈ IR × D). This means prevalence rises if incidence rises OR if disease duration lengthens (e.g., better survival with treatment, as with HIV on ART) — a rising prevalence does NOT necessarily mean the disease is spreading faster.
🔢 Frequency Measures
  • Count — total number of cases (raw numerator); not comparable across populations of different size.
  • Proportion — numerator is a subset of denominator (ranges 0–1). e.g., case-fatality proportion = deaths from disease / all cases.
  • Ratio — numerator / denominator where the two are NOT necessarily related (e.g., sex ratio).
  • Rate — speed at which events occur over time (events / person-time).
Worked example: prevalence vs incidence ▼

A district of 20,000 adults is surveyed on 1 January (point prevalence survey) and 850 are found to have hypertension. Over the following year, 300 new cases are diagnosed among the 19,150 who were disease-free at baseline.

  • Point prevalence = 850 / 20,000 = 4.25% (existing cases at one moment — no time dimension, so it is a proportion, not a rate).
  • Cumulative incidence (1-year risk) = 300 / 19,150 ≈ 1.57% — note the denominator is restricted to those at risk (disease-free at baseline); prevalent cases are excluded from the incidence denominator.
  • If average duration of hypertension (time from diagnosis to cure/death) is roughly 28 years and incidence rate ≈ 1.6 per 100 person-years, then P ≈ IR × D ≈ 0.016 × 28 ≈ 0.45 (45%) in the long-run steady state — illustrating how a manageable chronic condition accumulates high prevalence despite modest annual incidence.

Common pitfall: 'prevalence rate' is a misnomer — prevalence is a proportion, not a rate, because there is no time dimension.
⏱️ Person-Time & Incidence Rate

Incidence rate (density)

IR = number of new cases / total person-time at risk. Person-time sums each individual's time from entry until event, loss-to-follow-up, or study end. Allows unequal follow-up and late entry.

  • Cumulative incidence (risk) = new cases / population at risk at start; assumes complete follow-up; a proportion (0–1).
  • Incidence rate (density) = new cases / person-time; permits varying observation times.
  • When rare (<10%), cumulative incidence ≈ incidence rate × average follow-up time.
Attack rate is cumulative incidence used in outbreak investigations (e.g., food-borne): cases / exposed persons.
⚖️ Standardization & Crude vs Specific Rates

A crude rate ignores population structure (age, sex). Two populations can have identical age-specific risks yet different crude rates simply because one population is older — comparing crude rates alone can be misleading. Standardization is a set of techniques that removes, as far as possible, the effect of such compositional differences so that two or more populations can be compared fairly.

  • Direct standardization — used when the age-specific rates of the populations being compared are known. Apply each population's own age-specific rates to a common standard (reference) population to obtain an "expected" number of events, then derive an age-adjusted rate.
  • Indirect standardization — used when the age-specific rates of the study population are unknown, unstable (small numbers), or when comparing a small population against a large one. Apply the standard population's age-specific rates to the study population's own age structure, sum the expected events, then compute a Standardized Mortality/Morbidity Ratio (SMR = Observed/Expected × 100).

Choosing direct vs indirect: use direct when age-specific rates for the populations being compared are stable and known (large populations); use indirect when the study population is small (unstable age-specific rates) or those rates are simply unavailable — only its structure and total observed events are needed.
2. Measurement of Risk & Association
Source: GKT 502 Course Notes — Measurement of Risk & Association; Szklo & Nieto
📈 Relative Risk (Risk Ratio)

RR = cumulative incidence in exposed / cumulative incidence in unexposed. Derived from cohort studies where both denominators are known.

RRInterpretation
RR = 1No association
RR > 1Exposure increases risk (risk factor)
RR < 1Exposure decreases risk (protective)
Confidence interval: if the 95% CI for RR includes 1, the association is not statistically significant.
🔁 Odds Ratio (OR)

OR = (a/c) / (b/d) = ad/bc in a 2×2 table. In case-control studies incidence is unknown, so OR is the effect measure. For rare diseases OR ≈ RR.

  • Cohort / cross-sectional: can compute both RR and OR; report RR when the rare-disease assumption fails.
  • Case-control: only OR estimable (sampling on outcome).
Rare-disease assumption: when the outcome is common (>10%), OR overestimates RR. Prefer RR (or prevalence ratio) from cohort/cross-sectional designs.
➖ Risk Difference & Attributable Risk

Risk Difference (RD) / Attributable Risk (AR)

RD = IE − I0 (exposed incidence − unexposed incidence). Absolute measure — the extra risk due to exposure; essential for public-health impact.

Number Needed to Harm (NNH) = 1/RD — how many exposed persons for one additional adverse outcome vs unexposed.
🎯 Attributable Fraction & Population PAR
MeasureFormulaMeaning
Attributable Fraction in exposed (AFe)(RR−1)/RRProportion of exposed cases due to exposure
Population Attributable Risk (PAR)Ipop − I0Excess risk in whole population

PAF depends on both the RR and the exposure prevalence — a strong but rare exposure contributes little at population level; a modest risk factor that is ubiquitous (smoking, hypertension) dominates population burden.
⚠️ Odds vs Probability — do not confuse

Probability (risk) = a/(a+b), bounded 0–1. Odds = a/b, bounded 0–∞. They diverge as the outcome becomes common.

  • Probability 0.5 → odds 1.0
  • Probability 0.1 → odds 0.11 (≈ similar)
  • Probability 0.8 → odds 4.0 (very different)
Misreading OR as RR overstates effect when the outcome is common. Always report which measure you computed.
🧮 Worked Numeric Example — Putting It All Together

A cohort study of 2,000 smokers and 8,000 non-smokers is followed for 10 years for incident ischaemic heart disease (IHD):

Step-by-step calculations ▼
  • Cumulative incidence, exposed (Ie) = 120/2,000 = 0.060 (6.0%)
  • Cumulative incidence, unexposed (I0) = 160/8,000 = 0.020 (2.0%)
  • Risk Ratio (RR) = 0.060/0.020 = 3.0 → smokers have 3× the risk of non-smokers.
  • Odds Ratio (OR) = ad/bc = (120×7,840)/(1,880×160) = 940,800/300,800 ≈ 3.13 — close to RR because IHD, though not rare here, is still relatively uncommon in both groups (rare-disease assumption reasonably holds).
  • Risk Difference / Attributable Risk (RD) = Ie − I0 = 0.060 − 0.020 = 0.040 (4.0 per 100, or 40 per 1,000) — the excess IHD risk directly attributable to smoking among smokers.
  • Number Needed to Harm (NNH) = 1/RD = 1/0.040 = 25 — one additional IHD case for every 25 people who smoke for 10 years (vs not smoking).
  • Attributable Fraction in the exposed (AFe) = (RR−1)/RR = (3.0−1)/3.0 = 0.667 (66.7%) — among smokers who develop IHD, about two-thirds of cases are attributable to smoking; the remaining third would have occurred anyway.
  • If 20% of the source population smokes, Population Attributable Fraction (PAF) ≈ [Pe(RR−1)] / [1 + Pe(RR−1)] = [0.20×2.0] / [1+0.20×2.0] = 0.40/1.40 ≈ 28.6% — roughly 29% of all IHD cases in this population are attributable to smoking and could theoretically be prevented if smoking were eliminated.
Interpretation summary: RR/OR describe the strength of association (aetiological importance); RD/NNH describe the absolute public-health impact on exposed individuals; PAF describes the population-level burden attributable to the exposure — the figure most relevant to policy (e.g., tobacco control budgets).
3. Epidemiological Study Design
Source: GKT 502 Course Notes — Epidemiological Study Design; Gordis
🌳 Design Taxonomy

Studies are broadly observational (no intervention) or experimental (investigator assigns exposure). Observational designs split by whether exposure or outcome is identified first.

  • Cohort — exposure → follow → outcome (temporality explicit).
  • Case-control — outcome → look back → exposure.
  • Cross-sectional — exposure & outcome measured once, simultaneously.
  • Ecological — groups/populations as units.
  • Experimental / RCT — exposure assigned by investigator.
🔬 Cohort Study

Follow a defined exposed and unexposed population over time to compare incidence of outcome. Can be prospective (forward) or retrospective/historical (using records).

  • Strengths: establishes temporality; studies multiple outcomes; computes incidence, RR, AR directly; less recall bias.
  • Limitations: expensive/slow for rare diseases or long latency; loss to follow-up; not ethical for harmful exposures.
  • Measures: Incidence, RR, AR, SMR (historical).
Best for: rare exposures (occupational), common outcomes, aetiology with long follow-up.
🔍 Case-Control Study

Start with cases (diseased) and controls (non-diseased), compare past exposure. Efficient for rare diseases.

  • Strengths: fast, cheap, few subjects; multiple exposures for one outcome; only design for very rare diseases.
  • Limitations: recall & selection bias; cannot compute incidence/RR directly (OR only); temporality inferred.
  • Control selection is critical — must represent the exposure distribution of the source population.
Nested case-control: embedded in a cohort — cases + at-risk controls; gains efficiency while preserving cohort temporality.
📸 Cross-Sectional Study

Exposure and outcome measured at a single point. Estimates prevalence and associations (prevalence OR / prevalence ratio).

  • Strengths: cheap, fast; good for burden/planning; many variables at once.
  • Limitations: cannot establish temporality ('chicken-and-egg'); prevalence odds ≠ incidence odds when chronic; survival bias.
Use for: prevalence surveys, health needs assessment, generating hypotheses.
🌐 Ecological & Experimental Designs

Ecological

Units are groups (countries, states). Cheap, hypothesis-generating, but vulnerable to the ecological fallacy (group-level association ≠ individual-level).

Experimental / RCT

Investigator randomizes exposure. Gold standard for causal inference because randomization (on average, and with adequate sample size) balances both known and unknown confounders across arms.

FeatureObservationalExperimental (RCT)
Exposure assignmentNaturalInvestigator (random)
ConfoundingPresent, must adjustBalanced by randomization
CausalityInferenceStrongest evidence
RCT variants & design-selection guide ▼
  • Parallel-group RCT — each participant receives only one treatment for the whole trial; simplest, most common.
  • Crossover RCT — each participant receives both treatments in sequence (with washout between); each subject acts as their own control, reducing between-person variability, but only suitable for chronic, stable, non-curable conditions (effect must not persist/carry over).
  • Cluster RCT — whole groups (clinics, villages, schools) are randomised rather than individuals; needed when the intervention operates at group level (e.g., a health-education campaign) or contamination between arms is likely; requires larger sample sizes (design effect / intra-cluster correlation).
  • Factorial design — tests two or more interventions simultaneously in one trial (2×2 factorial), efficient when interventions are unlikely to interact.

4. Causal Inference
Source: GKT 502 Course Notes — Causal Inference & Causation; Rothman
🧩 Bradford Hill Criteria

Nine considerations (not a checklist) for judging whether an observed association is likely causal:

1–5: Strength, Consistency, Specificity, Temporality, Biological gradient ▼
  • Strength — strong associations less likely due to confounding (but weak ones can be causal).
  • Consistency — reproduced across studies/populations.
  • Specificity — one exposure → one outcome (weak criterion; many-to-many common).
  • Temporality — cause MUST precede effect (essential, non-negotiable).
  • Biological gradient — dose-response.
6–9: Plausibility, Coherence, Experiment, Analogy ▼
  • Plausibility — biologically credible (knowledge-dependent).
  • Coherence — fits natural history & lab findings.
  • Experiment — evidence from removal/intervention.
  • Analogy — similar established exposures suggest causality.
Sufficient-component cause model ("causal pie", Rothman): disease occurs only when all component causes of at least one "sufficient cause" (pie) are present. A single component present in every pie is a necessary cause (e.g., Mycobacterium tuberculosis for TB) but is rarely sufficient alone — cofactors (crowding, malnutrition, immunosuppression) usually complete the pie. Different individuals may develop the same disease via different pies (multiple sufficient causes, no single "the cause"), which explains why removing just one component cause (e.g., smoking cessation) can prevent disease even without eliminating every possible causal pathway.
⚠️ Bias (Systematic Error)
TypeDefinitionExample / Fix
Selection biasWrong study base / differential inclusion into (or out of) the studyBerkson's bias (hospital controls over-represent certain exposure-disease combos), healthy-worker effect → proper sampling frame, high response rate
Information (measurement) biasMisclassification of exposure/outcomeRecall bias (cases recall past exposure more/less accurately than controls) → biomarkers, blinding, structured records
Interviewer / observer biasData collector probes differently by group knowledge of hypothesis or statusBlind interviewers to case/control status; standardised questionnaires
Hawthorne effectBehaviour changes because subjects know they are observedBlinding; unobtrusive measurement where feasible
ConfoundingThird variable distorts associationAdjust in design (randomisation, restriction, matching) or analysis (stratification, regression)
Non-differential misclassification (error rate similar in both compared groups) biases estimates toward the null (RR/OR closer to 1); differential misclassification (error rate differs by group) can bias the estimate in either direction — toward or away from the null.
🔗 Confounding

A confounder must satisfy three conditions: (1) associated with the exposure, (2) an independent risk factor for the outcome (associated with outcome even among the unexposed), and (3) is not on the causal pathway between exposure and outcome.

  • Control in design: randomization (balances confounders on average), restriction (only enrol one level of the confounder, e.g. non-smokers only), matching (pair exposed/unexposed or case/control on the confounder).
  • Control in analysis: stratification (e.g., Mantel-Haenszel pooled estimate across strata), multivariable regression (logistic/Cox), propensity-score methods.
Worked example — recognising & adjusting for confounding ▼

Crude analysis of coffee drinking and lung cancer shows OR = 2.0. But coffee drinkers in this sample are also more likely to smoke, and smoking independently causes lung cancer. Stratifying by smoking status:

  • Among smokers: OR (coffee–lung cancer) = 1.0 (no association)
  • Among non-smokers: OR (coffee–lung cancer) = 1.0 (no association)

The crude OR of 2.0 was entirely due to confounding by smoking (smoking is associated with coffee drinking AND independently causes lung cancer, and is not a step on any coffee→cancer pathway). The stratum-specific (adjusted, e.g. Mantel-Haenszel) OR of 1.0 is the valid estimate.

A variable is NOT a confounder if it lies on the causal pathway (then it is a mediator — adjusting for it would block part of the true effect and bias the estimate of the total effect toward the null). Distinguishing confounders from mediators requires substantive/biological knowledge, not statistics alone.
🔀 Effect Modification (Interaction)

Effect modification = the effect of exposure differs across levels of a third variable (e.g., smoking modifies asbestos→lung cancer). It is not bias — it is real and reported by stratum.

Confounding ≠ effect modification: confounding is nuisance to remove; effect modification is a finding to describe (report stratified estimates).
🎲 Random & Systematic Error
  • Random error — sampling variability; reduced by larger sample; quantified by confidence intervals / p-values.
  • Systematic error (bias) — pushes all estimates one direction; NOT fixed by sample size.
  • Validity = absence of bias (internal) + generalizability (external).
5. Screening, Demography & Sources of Data
Source: GKT 502 Course Notes — Screening; Demography; Source of Epidemiological Data & Vital Statistics
🩺 Principles of Screening

Screening is the presumptive identification of unrecognised disease (or risk factor) by applying a test to an asymptomatic population. It is NOT diagnosis — a positive screen must be followed by a definitive diagnostic test before treatment. Screening aims to: (1) reduce mortality/morbidity by early treatment, (2) reduce incidence by treating precursors, (3) reduce severity by early effective treatment, or (4) widen choices by detecting risk early in the life-course.

  • Condition should be an important health problem with a recognisable latent/early symptomatic stage and well-understood natural history.
  • Test should be acceptable, reliable, and accurate (adequate sensitivity & specificity) — simple and safe.
  • Facilities for diagnosis & treatment available; agreed policy on whom to treat; treatment more effective when started early; cost of case-finding (including diagnosis and treatment) economically balanced against overall medical expenditure; case-finding should be a continuing process, not a one-off project.
WHO Wilson & Jungner criteria (1968) are the classic 10 principles, commonly grouped under: the condition (important, understood natural history, detectable latent stage), the test (acceptable, valid, reliable), treatment (available, effective, agreed policy), and programme/cost (cost-effective, sustainable, quality-assured). A revised WHO framework (2008) adds: clearly defined objectives, a well-defined target population, evidence of overall programme effectiveness, integration with the health system, informed choice, and ethical conduct (autonomy, equity, confidentiality).
🎯 Diagnostic & Screening Test Validity

A diagnostic test is applied to a symptomatic person to confirm/exclude disease; a screening test is applied to an asymptomatic population, and a positive screen is followed by a diagnostic (confirmatory) test. Both are evaluated against a gold-standard test using a 2×2 table.

Disease + (gold standard)Disease − (gold standard)
Test +TP (a)FP (b)
Test −FN (c)TN (d)
  • Sensitivity = a/(a+c) — proportion of true cases correctly identified as positive (a fixed property of the test; minimises false negatives). High sensitivity is prioritised when missing a case is dangerous/costly (fatal, contagious, treatable).
  • Specificity = d/(b+d) — proportion of true non-cases correctly identified as negative (also fixed). High specificity is prioritised when the confirmatory diagnostic test is invasive, risky, or expensive (to minimise unnecessary false-positive work-up).
  • PPV = a/(a+b) — probability of truly having disease given a positive test. Depends on prevalence, not fixed.
  • NPV = d/(c+d) — probability of truly being disease-free given a negative test. Also prevalence-dependent.
  • Likelihood ratio positive (LR+) = Sensitivity / (1−Specificity) — how much a positive result increases the odds of disease. LR− = (1−Sensitivity)/Specificity — how much a negative result decreases the odds. LRs, unlike PPV/NPV, do not vary with prevalence and can be combined with pre-test odds (Bayes' theorem) to get post-test odds.
Worked example — Sens/Spec/PPV/NPV/LR calculation ▼

A screening test with sensitivity 90% and specificity 95% is applied to a population of 10,000 with disease prevalence 2% (200 diseased, 9,800 healthy).

  • TP = 90% × 200 = 180; FN = 200 − 180 = 20
  • TN = 95% × 9,800 = 9,310; FP = 9,800 − 9,310 = 490
  • PPV = 180/(180+490) = 180/670 ≈ 26.9% — only about 1 in 4 people who test positive truly has the disease.
  • NPV = 9,310/(9,310+20) = 9,310/9,330 ≈ 99.8% — a negative result is highly reassuring at this (low) prevalence.
  • LR+ = 0.90/(1−0.95) = 0.90/0.05 = 18 (a positive result substantially raises the probability of disease). LR− = (1−0.90)/0.95 = 0.10/0.95 ≈ 0.105 (a negative result substantially lowers the probability of disease).
If the same test is applied only to a high-risk subgroup with prevalence 20% (2,000/10,000): TP=1,800, FN=200, FP = 80%×8,000=... i.e. TN=95%×8,000=7,600, FP=400. PPV = 1,800/(1,800+400) = 81.8% — dramatically higher. Sensitivity and specificity stay fixed, but PPV rises steeply with prevalence (targeting screening at high-risk groups is a key strategy to improve programme efficiency).
Prevalence matters: at low prevalence, even a highly specific test yields many false positives relative to true positives → low PPV. This is why universal screening of rare conditions is inefficient and risks overwhelming confirmatory/diagnostic services; targeting high-risk groups (raising the pre-test prevalence) is the standard mitigation.
Choosing the cut-off point for a continuous test result: moving the cut-off to increase sensitivity always decreases specificity, and vice versa (trade-off, visualised with a ROC curve, plotting sensitivity vs 1−specificity across cut-offs — area under the ROC curve, AUC, summarises overall discriminative ability). Set a low/lenient cut-off (favouring sensitivity) when missing a case is dangerous; set a high/strict cut-off (favouring specificity) when the confirmatory test is invasive/expensive or false positives cause serious harm/anxiety.
⏳ Lead-Time & Length & Overdiagnosis Biases
  • Lead-time bias — early detection makes survival appear longer even if death unchanged (clock starts earlier).
  • Length-time bias — screening favours slow-progressing (indolent) cases, which survive longer anyway.
  • Overdiagnosis — detecting lesions that would never harm in a lifetime.
Use randomised trials with mortality endpoints (not 5-year survival) to prove screening saves lives.
👥 Demography

Demography (Greek: demos = people, graphein = to describe) is the scientific study of human populations — their size, density, composition/structure, and how these change through the interaction of three components: fertility, mortality, and migration. Demographic data underpin epidemiology by providing denominators (population at risk) and by shaping disease burden through age structure.

  • Population size — total number of individuals in a defined area; estimated via census (complete count) or sampling with extrapolation.
  • Population density — population per unit area; rises with births/in-migration, falls with deaths/out-migration.
  • Population composition — description by age, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic status; commonly visualised with a population pyramid.

Population pyramids & demographic transition ▼

A population pyramid plots age groups (y-axis) against the proportion of males/females (x-axis). Typical stages of demographic transition:

  • Expansive/young (high birth, high death) — broad base, rapid fall up the pyramid, short life expectancy — typical of pre-transition, high-fertility settings.
  • Early transition — high birth rate persists but death rates fall (especially infant/child mortality) → wide base, slightly longer life expectancy, rapid population growth.
  • Late transition — declining birth rate, low death rate → pyramid narrows at the base, more people surviving to older age.
  • Constrictive/ageing (low birth, low death) — narrow base, bulging middle/upper age bands, higher old-age dependency ratio, longer life expectancy — the direction many middle- and high-income countries (including Malaysia) are moving toward.

Life tables summarise age-specific mortality/survival probabilities for a hypothetical cohort; a period life table uses current age-specific death rates (cross-sectional), while a cohort life table follows an actual birth cohort over its lifetime. Life tables generate life expectancy at birth (e₀) and at other ages — key summary indicators of population health used in national vital-statistics reporting (e.g., Malaysia's Abridged Life Tables).

An ageing population (rising proportion aged 65+) increases the old-age dependency ratio and shifts disease burden toward chronic non-communicable diseases — a major driver of health-system planning in Malaysia and the region.
🗂️ Sources of Epidemiological Data & Vital Statistics
  • Vital statistics: births, deaths, marriages (civil registration).
  • Disease registries: cancer, congenital anomalies.

6. Applied Epidemiology, Causation & Evidence-Based PH
Source: GKT 502 Course Notes — Epidemiology in PH; Evidence-Based PH; Model of Disease Causation
🧬 Models of Disease Causation
Web of Causation & Multicausal Model ▼

Disease arises from interplay of multiple factors (agent, host, environment) — not single linear cause. The 'web of causation' shows interlocking antecedents.

Rose's Prevention Paradox ▼

A preventive measure that benefits the population may offer little to the individual at high risk. Small shifts in a risk factor across the whole population can yield more cases averted than targeting high-risk groups alone.

Socio-ecological / Social Model ▼

Causal factors operate at intrapersonal, interpersonal, organisational, community & policy levels — central to public-health intervention design.

Agent–Host–Environment (triad) remains the foundational framework for communicable & many non-communicable conditions.
🩹 Natural History of Disease & Levels of Prevention

The natural history of disease is the progression of a disease process from onset to resolution (recovery, disability, or death) in the absence of intervention. It has two broad phases: the pre-pathogenesis phase (susceptibility — before disease has begun, when host, agent and environmental factors interact) and the pathogenesis phase (from earliest sub-cellular changes through subclinical/latent disease, to symptomatic clinical disease, then recovery, disability, or death). Understanding this progression identifies where interventions can act, and defines the three (or four) classic levels of prevention (Leavell & Clark, 1965).

Primordial & Primary Prevention ▼

Primordial prevention (a modern addition) targets the underlying social, economic and environmental conditions that give rise to risk factors in the first place (e.g., urban planning that enables active transport, preventing the emergence of a sedentary-lifestyle risk factor at population level).

Primary prevention acts in the pre-pathogenesis phase, keeping the disease process from becoming established by eliminating causes or increasing host resistance. Two categories:

  • Health promotion — enabling people to increase control over the determinants of health (health education, nutrition, lifestyle/behavioural change, environmental improvement) — a population and community-level responsibility.
  • Specific protection — immunisation (e.g., measles, hepatitis B), chemoprophylaxis (e.g., antimalarials for travellers, isoniazid for TB contacts), environmental modification (water fluoridation/chlorination, vector control), and injury prevention (seatbelts, helmets, workplace safety regulation).
Secondary Prevention ▼

Acts during the early/latent (subclinical) part of the pathogenesis phase — reduces the consequences of disease through early diagnosis and prompt treatment (this is exactly where screening fits, see Section 5). It applies only where the natural history includes a detectable early stage that responds better to treatment than a later stage. Aims: prevent spread to others, cure at earliest stage, prevent complications/disability, and reduce/reverse transmission.

Tertiary Prevention ▼

Acts once disease is symptomatic/clinical — limits physical and social consequences and improves survival/quality of life through: disability limitation (halting the disease process, preventing/limiting impairment) and rehabilitation (medical, social, psychological, economic, vocational) — restoring the person to a useful, self-sufficient role in society.

Stage of diseaseLevel of preventionTypical response
Pre-disease (susceptibility)Primordial / PrimaryHealth promotion, specific protection

Recognising which phase of the natural history an intervention targets clarifies programme design: vaccination campaigns are primary prevention, mammography/Pap-smear screening programmes are secondary prevention, and stroke rehabilitation services are tertiary prevention.

📚 Evidence-Based Public Health (EBPH)

EBPH = conscientious use of the best available scientific evidence, combined with population data, and community preference, in decision-making.

  1. Ask an answerable question (PICO).
  2. Acquire the evidence (systematic search).
  3. Appraise for validity & applicability.
  4. Apply to the local context.
  5. Assess the outcome / evaluate.
Levels of evidence: RCT > cohort > case-control > cross-sectional > ecologic > expert opinion. But study design alone is insufficient — appraisal of bias & applicability matters.
📡 Public Health Surveillance

Surveillance is the systematic, ongoing collection, analysis, interpretation, and dissemination of health data essential to planning, implementing and evaluating public-health action, closely integrated with timely dissemination to those who need it. Distinct from research — surveillance is oriented to action/control, not hypothesis-testing.

Surveillance system attributes (evaluation criteria) ▼
  • Simplicity — simple structure (database, notification process) and operation (clear case definitions, easy case verification).
  • Flexibility — can adapt to new diseases, changing case definitions, or operating conditions with minimal extra cost.
  • Acceptability — willingness of reporters/participants to provide accurate, complete, timely data.
  • Sensitivity — ability to detect true cases and true epidemics.
  • Representativeness — accurately describes the occurrence of a health event over time, place and person.
  • Timeliness — data available fast enough to allow effective control action.
  • Also considered: predictive value positive, data quality, and stability of the system.
  • Other types: syndromic surveillance (monitors symptom clusters/proxy indicators before confirmed diagnosis, useful for early outbreak signal), event-based surveillance (captures unstructured signals — media, rumours, community reports).
  • Functions: early outbreak/epidemic detection, monitoring trends and disease burden, guiding resource allocation, and evaluating control programmes.
  • Malaysia: mandatory notification of scheduled infectious diseases to the Ministry of Health (e.g., via the national notification system), supplemented by sentinel sites (e.g., for influenza-like illness) and routine health management information systems.

Practice questions in the style of GKT 502 past-year papers
Q1 Measures of Occurrence 15 marks
A cohort of 4,000 workers is followed for 5 years. 80 new cases of disease X occur among 1,000 exposed workers, and 120 among 3,000 unexposed. (a) Calculate the cumulative incidence in exposed and unexposed. (b) Calculate the risk ratio and interpret it. (c) Name one limitation of cumulative incidence here.
Marks guide: 15 marks: (a) 4 marks · (b) 6 marks · (c) 5 marks for discussion
Answer (a) — Cumulative incidence

CIexposed = 80/1,000 = 0.08 (8%). CIunexposed = 120/3,000 = 0.04 (4%).

Answer (b) — Risk ratio

RR = 0.08 / 0.04 = 2.0. Exposed workers have twice the risk of disease X versus unexposed. A 95% CI excluding 1 would indicate significance.

Answer (c) — Limitation

Cumulative incidence assumes complete follow-up — with 5 years of observation, loss-to-follow-up is likely and would bias estimates. An incidence rate (person-time) would better handle unequal follow-up. It is also a proportion bounded 0–1, so it cannot exceed 1 even with very high risk.

Q2 Study Design 20 marks
You want to study whether a rare occupational solvent exposure causes leukaemia. (a) Name the most efficient study design and justify. (b) Describe how you would select controls. (c) State two key biases and how to minimise them.
Marks guide: 20 marks: design 5 · controls 5 · 2 biases + mitigation 10
Answer (a)

Case-control study — leukaemia is rare, so a cohort would need an enormous population and long follow-up. Case-control is efficient: sample cases (leukaemia patients) and compare solvent exposure history to controls.

Answer (b) — Control selection

Controls must represent the exposure experience of the source population that produced the cases (same hospitals/region, similar age/sex). Avoid controls whose exposure is itself disease-related. Consider a nested case-control within an existing cohort for efficiency.

Answer (c) — Biases
  • Recall bias — cases may over-report past solvent exposure; mitigate with records/biomarkers and blind interviewers.
  • Selection bias — controls not from same study base; mitigate with rigorous sampling frame & response maximisation.
Q3 Causation & Screening 20 marks
(a) Explain why temporality is the non-negotiable Bradford Hill criterion. (b) A new screening test has sensitivity 90% and specificity 95% in a disease with 1% prevalence. Calculate the PPV and explain its public-health implication.
Marks guide: 20 marks: temporality 8 · PPV calc 6 · implication 6
Answer (a)

For X to cause Y, X must occur before Y. Without temporality the direction of causation is indeterminate (the observed association could be reverse). All other Hill criteria can be satisfied by a non-causal or reverse-causal relation, so temporality is the only essential precondition for a causal claim.

Answer (b) — PPV

Per 10,000 people: 100 diseased, 9,900 healthy. TP = 90, FP = 5% × 9,900 = 495. PPV = 90/(90+495) = 15.4%. Only ~1 in 6 positive results is a true case.

Answer (b) — Implication

At low prevalence, even a highly specific test yields mostly false positives → low PPV. Universal screening here would overwhelm confirmatory services and cause harm from unnecessary invasive follow-up. Target high-risk groups to raise prevalence and PPV.

Q4 Standardization of Rates 15 marks
Two generic districts report crude death rates of 17.4 and 24.6 per 1,000 population respectively. (a) Explain why comparing these crude rates directly could be misleading. (b) Describe the steps of direct standardization. (c) When would you use indirect standardization instead, and how is the resulting statistic interpreted?
Marks guide: 15 marks: (a) 4 marks · (b) 6 marks · (c) 5 marks
Answer (a)

Crude rates are heavily influenced by the age (and sex) structure of each population. If one district has an older population, it will have a higher crude death rate even if its age-specific mortality risk is actually equal to or lower than the other district's. The comparison confounds true underlying risk with demographic composition.

Answer (b) — Direct standardization steps
  1. Calculate age-specific mortality rates for each population.
  2. Select a standard (reference) population structure.
  3. Apply each population's own age-specific rates to the standard population's age bands to calculate expected deaths per age band.
  4. Sum expected deaths and divide by the total standard population, ×1,000, to obtain the age-adjusted rate for each population.
  5. Compare the age-adjusted rates (not the crude rates).
Answer (c) — Indirect standardization

Use indirect standardization when the study population's own age-specific rates are unknown or unstable (e.g., a small population with few deaths in some age bands), but its age structure and total observed deaths are known. Apply a standard population's age-specific rates to the study population's age structure to calculate expected deaths, then compute the Standardized Mortality Ratio, SMR = (Observed/Expected) × 100. An SMR above 100 indicates more deaths occurred than would be expected if the study population had experienced the standard population's mortality experience (worse than reference); below 100 indicates fewer than expected (better than reference).
