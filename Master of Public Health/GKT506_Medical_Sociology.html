1. Sociological Imagination
Source: C. Wright Mills (1959) The Sociological Imagination; Durkheim (1897) Suicide; Berger (1963) Invitation to Sociology; Germov (ed.) Second Opinion
🌀 C. Wright Mills & the Concept

The sociological imagination is the capacity to shift from one perspective to another — from the personal to the historical, the private to the public — and to see the relation between individual biography and the wider society. Mills was writing partly as a critique of two dominant strands in 1950s American sociology: grand theory (abstract, disconnected from empirical reality) and abstracted empiricism (data-collection with no theoretical imagination). He wanted a sociology that connected lived experience to social structure.

Mills argued the sociological imagination enables a person to ask three linked questions of any society: (1) What is the structure of this particular society — its essential components and how they relate? (2) Where does this society stand in human history, and what are the mechanics by which it is changing? (3) What varieties of men and women prevail in this society and this period, and what is happening to them?

Core idea: "Neither the life of an individual nor the history of a society can be understood without understanding both." Health and illness are not purely biological — they are shaped by social context, and the imagination is the tool that reveals that shaping.
🔎 Deep dive: why this matters for public health practice ▼

Clinical medicine is trained to look for proximate, individual-level causes (a pathogen, a genetic variant, a personal behaviour). The sociological imagination is the discipline of also asking about distal, structural causes — housing, income, labour markets, racism, colonial history, food systems. Geoffrey Rose's distinction between the "causes of cases" (why did this person get sick) and the "causes of incidence" (why does this population have this rate of sickness) is the epidemiological cousin of Mills's trouble/issue distinction — both push the analyst upstream from the individual to the population determinants.

Practically: a clinician who only ever prescribes for hypertension is treating a "trouble." A public health practitioner who also asks why salt intake, stress, and access to fresh food are so patterned by neighbourhood and income is exercising the sociological imagination and can design population-level, "issue"-level interventions (salt-reduction policy, urban planning, income support).

⚖️ Personal Troubles vs Public Issues
Personal troublePublic issue
Private problem of individual, located within the character of the individual and their immediate relationsPublic matters of social structure, located in the organisation of institutions and the historical period
Blamed on personal failings ("victim blaming")Requires structural explanation and structural remedy
e.g., one person's obesity, one worker's unemploymente.g., population obesity epidemic & obesogenic environment; a national unemployment rate of 12%
Resolved case-by-case, clinicallyResolved through policy, regulation, redistribution
Medical sociology reframes "troubles" (individual, medical) as "issues" (population, social) — the foundation of social medicine and public health. This is not an either/or: both levels of explanation are usually true simultaneously, and good practice holds both at once.
Worked example

Mills's classic illustration: if only one person in a city of 100,000 is unemployed, that is his personal trouble — we look to his character, skills and motivation. But if 12,000 of the city's 100,000 workers are unemployed (an unemployment rate of 12%), that is a public issue — the remedy lies not in retraining one man but in the structure of opportunities, the economy and the labour market.

Public-health translation: if adult obesity prevalence in a country rises from 15% to 30% of the adult population over 20 years, calculated simply as prevalence = obese adults ÷ total adults × 100, that doubling cannot plausibly be explained by 15 percentage points' worth of individuals independently losing willpower. The explanation must be sought upstream — in food environments, portion sizes, urban design that discourages walking, marketing of ultra-processed food, and working hours — i.e. it is a public issue requiring population-level (fiscal, regulatory, environmental) intervention, not only individual clinical counselling.

🔭 Levels of Sociological Analysis
  • Micro: illness experience, doctor–patient interaction, health beliefs, everyday meaning-making (symbolic interactionism, phenomenology).
  • Meso: organisations, professions, hospitals, clinics, social networks, local communities.
  • Macro: social class, gender, ethnicity, globalisation, the state, capitalism, colonial/post-colonial history (structural functionalism, conflict/Marxist theory, feminist theory).
A complete account of any health problem needs all three levels — a purely micro account risks psychologising structural problems; a purely macro account risks ignoring lived agency and meaning.
Worked example: Type 2 diabetes across three levels

Micro: a patient's daily choices about diet and medication adherence, their explanatory model of "sugar sickness," their relationship with their treating clinician.

Meso: whether their workplace allows meal breaks and rest, whether their neighbourhood has a primary-care clinic with continuity of care, whether their extended family and religious community provide support or pressure around food.

Macro: national food policy (sugar taxation, subsidies on staple oils/rice), trade liberalisation that increased availability of cheap processed food, urbanisation reducing incidental physical activity, and the historical political economy that shaped income distribution in the first place.

📊 Durkheim's Suicide (1897): the first "sociological imagination" study

Before Mills coined the term, Émile Durkheim demonstrated the method. Suicide looks like the most private, individual act imaginable — yet Durkheim showed that suicide rates are stable, patterned social facts that vary systematically with the degree of social integration (how attached individuals are to social groups) and social regulation (how much norms constrain individual desires) — not with individual psychology alone.

TypeMechanismExample context
EgoisticToo little integration — the individual is insufficiently bound to societyDurkheim found unmarried and Protestant populations (weaker communal ties than Catholic) had higher rates
AltruisticToo much integration — the self is submerged in the groupMilitary self-sacrifice; some collective/cult suicides
AnomicToo little regulation — norms breakdown, often during rapid social/economic changeRates rise in both economic booms and sudden depressions
FatalisticToo much regulation — excessive oppressive control (a minor category Durkheim mentioned in a footnote)Highly oppressive institutional settings
🧮 Worked calculation: computing a suicide (mortality) rate ▼

The standard formula, still used in vital statistics and epidemiology today:

Rate per 100,000 = (number of suicide deaths in a period ÷ mid-year population at risk) × 100,000

Worked example (illustrative figures): Suppose a country records 1,200 suicide deaths in a calendar year, with a mid-year population of 32,000,000.

Rate = (1,200 ÷ 32,000,000) × 100,000 = 3.75 per 100,000 population

Durkheim used exactly this kind of rate, disaggregated by religion, marital status, and country, to argue that the variation between groups (e.g., a rate several times higher among Protestants than Catholics in the countries he studied) could not be explained by individual psychiatric predisposition alone, since psychiatric morbidity did not vary in step with the rate — it had to be explained sociologically, by differing levels of social integration in Protestant vs. Catholic communal life.

Public-health relevance: the same logic underlies contemporary suicide-prevention epidemiology, which tracks rates by sex, age, occupation and region to identify structurally at-risk groups (e.g., farmers, indigenous communities, LGBTQ+ youth) rather than treating each death purely as an individual clinical failure.

🕵️ Berger: The Sociologist's Perspective

Peter Berger (Invitation to Sociology, 1963) described the sociological consciousness through several linked motifs that complement Mills:

  • Debunking motif: official, taken-for-granted explanations of social arrangements are not necessarily the real ones — sociology looks behind the facade (e.g., "compliance" framing in medicine masks unequal power in the consultation).
  • Unrespectability: sociology is curious about all levels of society, not just the respectable and official, including stigmatised illness experience.
  • Relativising perspective: what looks natural or inevitable (a diagnostic category, a health behaviour norm) is often historically and culturally specific.
  • Seeing the general in the particular: an individual clinical encounter can reveal a general pattern (e.g., a single case of delayed cancer presentation reveals a pattern of health-seeking delay across a whole social group).
Together, Mills, Durkheim and Berger provide the conceptual toolkit that justifies why medical sociology exists as a discipline distinct from clinical medicine and psychology: illness is simultaneously biological, experiential and structural.
🏗️ Social Structure — the "structure" in structural explanation

Every claim in this module that health is shaped by "structural" forces rests on the concept of social structure: the patterned, relatively durable arrangements — institutions, roles, norms, networks, stratification — that both emerge from and shape individual action. Structure can be analysed at the same three levels introduced above: on the macro scale it is the class structure, the state, and major institutions; on the meso scale it is the structure of organisations and social networks; on the micro scale it is the way internalised norms shape individual behaviour.

TheoristContribution to structural theory
MarxThe economic "base" (mode of production, ownership of the means of production) substantially determines the cultural and political "superstructure" of a society — social class position is therefore a root structural determinant of life chances, including health.
DurkheimDistinguished mechanical solidarity (cohesion through shared culture in simple, undifferentiated societies) from organic solidarity (cohesion through interdependence and division of labour in complex, differentiated societies) — both are structural bases of social integration, which he linked directly to suicide rates (see above).
ParsonsStructural-functionalism: social structures (including the sick role) exist because they perform necessary functions for the stability of the whole social system.
GiddensStructuration theory: structure and individual agency cannot be separated — structures are simultaneously the medium and the outcome of the practices they organise; actors draw on rules and resources that, in the aggregate, they also reproduce or change.
BourdieuPractice theory: structure is embedded in individuals as habitus (durable, internalised dispositions) and expressed through different forms of capital (economic, cultural, social — see Module 4) that are unevenly distributed and reproduce class advantage across generations.
The recurring theoretical tension in medical sociology is the structure–agency debate: how much of a health outcome (e.g., smoking, treatment adherence, vaccine uptake) should be explained by individual choice/agency versus by the structures (poverty, marketing, education, the built environment) that make some choices easier than others? Most contemporary medical sociology, following Giddens and Bourdieu, treats this as a false dichotomy — agency is always structured, and structures are always reproduced or altered through agency.
2. Social Determinants of Health
Source: WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health (2008); Marmot Review (2010, "Fair Society, Healthy Lives"); Whitehall Studies; Link & Phelan (1995); Dahlgren & Whitehead (1991)
🌍 Definition & Dahlgren–Whitehead Model

Social determinants of health (SDH) are "the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age" — shaped by the distribution of money, power and resources at global, national and local levels (WHO CSDH, 2008).

  • Structural drivers: governance, macroeconomic policy, social policies (labour market, welfare state, education), culture and societal values — these determine the socioeconomic position a person occupies.
  • Intermediary determinants: material circumstances (housing, income, neighbourhood), psychosocial factors (stress, social support, control over one's life), behavioural/biological factors (diet, smoking, physical activity — themselves socially patterned), and the health system itself.
  • Dahlgren–Whitehead "rainbow" (1991): concentric layers around the individual — (i) age, sex, constitutional factors (fixed) → (ii) individual lifestyle factors → (iii) social & community networks → (iv) living & working conditions (agriculture/food production, education, work environment, unemployment, water & sanitation, health care services, housing) → (v) general socioeconomic, cultural & environmental conditions.
WHO CSDH (Marmot, 2008): "Social injustice is killing on a grand scale." Action on SDH is framed as an ethical imperative and a matter of social justice, not just technical health-system efficiency.
Worked example: mapping one exposure through the rainbow

Childhood asthma exacerbation: (i) genetic atopic predisposition (fixed layer); (ii) individual factors — a caregiver who smokes indoors; (iii) social network — extended family norms around smoking; (iv) living conditions — damp, mould-prone rental housing near a major road (traffic air pollution), lack of paid sick leave preventing follow-up clinic visits; (v) macro layer — housing policy, urban planning and air-quality regulation. A purely biomedical inhaler prescription addresses none of layers (iii)–(v).

📉 The Social Gradient

Health inequalities follow a gradient: each step down the socioeconomic ladder is associated with worse health. Critically, it is not only the poor who suffer — even among comfortably-off groups, lower relative rank is associated with worse outcomes than higher rank.

  • Absolute income/material — direct effect of material living standards (nutrition, housing quality, exposure to hazards).
  • Relative income/status — the psychosocial effect of income inequality and social rank itself (Wilkinson & Pickett's "status anxiety" / relative deprivation hypothesis), operating partly through chronic stress and the HPA axis.
  • Life expectancy gaps of 7–10+ years are commonly documented between the richest and poorest areas within the same country; healthy life expectancy gaps are typically larger still.
The gradient is not explained by health-care access alone — it reflects the cumulative effect of living and working conditions across the whole life course, including before birth (the "life-course" and "developmental origins" perspectives).

The Whitehall Studies (Marmot et al.)

The Whitehall I (1967) and Whitehall II (1985 onward) studies followed British civil servants — none of whom were poor by absolute standards, and all of whom had equal access to the NHS — and still found a strong inverse gradient between employment grade and mortality/morbidity. Men in the lowest employment grade had roughly a 3–4 times higher risk of coronary heart disease mortality than men in the highest administrative grade, even after adjusting for classic risk factors (smoking, cholesterol, blood pressure), which explained only part of the gap. This was pivotal evidence that relative position, job control ("demand–control" model, Karasek) and psychosocial stress matter independently of material deprivation and health-care access.

🧮 Worked calculation: measuring the gradient (Relative Index of Inequality & a Risk Ratio) ▼

1. Simple risk ratio across socioeconomic quintiles. Suppose annual mortality rates by household-income quintile (illustrative) are:

QuintileMortality rate (per 1,000/yr)
Q1 (poorest)12.0
Q5 (richest)4.0

Risk ratio (RR) = rate in Q1 ÷ rate in Q5 = 12.0 ÷ 4.0 = 3.0

Interpretation: mortality risk in the poorest quintile is 3 times that of the richest quintile — a relative measure of inequality.

2. Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and Relative Index of Inequality (RII). The RR above only compares the two extreme groups and ignores the gradient in between. Epidemiologists instead rank the whole population by socioeconomic position (0 = most disadvantaged, 1 = most advantaged) and fit a regression of the health outcome against that rank. The SII is the absolute difference in the outcome predicted between the very bottom and very top of that ranking (the slope of the regression line); the RII expresses this as a ratio rather than a difference, and — unlike a simple top-vs-bottom ratio — both SII and RII use information from every group along the gradient, which is why they are preferred in the SDH literature (Mackenbach & Kunst).

Why this matters for a written answer: being able to say "the gradient was measured using the RII, not simply the richest-vs-poorest ratio, because the RII captures the full continuous social gradient rather than just the extremes" is a marker of a strong answer.

🚧 Health Inequalities vs Inequities

Inequalities = measurable differences in health across groups (a descriptive, empirical fact). Inequities = differences that are unfair, avoidable and socially produced — the proper normative target of policy (Whitehead, 1992: a difference is inequitable if it is "avoidable, unnecessary and unfair").

  • Patterned by class, gender, ethnicity, geography, disability, migration status, indigeneity.
  • Malaysia: documented urban–rural gaps in maternal and child health indicators; B40 (bottom 40% income) vs T20 (top 20%) disparities in NCD risk-factor prevalence and catastrophic health spending; long-standing disparities affecting Orang Asli and indigenous communities in Sabah/Sarawak in access to clean water, skilled birth attendance and under-five mortality.
  • Addressed via proportionate universalism (Marmot Review, 2010) — universal action across the whole gradient, but with effort and resourcing scaled proportionately to the level of disadvantage, avoiding both the stigma of narrowly targeted programmes and the under-reach of purely universal ones.

Marmot Review (2010) — six policy objectives

  1. Give every child the best start in life.
  2. Enable all children, young people and adults to maximise their capabilities and have control over their lives.
  3. Create fair employment and good work for all.
  4. Ensure a healthy standard of living for all.
  5. Create and develop healthy and sustainable places and communities.
  6. Strengthen the role and impact of ill-health prevention.
🧩 Fundamental Cause Theory (Link & Phelan, 1995)

Link and Phelan argue that socioeconomic status (SES) is a "fundamental cause" of disease because it shapes access to flexible resources — money, knowledge, prestige, power and beneficial social connections — that can be redeployed to avoid risks or minimise the consequences of disease whatever the currently dominant mechanism of risk happens to be.

Prediction of the theory: even when a specific risk factor is eliminated or a specific disease becomes fully treatable, the SES gradient re-emerges around whatever new mechanism of risk or new treatment becomes available — because the advantaged are best placed to exploit new knowledge and new resources first. This explains why the social gradient in health has persisted across radically different disease profiles and medical eras (from infectious disease before antibiotics, to cardiovascular disease, to COVID-19 vaccine uptake), even though the proximate cause of death has changed completely.
3. Culture & Health
Source: Kleinman (1980) Patients and Healers in the Context of Culture; Helman Culture, Health and Illness; Parsons (1951) The Social System; Goffman (1963) Stigma; Rosenstock (Health Belief Model); Zola/Conrad (medicalisation)
🪄 Culture & the Meaning of Illness

Culture shapes how symptoms are interpreted, whether care is sought, and from whom. Clinical reality is co-constructed between lay and professional systems, not simply "discovered" by biomedical testing alone.

Medical sociologists distinguish a three-part vocabulary:

  • Disease = the biological/pathological reality as defined by biomedicine (etic — an outsider's, professional perspective); measurable via pathology, imaging, biomarkers.
  • Illness = the personally and socially experienced phenomenon — how the person feels, interprets and communicates the problem (emic — an insider's, lay perspective).
  • Sickness (Twaddle; Young) = the social role and social process around being unwell — how a community and its institutions (workplace, insurer, state) recognise and respond to the person's condition (e.g., certified sick leave).
Kleinman's distinction (1980): treat the illness, not just the disease — a patient can have illness without demonstrable disease (e.g., medically unexplained symptoms) and can have disease without illness (e.g., asymptomatic hypertension, silent early-stage cancer detected on screening).

Stigma (Goffman, 1963)

Stigma is a deeply discrediting attribute that reduces a person "from a whole and usual person to a tainted, discounted one." Goffman distinguished:

  • Discredited stigma: the condition is already visible or known (e.g., a visible skin condition, a physical disability).
  • Discreditable stigma: the condition is concealable, and the central challenge is information management — whether, when and to whom to disclose (e.g., HIV status, a mental health diagnosis, past addiction).
  • Courtesy stigma: stigma that spreads to people associated with the stigmatised person (e.g., family members of someone with mental illness or leprosy).
Historically stigmatised conditions in many settings include leprosy, tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS and severe mental illness; stigma is a major structural barrier to care-seeking and is itself a legitimate target of public health intervention (anti-stigma campaigns, contact-based education, legal protection).
🧠 Explanatory Models (Kleinman)

Every actor in a clinical encounter (patient, family, practitioner) holds an explanatory model of an episode of sickness, covering five domains: aetiology (cause), timing/onset, pathophysiology (how it works in the body), course of illness (severity, expected trajectory), and appropriate treatment.

Kleinman's eight questions to elicit a patient's explanatory model

  1. What do you call this problem?
  2. What do you think has caused it?
  3. Why do you think it started when it did?
  4. What do you think the sickness does — how does it work?
  5. How severe is it? Will it have a short or long course?
  6. What kind of treatment do you think you should receive?
  7. What are the chief problems the sickness has caused for you?
  8. What do you fear most about the sickness?
Illustrative example

In several South-East Asian and South Asian lay traditions, illness may be partly explained through a humoral "hot–cold" balance framework, or through concepts such as excess "wind" (angin) causing bloating, weakness or stroke-like symptoms — frameworks that coexist with, rather than simply being replaced by, biomedical explanations. A patient may accept a biomedical diagnosis of hypertension while simultaneously attributing an acute exacerbation to having eaten too many "heaty" foods or having been exposed to wind/draught after bathing. A clinician who dismisses this as mere ignorance risks losing the patient's trust and adherence; one who elicits it using Kleinman's questions can negotiate a shared plan (e.g., accepting dietary modification framed in the patient's own hot–cold vocabulary alongside antihypertensive medication).

Aligning explanatory models — not necessarily agreeing with them, but understanding and respectfully negotiating with them — improves communication, trust and adherence far more reliably than simply repeating biomedical information more forcefully ("knowledge-deficit" model of non-adherence is usually wrong).
🚶 Illness Behaviour & Lay Referral
  • Illness behaviour (Mechanic, 1962): the way symptoms are differentially perceived, evaluated and acted (or not acted) upon by different people — shaped by prior experience, cultural background, and the meaning attached to symptoms, not by symptom severity alone.
  • Lay referral system (Freidson): before consulting a professional, people typically consult a lay network — family, friends, traditional healers, online communities — who filter, validate or delay the decision to seek formal care.
  • Stages of delay (Safer, Andersen model of total patient delay): appraisal delay (recognising a symptom as significant), illness delay (deciding one is ill and needs care), behavioural/utilisation delay (deciding to seek professional help), scheduling delay (time to get an appointment), and treatment delay (time from first professional contact to actual treatment) — cumulatively relevant to late presentation in cancer and TB.

Parsons's Sick Role (1951)

Talcott Parsons, from a structural-functionalist perspective, described illness as a form of deviance that must be socially regulated through the "sick role," which grants two rights conditional on two obligations:

RightsObligations
Exemption from normal social role responsibilitiesThe sick person must want to get well
Not held personally responsible for their condition; right to be taken care ofMust seek competent (professional) help and cooperate with treatment

Parsons, writing from a structural-functionalist perspective, treated illness as a form of sanctioned deviance: an unwell person is temporarily unable to perform their normal productive social functions, which — left unregulated — would threaten social order, so society "polices" this deviance through the medical profession, which alone has legitimate authority to certify entry into and exit from the sick role. Parsons distinguished three versions of the role: conditionally legitimate (the everyday case — rights granted provided the two obligations are met), unconditionally legitimate (irreversible conditions, e.g. some disabilities or terminal illness, where the "must want to get well" obligation is waived), and illegitimate (conditions that are stigmatised by others, e.g. historically some sexually transmitted infections or addiction, where society is reluctant to grant full sick-role rights at all).

Critique: the sick role model fits acute, short-term illness reasonably well but breaks down for chronic illness (where "getting well" is not achievable, and the exemption from social roles is not sustainable or even desired long-term), for stigmatised conditions (where the "not responsible" clause is contested, e.g., substance use or obesity), and cross-culturally (the model assumes a Western biomedical authority structure and a voluntary, compliant patient). It also assumes the individual accepts the role — in practice many people resist dependency, refuse to relinquish social obligations, or avoid the public sick role altogether if the illness is stigmatised.

Why the sick role fell out of favour

By the 1990s, most medical sociologists had moved away from Parsons's functionalist model. Two intellectual shifts drove this (Burnham, 2014): first, the rise of labelling theory, which reframed illness — especially mental illness and stigmatised deviance — as a social construction used to mark certain people as inferior, with the medical system and physicians acting as an instrument of social control rather than neutral healers (compare Goffman's stigma work and Conrad's medicalisation critique above). Second, a broader conflict/Marxist turn in medical sociology that located the causes of disease in economic and political structures (poverty, unsafe work, unequal power) rather than in the functional needs of the social system as a whole. Freidson's work on the profession of medicine similarly emphasised that it is doctors — not some abstract social consensus — who hold the socially sanctioned power to define and control illness, foreshadowing later critiques of medical dominance.

Health Belief Model (Rosenstock)

A widely used framework for predicting preventive health behaviour (e.g., screening or vaccination uptake), built from six constructs: perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action, and self-efficacy.

Worked example: cervical screening uptake

A woman is more likely to attend cervical (Pap/HPV) screening if she believes she is personally at risk of cervical cancer (susceptibility), believes cervical cancer is serious (severity), believes screening genuinely reduces her risk (benefit), does not face major cost, embarrassment, language, or time barriers (barriers), receives a reminder from her clinic or sees a public campaign (cue to action), and feels confident she can navigate the appointment process (self-efficacy). Public health screening programmes are frequently redesigned around this model — for example, replacing a single mass-media message with SMS reminders (cue to action) plus free or mobile screening (removing barriers) — because barrier-reduction and cueing tend to move uptake more than raising perceived susceptibility alone.

💊 Medicalisation

Medicalisation (Zola, 1972; Conrad, 1992) is the process by which non-medical problems become defined and treated as medical problems, typically in terms of illnesses or disorders. Drivers include professional expansion, pharmaceutical industry marketing, patient advocacy seeking legitimacy/resources, and broader cultural faith in scientific authority.

  • Examples commonly cited in the literature: childbirth (from a family/community event to a hospital procedure), menopause (reframed as an "oestrogen deficiency disease"), shyness (reframed as social anxiety disorder), and attention/behaviour variation in children (ADHD).
  • Consequences: can bring genuine benefit (access to treatment, insurance coverage, reduced individual blame) but also risk of overdiagnosis, overtreatment, iatrogenesis, and narrowing of what counts as "normal" human variation.
Medicalisation is the conceptual mirror of "public issue" framing: while Mills asks us to move problems out of the individual/medical frame and into the structural frame, medicalisation theory studies the opposite historical movement — problems moving into the medical frame — and asks who benefits and who is harmed by that shift.
4. Social Capital & Social Support
Source: Putnam (2000) Bowling Alone; Bourdieu (1986); Coleman (1988); Portes (1998); Berkman & Syme (1979) Alameda County Study; Holt-Lunstad et al. (2010) meta-analysis
🤝 Social Capital — Definition

Social capital = "features of social organisation — networks, norms, trust — that enable coordination and cooperation for mutual benefit" (Putnam). It sits alongside physical, human and financial capital as a resource, but is held collectively rather than individually.

TheoristEmphasis
BourdieuSocial capital is one of several convertible forms of capital (alongside economic and cultural capital) that reproduces class advantage — access to networks is itself unequally distributed
ColemanSocial capital inheres in the structure of relations between actors — closure of a network (everyone knows everyone) enables trust and enforceable norms, e.g., in raising children
PutnamA societal-level, communitarian resource: civic engagement (club membership, voting, volunteering) generates generalized trust and reciprocity that benefit a whole community, not just direct participants
PortesA more critical, individual-level view — social capital is the ability of actors to secure benefits by virtue of membership in networks; warns against romanticising it, since it can also exclude outsiders
Putnam's Bowling Alone (2000) documented declining US civic participation (e.g., falling membership of bowling leagues, PTAs, unions) as evidence of eroding social capital, with implications for democratic engagement and, by extension, collective capacity to advocate for public goods including health services.
🔗 Bonding vs Bridging vs Linking
TypeDefinitionHealth example
BondingTies within a homogeneous group (strong, dense ties)Family and close ethnic/religious community support during acute illness or bereavement
BridgingTies between different groups (horizontal, weaker ties)Cross-community information exchange about which clinics/services are trustworthy or affordable
LinkingTies to institutions and people with formal power/authority (vertical)Access to state health programmes, ability to navigate bureaucracy, having someone who can advocate within the system
Bridging and linking capital often matter most for marginalised groups' health access, since bonding capital alone (however emotionally supportive) may lack the resources or institutional leverage to solve structural problems like housing or benefits access.

The "dark side" of social capital

Social capital is not unconditionally beneficial. Tight bonding capital can be exclusionary (out-group discrimination), can enforce restrictive norms that limit individual autonomy (e.g., pressure against women's healthcare-seeking outside the family, resistance to vaccination within a tightly bonded community susceptible to shared misinformation), or can facilitate collective harm (organised crime networks are, structurally, very high in bonding social capital). Portes and others caution against treating "more social capital" as automatically good — its health effect depends on the norms the network enforces and what resources it can access.

💗 Social Support & Mortality

Strong social relationships are associated with a substantially increased likelihood of survival, in effect sizes comparable to established clinical risk factors.

  • Types of support: emotional (empathy, caring), instrumental (tangible aid, e.g., transport to a clinic), informational (advice, guidance), appraisal (feedback useful for self-evaluation).
  • Buffering model: social support protects against the harmful health effects of stress specifically under conditions of high stress.
  • Main-effect model: social support benefits health directly, regardless of current stress level (e.g., through encouraging healthy behaviour, providing a sense of purpose and belonging).
  • Loneliness and social isolation are independent risk factors for all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease and dementia — independent of depression and baseline health status.

Berkman & Syme, Alameda County Study (1979)

A landmark 9-year prospective cohort study of ~7,000 adults in Alameda County, California. People who were socially isolated (few close relationships, little community/organisational participation) at baseline had roughly 2 to 3 times the age-adjusted mortality risk over the follow-up period compared with those who were socially well-connected, an association that persisted after adjusting for baseline physical health, socioeconomic status and health behaviours (smoking, alcohol use, physical activity).

Holt-Lunstad et al. (2010) meta-analysis

A meta-analysis pooling 148 studies (over 300,000 participants) found that stronger social relationships were associated with a 50% increased likelihood of survival — expressed as an average odds ratio of ~1.50 — an effect comparable in magnitude to quitting smoking, and larger than well-established risk factors such as physical inactivity (OR ~1.30) or obesity (OR ~1.20 in that analysis).

🧮 Worked calculation: relative risk from a hypothetical isolation cohort ▼

Suppose a simplified hypothetical cohort of 2,000 adults followed for 9 years is split into "socially isolated" and "socially connected" groups, with the following outcomes:

GroupNDeaths over 9 yrsCumulative incidence (risk)
Isolated5009090 ÷ 500 = 0.18 (18%)
Connected1,500135135 ÷ 1,500 = 0.09 (9%)

Relative Risk (RR) = risk in isolated ÷ risk in connected = 0.18 ÷ 0.09 = 2.0

Interpretation: in this hypothetical cohort, socially isolated individuals had twice the risk of death over 9 years compared with socially connected individuals — consistent in direction and rough magnitude with the real Alameda County findings. Note the distinction from an odds ratio (used in the Holt-Lunstad meta-analysis, which pooled studies with varying designs including case-control-type comparisons): RR compares cumulative risk directly and is more intuitive to interpret in a cohort design, while OR approximates RR well when the outcome is relatively rare.

5. Globalisation & Health
Source: Labonté & Schrecker; WHO; Omran (1971) epidemiological transition theory; Beck (1992) Risk Society; WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control
🌐 Globalisation & Health

Globalisation = the widening, deepening and speeding up of worldwide interconnectedness — of trade, finance, people, information, and disease. Health effects run in both directions: beneficial (knowledge diffusion, medical technology transfer, development aid, global disease surveillance) and harmful (unhealthy commodity flows, financial-crisis contagion, weakened policy space for health regulation).

  • Epidemics and pandemics spread faster and further via global travel and trade networks (SARS 2003, H1N1 2009, COVID-19 2020).
  • Global marketing and distribution of unhealthy commodities — tobacco, alcohol, ultra-processed food and sugar-sweetened beverages — track globalisation's trade routes and marketing infrastructure ("commercial determinants of health").
  • Health-worker migration ("brain drain") flows disproportionately from low- and middle-income to high-income countries, driven by wage differentials and working conditions.
Beck's "risk society" concept: in late-modern, globalised societies, major risks (pandemics, climate change, financial crises) are manufactured and global in scope, no longer confined by national borders — health governance must therefore also become global (WHO International Health Regulations).

Glocalisation

Global forces do not simply overwrite local culture; they are adapted and reinterpreted locally ("glocalisation" — Robertson). For example, global fast-food chains adapt menus to local tastes, and traditional medicine systems selectively incorporate biomedical elements — globalisation is a two-way, hybridising process, not one-directional Westernisation.

🔄 Epidemiological / NCD Transition

Omran's epidemiological transition theory (1971) describes three classic stages as societies develop:

StagePattern
1. Age of pestilence & famineHigh mortality dominated by infectious disease, malnutrition and maternal mortality; low, fluctuating life expectancy
2. Age of receding pandemicsInfectious-disease mortality declines with sanitation, nutrition, and public health measures; life expectancy rises
3. Age of degenerative & man-made diseaseChronic non-communicable diseases (cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes) become dominant causes of death as populations age and lifestyles shift

A commonly proposed fourth stage — the "age of delayed degenerative diseases" — describes further-postponed NCD mortality through medical treatment (e.g., statins, revascularisation) even without eliminating the underlying risk factors.

Malaysia — like many middle-income countries — exhibits the double burden of disease: an unfinished agenda of communicable disease and maternal/child health issues, alongside a rapidly rising burden of diabetes, hypertension, obesity and cancers, compressed into a much shorter timeframe than the transition took in early-industrialising Europe.
🧮 Worked calculation: DALYs and a simple prevalence estimate ▼

Disability-Adjusted Life Year (DALY) is the standard summary measure of population disease burden used to compare the impact of communicable and non-communicable disease side by side (essential for describing a "double burden"):

DALY = YLL + YLD

where YLL (Years of Life Lost) = number of deaths × standard life expectancy at age of death, and YLD (Years Lived with Disability) = number of prevalent cases × disability weight (0–1 scale) × average duration of the condition.

Illustrative worked example: Suppose in a hypothetical district, diabetes causes 50 deaths in a year, each occurring on average 15 years earlier than the standard life expectancy at that age (YLL = 50 × 15 = 750), and there are 2,000 people currently living with diabetes-related complications, with an average disability weight of 0.15 sustained for that year (YLD = 2,000 × 0.15 × 1 = 300). Total DALYs from diabetes that year = 750 + 300 = 1,050 DALYs.

Simple prevalence calculation for describing the "double burden": Crude prevalence = number of existing cases ÷ total population at risk. If a national health survey finds 3.6 million adults with diabetes among 24 million adults, crude prevalence = 3,600,000 ÷ 24,000,000 = 0.15, i.e. 15% of adults — a figure regularly cited to describe the scale of Malaysia's NCD burden, sitting alongside continuing efforts on dengue, TB and maternal/child health (the "unfinished agenda").

📜 Trade, Neoliberalism & Health
  • Neoliberal policies (privatisation, deregulation, trade liberalisation, fiscal austerity) can erode the welfare state and widen health inequalities by reducing redistribution and social protection.
  • Structural adjustment programmes (SAPs) imposed by international financial institutions on indebted countries from the 1980s onward often required cuts to public health and education spending, user fees for health services, and currency devaluation — associated in the literature with worsened health outcomes and reduced health-service utilisation in affected countries, particularly for the poorest.
  • Trade agreements (e.g., regional trade/investment pacts) can affect tobacco control regulation, intellectual-property protection on medicines, and investor-state dispute mechanisms that allow companies to challenge public-health regulation.
  • WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC, 2003): the first WHO treaty, explicitly designed to give tobacco-control measures priority over conflicting trade obligations, and to prevent tobacco-industry interference in policy-making.
  • TRIPS and medicines access: the WTO's Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights agreement extended patent protection globally, raising concerns about access to affordable generic medicines in low- and middle-income countries; the Doha Declaration (2001) affirmed countries' right to use compulsory licensing flexibilities to protect public health.

6. Welfare State, Health Policy & Reform

🏛️ Welfare Regimes (Esping-Andersen)

Three "worlds of welfare capitalism," classified by decommodification (the extent to which social services are available as a right of citizenship rather than purchased on the market) and social stratification (how welfare arrangements reinforce or reduce class divisions):

🧮 Worked calculation: catastrophic health expenditure ▼

Out-of-pocket (OOP) payment is a key indicator of financial protection. WHO commonly defines catastrophic health expenditure as household OOP health spending exceeding 40% of the household's capacity to pay (non-subsistence income, i.e. total expenditure minus spending on basic food needs).

Worked example: a household has monthly total expenditure of RM 3,000, of which RM 1,200 goes to basic food/subsistence needs, leaving a capacity to pay of RM 1,800. If the household spends RM 800 out-of-pocket that month on a family member's hospitalisation:

OOP share of capacity to pay = 800 ÷ 1,800 = 0.44 (44%)

Since 44% exceeds the 40% threshold, this household has incurred catastrophic health expenditure — a key motivation for moving health systems away from OOP-dominant financing toward pooled, prepaid mechanisms (tax or insurance funded) as part of Universal Health Coverage (UHC).

🇬🇧 The UK NHS & Its Significance

The 1948 NHS embodied the Beveridge Report (1942) principles: health care free at the point of use, funded from general taxation, and available to all citizens regardless of ability to pay — conceived as part of a broader post-war welfare settlement attacking Beveridge's "five giants" (want, disease, ignorance, squalor, idleness). It became an internationally influential template, widely debated and partially emulated.

  • Reforms: the 1990s internal market reforms introduced a purchaser–provider split; subsequent New Public Management approaches introduced competition, performance targets and greater managerial control.
  • Ongoing tension between equity (universal, needs-based access) and efficiency/responsiveness (market-style incentives, patient choice).
  • Contemporary pressures: an ageing population increasing demand, rising costs of new technology and medicines, workforce shortages, and growing waiting lists — recurring themes in comparative health-policy debate about the sustainability of tax-funded universal systems.
🇲🇾 Health Systems Reform & Malaysia
  • Malaysia operates a mixed system: a predominantly tax-funded public sector delivering highly subsidised care (historically nominal fees at government facilities) alongside a growing, more expensive private sector — raising concerns about a widening dual-tier system and workforce drift from public to private practice.
  • 1Care reform proposals explored moving toward a single-payer, social health insurance-style unification of financing — a contested proposal that was ultimately not implemented, illustrating the political difficulty of large-scale health-financing reform even where the technical case is debated on its merits.
  • More recent policy discussion (health white-paper style reform proposals) has focused on strengthening primary care, purchaser–provider separation, and sustainable financing mechanisms as the population ages and NCD burden grows.
  • Universal Health Coverage (UHC) is typically visualised as a "coverage cube" with three dimensions that must each expand: (1) population covered, (2) services covered, and (3) proportion of costs covered (reducing OOP) — reform debates in Malaysia and elsewhere are essentially arguments about how to expand along these three axes without triggering catastrophic expenditure or unsustainable fiscal cost.
  • Social medicine tradition (Rudolf Virchow, 1848, reporting on a typhus epidemic in Upper Silesia): medicine is fundamentally a social science, and physicians are "natural attorneys of the poor" — an early and influential articulation of the idea that clinical and structural/political action on health are inseparable.
Virchow: "Medicine is a social science, and politics is nothing else but medicine on a large scale." This single sentence is frequently used to open or close essay answers linking medical sociology back to public health practice and policy.
7. Discourse Analysis
Source: Foucault (discourse & power/knowledge); Raphael (2011) "A Discourse Analysis of the Social Determinants of Health," Critical Public Health; Esping-Andersen (1990); Saint-Arnaud & Bernard (2003)
🗣️ What Is a "Discourse," and Why Analyse It?

In everyday use, "discourse" just means talk or text. In critical social theory, following Michel Foucault, a discourse is something stronger: a system of thought — composed of ideas, concepts, categories, and practices — that systematically constructs the objects and subjects of which it speaks, rather than simply describing a pre-existing reality. Discourses carry power: they determine what counts as legitimate knowledge, who is authorised to speak with expertise, which questions are askable, and which policy responses appear "common sense" versus unthinkable.

Discourse analysis in medical sociology therefore asks not just "what does this policy document/textbook/news article say about health?" but "what does this way of talking about health make possible, and what does it make invisible? Whose interests does this framing serve, and whose does it obscure?"

Why this matters for public health practice

Two texts can present the identical evidence base on, say, obesity, yet one frames it as a matter of individual "lifestyle choice" (implying the remedy is health education and willpower) while the other frames it as a matter of an "obesogenic environment" shaped by food marketing, urban design and income (implying the remedy is fiscal and regulatory policy). Both are technically compatible with the data, but each discourse legitimises a different, and sometimes mutually exclusive, set of interventions and a different attribution of responsibility and blame.

📊 Raphael's Seven Discourses of the Social Determinants of Health

Dennis Raphael (2011) analysed how the same underlying concept — the social determinants of health — is discussed in radically different, and progressively more structural, ways across Canadian (and, by extension, other) health policy documents and research literature. He identified seven discourses, each with different practical implications:

#DiscoursePractical implication
1SDH as identifying who needs health/social servicesFocus limited to service provision for those already affected; does not reduce the underlying need
2SDH as identifying modifiable medical/behavioural risk factors ("healthy lifestyles")Targets individual behaviour change; risks victim-blaming and shows weak evidence of effectiveness
3SDH as the material living conditions that shape health via biological/psychosocial pathwaysIdentifies pathways (how SDH "get under the skin"), strengthening the evidence base, but can be diluted into parenting/school programmes rather than income or housing policy
4SDH as material conditions that differ by group membership (class, gender, race)Documents systematic inequality between groups; risk of reducing solutions to anti-discrimination training rather than redistribution
5SDH and their distribution as the result of public policy decisionsDirects attention to government policymaking itself as the lever for change (the WHO CSDH's own position)
6SDH and their distribution as the result of a nation's economic/political structure and ideology ("worlds of welfare")Explains cross-country variation via welfare-regime type (social-democratic vs liberal vs conservative) — see Module 6
7SDH and their distribution as the result of the power and interests of those who benefit from inequalityNames the classes/interests that gain from inequality, and mobilises advocacy against them — Navarro: "it is not inequalities that kill, but those who benefit from the inequalities that kill"
Raphael's key argument: discourses 1–4 are individually true and useful but, on their own, tend to depoliticise the SDH agenda by keeping the analysis at the level of the affected individual or group. Discourses 5–7 progressively re-politicise the SDH by tracing the causal chain back to public policy, then to the political economy/welfare-regime type, and finally to the power and interests that keep an inequitable system in place. A written answer that only rehearses discourse 2 or 3 (lifestyle/pathways) without noting discourses 5–7 (policy, political economy, power) is, on Raphael's own critique, reproducing exactly the depoliticised framing he warns against.
🔍 Applying Discourse Analysis
  • Method, briefly: discourse analysis typically involves close reading of texts (policy documents, media coverage, clinical guidelines, patient information leaflets) to identify recurring framings, metaphors, silences (what is not discussed), and the implied causal model and locus of responsibility.
  • Worked contrast: a national "obesity strategy" that opens with statistics on individual calorie intake and closes with recommendations for school nutrition education and personal responsibility campaigns is operating largely within Raphael's discourses 2–3. A strategy that opens with food-industry marketing expenditure and closes with recommendations for a sugar-sweetened beverage tax, restrictions on advertising to children, and urban planning for walkability is operating within discourses 5–6.
  • Link to welfare-regime discourse (6): Saint-Arnaud & Bernard's model (building on Esping-Andersen) shows how the same SDH evidence is read differently depending on a country's dominant political ideology — social-democratic systems foreground equality/universalism as the organising principle, liberal systems foreground minimal state intervention and means-tested residual support, conservative systems foreground social stability via employment-based insurance (see Module 6 table).
Examination tip: discourse analysis is not simply "critical thinking" in a vague sense — a strong answer names the specific framing being used, identifies whose interests or which ideology it serves, and states what alternative framing (and therefore what alternative policy response) becomes visible once the discourse is made explicit.
8. Social Problems & Public Health
Source: Mechanic (2000) & related AJPH editorials, "Social Issues as Public Health: Promise and Peril"; McKeown (1976); Szreter (2002) "Rethinking McKeown"; Link & Phelan (fundamental cause theory)
🧩 When Does a Social Problem Become a Public Health Problem?

Public health has long faced a fork in the road: a narrow direction that focuses on proximal, individual-level risk factors, and a broad direction that addresses the socio-cultural and structural foundations of health — poverty, discrimination, homelessness, violence, war. The broad direction has intellectual force because it targets fundamental causes rather than downstream risk factors (Link & Phelan) and gives public health a powerful rhetoric for tackling social and economic injustice — this is the same logic as Mills's move from "personal trouble" to "public issue" (Module 1) applied to policy practice rather than just analysis.

Bringing a social problem "into" public health can mobilise resources, evidence, and political attention that it might not otherwise receive — this is the promise.
⚠️ The "Peril": Healthification of Social Problems

Bringing social problems into the public health domain also carries risk — described as the "healthification" of social problems (echoing "medicalisation," Module 3): once a social problem is examined through the narrow, individual-level tools of epidemiology, it can end up depoliticised rather than solved. Three specific perils are commonly identified, illustrated with the historical example of homelessness research in the public health literature:

  1. Focus on the individual: although homelessness has well-understood structural causes (housing policy, welfare retrenchment, labour-market insecurity), public-health research on homelessness has often studied it "as if it were a disease" — identifying individual risk factors (mental illness, substance use, childhood disruption) using the same epidemiological tools built for studying disease in individuals, while leaving the structural, policy-level causes largely unexamined.
  2. Institutionalisation of a narrow research paradigm: once a social problem is established as a "public health research question," a body of literature, funding stream, and set of accepted methods and constructs builds up around individual-level explanation, which is then difficult to redirect toward structural analysis — the paradigm becomes self-perpetuating.
  3. Valuation of the problem only by its health consequences: if research findings become "the evidence for and definition of the wrongfulness" of a social problem, this implies the problem matters only insofar as it damages health. But homelessness, discrimination and violence are wrong independent of any measurable health effect — over-reliance on health outcomes as the moral justification for acting on social injustice is, on this critique, itself a subtle failure of the sociological imagination.
This is not an argument for retreating to a narrow, purely biomedical public health. It is a caution: naming a social problem as a "public health issue" is only useful if the analysis and the proposed remedies stay at the structural level that the problem actually requires — otherwise the label is used while the substance (individual-level solutions to a structural problem) reverts to exactly the narrow approach the broad framing was meant to move beyond.
📉 The McKeown Debate: Did Medicine or Society Save Lives?

Thomas McKeown argued (1976) that the dramatic decline in mortality in industrialising Britain from the 18th to 20th centuries was driven mainly by rising living standards, better nutrition, and broader economic growth — not by curative medicine or even organised public health intervention, which he argued arrived too late or contributed too little to explain the bulk of the decline. This became one of the most influential (and contested) theses in the history of public health, because it directly challenged clinical medicine's claim to have been the primary force behind modern gains in life expectancy.

Support for McKeown's broad claim

Aggregate historical mortality data show major declines in infectious-disease deaths well before the relevant vaccines or antibiotics were developed or widely available, consistent with nutrition/living-standard improvements (and, later, sanitation) doing much of the early work.

Szreter's (2002) critique of McKeown

Historian Simon Szreter argued McKeown understated the role of deliberate, organised political and public-health action — sanitary reform, vital registration and census systems, housing regulation, and redistributive social policy driven by 19th-century public health pioneers and reformers. Public health, Szreter insists, is "an intrinsically political subject" that cannot be reduced to the passive by-product of economic growth ("the rising tide lifts all boats"); it required organised human agency, political will, and funding decisions.

Link & Phelan's fundamental cause theory (Module 2) offers a synthesis: McKeown was right that social/economic conditions are a persistently powerful driver of population health, but this is precisely because socioeconomic resources let advantaged groups exploit whatever mechanism of protection is currently available — nutrition and sanitation in the 19th century, antibiotics and vaccines in the 20th, novel biologics and early cancer detection today. Medicine and social conditions are not competing explanations; social conditions determine who benefits most from medicine at any given time.

Why this debate matters for practice

The McKeown debate is frequently invoked (explicitly or implicitly) whenever health-system planners must decide how to allocate scarce resources between curative/clinical services and upstream social/economic investment (housing, income support, education, sanitation). Malaysia's own experience of the "double burden of disease" and the unfinished agenda of infectious disease control (Module 5) sits directly on this fault line: sustained gains typically require both continued clinical/public-health service delivery and deliberate socioeconomic policy — echoing Szreter's insistence that public health outcomes are never automatic products of growth alone, and Virchow's dictum (Module 6) that "politics is nothing else but medicine on a large scale."

Q1 Social Determinants 15 marks
Define social determinants of health and explain the 'social gradient' in health with one example of a policy response.
Marks guide: 15 marks: definition 4 · gradient 6 · policy 5
Definition

SDH are the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work and age, shaped by the distribution of money, power and resources (WHO CSDH 2008). They operate through structural determinants (governance, macroeconomic and social policy, culture) that shape socioeconomic position, and intermediary determinants (material circumstances, psychosocial factors, behavioural/biological factors, and the health system) that translate that position into health outcomes.

Social gradient

The gradient means health improves steadily with each step up the socioeconomic ladder — not only the poor suffer; even among the affluent, lower relative rank is associated with worse health than higher rank. Example: a 7–10+ year life-expectancy gap is commonly documented between the richest and poorest local areas within the same country. The Whitehall studies showed this gradient even among British civil servants with equal NHS access, where the lowest employment grade had roughly 3–4 times the coronary heart disease mortality of the highest grade, only partly explained by classic risk factors — evidence that relative status and job control (psychosocial pathways), not material deprivation or health-care access alone, drive part of the gradient.

Policy response

Marmot's proportionate universalism — universal policies delivered with intensity proportionate to the level of disadvantage (e.g., universal early-childhood services with additional home-visiting for the most deprived families; a living wage; targeted investment in education quality in deprived areas), consistent with the Marmot Review's six policy objectives (e.g., "give every child the best start in life," "create fair employment and good work for all").

Extension: measuring the gradient

A strong answer can note that the gradient is properly measured using the whole distribution (e.g., the Relative or Slope Index of Inequality), not just a top-vs-bottom ratio, since a two-group comparison discards information about the intermediate groups and can understate how continuous and pervasive the gradient is.

Q2 Culture & Illness 15 marks
Distinguish disease from illness. Using Kleinman's explanatory model, describe how a clinician might improve adherence in a patient from a different cultural background.
Marks guide: 15 marks: distinction 5 · explanatory model 5 · adherence 5
Disease vs illness

Disease = the biological/pathological reality (etic, professional perspective), measurable through pathology, imaging or biomarkers. Illness = the lived, personally and socially experienced problem (emic, lay perspective) — how the person interprets, feels and communicates being unwell. A third term, sickness, describes the social role assigned to the unwell person (e.g., certified leave). Disjunction between disease and illness — e.g., a patient who does not "feel" their asymptomatic hypertension as an illness — drives poor adherence.

Explanatory model

Kleinman: patients, family members and clinicians each hold an explanatory model of an episode of sickness covering aetiology, onset, pathophysiology, course and treatment. The clinician should actively elicit the patient's model, for example by asking what they think caused it, why it started when it did, how severe they believe it to be, and what they fear most — rather than assuming the biomedical model is self-evidently shared.

Adherence

By negotiating between models — validating the patient's explanatory framework (e.g., a humoral "hot–cold" or "wind" belief) while sharing the biomedical one in comprehensible terms — mutual understanding and trust increase, improving adherence without a coercive "compliance" framing that positions the patient as merely obedient or disobedient.

Extension: linking to stigma and the sick role

Where the condition is stigmatised (Goffman), disclosure and treatment-seeking may be further complicated by fear of discreditation, so the clinician should also consider confidentiality and information-management concerns. Where the illness is chronic, the classic Parsonian sick role (temporary exemption from duties, in exchange for wanting to get well and cooperating with treatment) fits poorly, and the clinician should instead support a sustainable long-term self-management identity rather than a short-term "patient" role.

Q3 Globalisation 20 marks
Discuss how globalisation produces both opportunities and risks for population health, with reference to the double burden of disease in Malaysia.
Marks guide: 20 marks: opportunities 6 · risks 8 · Malaysia 6
Opportunities

Rapid knowledge transfer and research collaboration across borders, cheaper diagnostics and medical technology through global supply chains, global disease surveillance and coordinated response under the WHO International Health Regulations, and pooled international procurement (e.g., for vaccines) lowering per-unit medicine and vaccine costs for participating countries.

Risks

Faster and wider pandemic spread via global travel and trade networks (SARS, H1N1, COVID-19); global marketing of tobacco, alcohol and ultra-processed food/sugar-sweetened beverages driving the rise of NCDs (the "commercial determinants of health"); health-worker migration/brain drain from lower- to higher-income health systems, driven by wage and working-condition differentials; and trade and investment rules that can constrain domestic tobacco-control regulation or raise medicine prices through extended patent protection (TRIPS), though instruments like the FCTC and the Doha Declaration attempt to protect policy space for health.

Malaysia double burden

Malaysia carries an unfinished agenda of infectious disease and maternal/child/nutritional health issues alongside a rapid rise in diabetes, hypertension, obesity and cancers — the double burden of disease — intensified by globalised commodity flows (processed food, tobacco, alcohol marketing) and rapid urbanisation reducing incidental physical activity. Illustratively, if national survey data show diabetes prevalence around 15% of adults, this sits alongside continuing public-health effort on dengue control, TB and maternal/newborn health, meaning health systems must resource both agendas simultaneously rather than sequentially, unlike the more gradual historical transition experienced by early-industrialising countries (Omran's epidemiological transition).

Extension: policy implications

A strong answer connects this back to welfare-state and financing themes: managing a double burden under a still-developing universal-coverage system raises the risk of catastrophic household health expenditure unless financing is pooled and prepaid (tax or insurance funded) rather than out-of-pocket, and argues for governance of trade and commercial determinants (e.g., sugar-sweetened beverage taxation, tobacco/alcohol marketing restriction) as a necessary complement to clinical NCD management.

Q4 Discourse & Social Problems 20 marks
Using Raphael's discourse analysis of the social determinants of health, explain why simply relabelling a social problem as a "public health issue" does not guarantee a structural policy response. Illustrate with an example.
Marks guide: 20 marks: discourse concept 5 · Raphael's discourses 8 · healthification peril & example 7
Discourse concept

A discourse (Foucault) is a system of thought that constructs, rather than neutrally describes, its object — it determines what counts as legitimate knowledge and which policy responses appear "common sense." The same evidence on a health problem can be discussed in radically different discourses, each legitimising a different remedy and a different attribution of responsibility.

Raphael's SDH discourses

Raphael (2011) identified seven discourses of the SDH, ranging from individually true but depoliticised framings — identifying who needs services (1), modifiable lifestyle risk factors (2), the pathways by which material conditions "get under the skin" (3), and differences between social groups (4) — through to progressively re-politicised framings that trace the causal chain to public policy decisions (5), a nation's political-economy/welfare-regime type (6), and finally the power and interests of those who benefit from inequality (7). Only discourses 5–7 point toward structural, policy-level remedies.

Healthification peril & example

Labelling a social problem a "public health issue" does not by itself move the analysis into discourses 5–7 — it can just as easily be absorbed into discourses 1–3, producing the "healthification" peril: the problem is studied with epidemiological tools built for individual-level disease, a research paradigm institutionalises individual risk-factor explanations, and the problem's wrongfulness becomes valued only via its health consequences. Example: homelessness framed as a public health issue can generate valuable clinical/preventive services (discourse 1) and risk-factor research on individual psychiatric or substance-use antecedents (discourses 2–4), while leaving housing policy, welfare retrenchment and labour-market insecurity — the actual structural causes — largely unaddressed. A structural response requires explicitly invoking discourses 5–7: identifying which housing and social policy decisions produced the problem, which welfare-regime type and ideology shape the policy response, and which interests benefit from underinvestment in affordable housing.

Extension

This connects back to Mills's personal-trouble/public-issue distinction (Module 1) and to McKeown/Szreter's debate about whether health gains are automatic products of economic growth or require deliberate political action (Module 8) — in both cases, the strength of an answer lies in showing the mechanism by which a structural framing is (or is not) translated into a structural remedy, not merely asserting that "social factors matter."
