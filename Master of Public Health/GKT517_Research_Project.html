1. The Research Process & Question
Source: GKT 517 Course Notes — Concept, Problem, Objectives, RQ
🔄 The Research Process
  1. Identify a research problem (gap in knowledge/practice).
  2. Review literature; refine the problem.
  3. State research question(s) & objectives/hypotheses.
  4. Design the study & sampling.
  5. Collect & analyse data.
  6. Interpret, write up, disseminate, apply.
An iterative, not strictly linear, cycle — proposals often loop back after literature review.
Why the cycle is iterative, not linear ▼

Textbooks draw the research process as a straight line (problem → literature → question → design → data → write-up) for teaching purposes, but in practice each step feeds back on the ones before it. This matters for grading and for real projects because examiners expect you to show awareness of the looping, not just recite the list.

  • Literature review reshapes the problem. A student starting with "factors associated with poor glycaemic control" may discover, after reading 30 papers, that the construct has already been extensively studied for adherence but not for the specific sub-group of newly-diagnosed patients — the problem statement narrows accordingly.
  • Design reshapes the question. If a cohort design is infeasible within a one-year master's timeline, the RQ may need to shift from "does X cause Y over 5 years" to a cross-sectional "what is the association between X and Y at one point in time" — with the causal language removed.
  • Piloting reshapes the instrument, and sometimes the objectives. A pilot may reveal that a key exposure variable is too sensitive to collect via self-administered questionnaire and needs face-to-face administration, or that a proposed scale has floor/ceiling effects in the local population.
  • Ethics review can reshape sampling. An IRB/JEPeM committee may require an opt-in rather than opt-out consent procedure, which changes the expected response rate and therefore the sample-size calculation.

Gordis (Epidemiology) frames this as the constant interplay between "what we want to know" and "what we can feasibly and ethically measure" — a mismatch anywhere in the cycle should send you back a step, not forward with a flawed plan.

Exam tip: When asked to "describe the research process," draw or narrate it as a loop with explicit feedback arrows (literature ⇄ problem, design ⇄ question, pilot ⇄ instrument) rather than a numbered list only — this earns application marks beyond simple recall.
❓ From Problem to Research Question

A good RQ is focused, feasible, and researchable. It should arise from a clear problem statement and literature gap.

  • Quantitative RQ often asks about relationship / difference / frequency.
  • Qualitative RQ asks how / why / what is the experience.
  • Avoid vague or unanswerable questions.
A weak RQ dooms the project — spend time here before designing.
FINER and SMART criteria for a research question ▼

Two complementary checklists are commonly used to critique a draft RQ before committing to it:

FINER (Hulley et al.)What it checks
FeasibleAdequate subjects, technical expertise, time, money, manageable scope
InterestingIntellectually engaging to the investigator (sustains motivation through a long project)
NovelConfirms, refutes, or extends prior findings — not simple duplication
EthicalAmenable to a study that an ethics committee will approve
RelevantTo scientific knowledge, clinical/public-health practice, or future research/policy

Once FINER is satisfied, the RQ itself is often refined against SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. In practice, "specific" and "measurable" are where most weak RQs fail — e.g. "Is smoking bad for health?" is neither specific (which health outcome? which population?) nor measurable as written.

Worked example of RQ refinement

Vague draft

"Does poor diet affect diabetes in Malaysia?"

Refined, FINER-checked RQ

"Among adults aged 30–65 years with type 2 diabetes attending a primary care clinic, what is the association between high dietary glycaemic index intake (assessed by validated FFQ) and poor glycaemic control (HbA1c ≥ 7%) within the past 6 months?"

Notice the refined version specifies population, exposure with a measurement method, outcome with a threshold, and a time frame — each addition closes off a source of ambiguity that would otherwise cause disagreement between researcher, supervisor and analyst about what is actually being tested.

🎯 PICO & Objectives

Frame quantitative questions with PICO:

PPopulation / Participants
IIntervention / Exposure

  • Objectives are specific, measurable statements translating the RQ.
  • Primary objective (main) vs secondary objectives.
Well-built PICO makes design, sampling and analysis flow logically.
PICO variants, and translating PICO into objectives ▼

PICO was originally developed for clinical questions about interventions; for observational aetiological or descriptive research the "I" is more naturally read as Exposure, giving the variant PECO (Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome). For prevalence/burden studies without a comparison group, some authors use PO only (Population, Outcome) plus a Time/Setting element, sometimes written PICOT or PEO.

Question typeFrameExample
Therapy/interventionPICODoes a text-message reminder (I) vs no reminder (C) improve medication adherence (O) in diabetic patients (P)?
Aetiology / risk factorPECOIs night-shift work (E) vs day work (C) associated with higher risk of hypertension (O) among nurses (P)?
Prevalence / descriptivePO(+T)What is the prevalence (O) of low back pain (O) among factory workers (P) in the past 12 months (T)?
Diagnostic accuracyPIROIn patients with suspected TB (P), how does GeneXpert (I=index test) compare with sputum culture (R=reference standard) for detecting infection (O)?

Turning PICO into SMART objectives

Each PICO element usually maps onto one clause of the objective statement. Using the diabetes-adherence example from PYQ 1:

Primary objective

"To determine the prevalence of, and factors associated with, non-adherence to oral hypoglycaemic medication among adults with type 2 diabetes attending [clinic] between [month] and [month] [year]."

Secondary objectives (examples)
  • To describe the socio-demographic profile of non-adherent patients.
  • To determine the association between health literacy and medication adherence.
  • To identify the most commonly reported reasons for non-adherence using the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8).

Note each objective is measurable (has a named variable/instrument), time-bound, and traceable directly back to a PICO element — an examiner marking an objectives section is essentially checking that every PICO component reappears somewhere in the objectives, and nothing appears in the objectives that wasn't in the PICO/RQ.

2. Literature Review, Protocol & Proposal
Source: GKT 517 Course Notes — Literature, Review, Protocol, Proposal
🔎 Literature Search & Management

Systematic, reproducible searching using a defined strategy.

  • Databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane, MyJurnal, Google Scholar (supplementary).
  • Build a search with MeSH / keywords, Boolean (AND/OR/NOT), limits.
  • Manage references with EndNote / Mendeley / Zotero; use a PRISMA flow if systematic.
Document the search (databases, dates, terms) for reproducibility.
📚 Types of Review
TypeSynthesis
Narrative reviewThematic, non-systematic
Systematic reviewExplicit, reproducible, low bias
Meta-analysisStatistical pooling of effect sizes
Scoping reviewMap breadth of evidence
A protocol (PROSPERO) pre-registers a systematic review to reduce bias.
📝 Protocol & Research Proposal

The protocol is the detailed plan of the study (the 'rulebook'). The proposal justifies and seeks approval for it.

  • Proposal sections: title, background, problem, RQ/objectives, significance, methods, ethics, timeline, budget, references.
  • A strong proposal shows feasibility, rigour and relevance.
Write the protocol before data collection — it is your quality guarantee and ethics-submission backbone.
3. Study Design, Sampling & Sample Size
Source: GKT 517 Course Notes — Study Design, Sampling, Determination
🌳 Choosing a Design

Design follows the RQ (see GKT 502 module for the taxonomy). For a master's project, feasibility is decisive.

  • Aetiologic 'what causes?' → cohort / case-control.
  • Prevalence / burden → cross-sectional.
  • Intervention → RCT / quasi-experimental (if ethical/feasible).
  • Experience/meaning → qualitative (phenomenology, grounded theory).
Mixed-methods combine quantitative + qualitative to answer complementary questions.
🎲 Sampling Methods
ProbabilityNon-probability
Simple randomConvenience
SystematicPurposive
StratifiedQuota
Cluster / multi-stageSnowball
  • Probability sampling → generalisable inference.
  • Sample frame must cover the target population.
The unit of sampling, unit of analysis and unit of inference must be explicit and aligned.
📐 Sample Size & Power
  • Determined by: expected effect size, α, power (1−β, ≥0.80), variability, design.
  • Use formulas or software (OpenEpi, G*Power, PASS).
  • For proportions/means, precision (margin of error) may drive size when no hypothesis test.
Under-powered studies risk type II error; over-sized waste resources. Justify every assumption.
4. Data Collection, Measurement & Tools
Source: GKT 517 Course Notes — Collection, Validity, Reliability, Questionnaire
🗂️ Data Collection

Plan who collects, how, when, and quality control (training, piloting, double-entry, SOPs).

  • Quantitative: questionnaire, records, biomarkers, observation.
  • Qualitative: interview, focus group, observation, document review.
  • Pilot test the instrument before full fieldwork.
Piloting reveals ambiguous items, timing and logistics problems cheaply.
✅ Validity & Reliability
ConceptMeaning
ValidityMeasures what it claims to (accuracy)
ReliabilityConsistent / reproducible results
InternalCorrectly measures the construct in this study
ExternalGeneralisable to other settings
  • A tool can be reliable but invalid (consistently wrong).
  • Improve via standardised tools, translation & back-translation, training.
Use validated, published instruments where possible; cite their psychometrics.
📋 Questionnaire Design
  • Clear, unbiased, single-concept items; logical flow; appropriate response scales.
  • Demographics first/last; sensitive items later; randomised where needed.
  • Pre-test readability and average completion time.
Avoid leading/double-barrelled questions; match language to respondents.
5. Analysis, Writing & Research Ethics
Source: GKT 517 Course Notes — Analysis, Writing, Ethics, Referencing
📊 Analysis Plan

Pre-specify analysis to avoid data dredging.

  • Descriptive then inferential; choose tests by data type & distribution (see GKT 515).
  • Handle missing data (complete-case, imputation) & confounders (regression).
  • Qualitative: thematic / content analysis, with coding framework & triangulation.
State the software (SPSS/R/STATA), significance level, and how you'll report effect sizes & CIs.
✍️ Thesis & Report Writing

Standard thesis/report structure (IMRaD + appendices): title, abstract, intro, lit review, methods, results, discussion, conclusion, references, appendices.

  • Results report findings; Discussion interprets vs literature, limits, implications.
  • Use a consistent referencing style (Harvard/APA/ Vancouver per faculty).
  • Write for clarity; figures/tables self-explanatory.
A good discussion addresses limitations honestly and situates findings in context.
⚖️ Research Ethics
  • Respect for persons (informed consent, autonomy), beneficence, justice (Belmont).
  • Minimise harm; confidentiality & anonymisation; secure data.
  • Submit to the relevant ethics committee / IRB (e.g., JEPeM) before data collection.
  • Special populations: children, vulnerable groups need extra safeguards.
No data collection without approved ethics clearance. De-identify and keep raw data out of shared/synced spaces per PDPA governance.
📝 Sample Questions (with model answers)
Practice questions in the style of GKT 517 past-year papers
Q1 Question & Design 15 marks
A master's student wants to study factors associated with non-adherence to diabetes medication in a clinic. (a) Frame a PICO question. (b) Suggest a suitable design and justify briefly.
Marks guide: 15 marks: PICO 8 · design 7
PICO

P: adults with type 2 diabetes attending the clinic. I: non-adherent (vs adherent) to oral hypoglycaemics. C: adherent patients (referent). O: prevalence/odds of non-adherence and associated factors (e.g., forgetfulness, cost, knowledge).

Design

A cross-sectional study is suitable to estimate the prevalence of non-adherence and its correlates efficiently; if testing specific causal predictors with temporality, a case-control (non-adherent vs adherent) could explore multiple factors. Cohort is less feasible for a master's timeline.

Q2 Sampling & Size 15 marks
Explain the difference between probability and non-probability sampling, and why sample-size justification matters for a research project.
Marks guide: 15 marks: difference 8 · justification 7
Difference

Probability sampling (simple random, stratified, cluster) gives every unit a known chance of selection → generalisable inference. Non-probability (convenience, purposive, snowball) is pragmatic and used in qualitative/pilot work but limits generalisability.

Sample-size justification

Size is driven by the desired power (1−β), α, expected effect size and variability. Justifying it prevents under-powering (type II error, wasted effort) and over-sizing (waste); it is required by ethics committees and shows feasibility.

Q3 Ethics 15 marks
List the three core principles of research ethics and describe three concrete protections you would build into a study interviewing patients about a sensitive condition.
Marks guide: 15 marks: principles 6 · protections 9
Principles

Belmont principles: respect for persons (informed consent, autonomy), beneficence (maximise benefit, minimise harm), justice (fair subject selection & burden-sharing).

Protections
  • Voluntary informed consent in the participant's language, with right to withdraw.
  • Confidentiality: anonymise recordings/transcripts; secure, access-restricted storage.
  • Minimise harm: private interview setting, trained interviewer, referral to support services if distress; submit to IRB before starting.
