1. The Research Process & Question
Source: GKT 517 Course Notes — Concept, Problem, Objectives, RQ
🔄 The Research Process
  1. Identify a research problem (gap in knowledge/practice).
  2. Review literature; refine the problem.
  3. State research question(s) & objectives/hypotheses.
  4. Design the study & sampling.
  5. Collect & analyse data.
  6. Interpret, write up, disseminate, apply.
An iterative, not strictly linear, cycle — proposals often loop back after literature review.
Why the cycle is iterative, not linear ▼

Textbooks draw the research process as a straight line (problem → literature → question → design → data → write-up) for teaching purposes, but in practice each step feeds back on the ones before it. This matters for grading and for real projects because examiners expect you to show awareness of the looping, not just recite the list.

  • Literature review reshapes the problem. A student starting with "factors associated with poor glycaemic control" may discover, after reading 30 papers, that the construct has already been extensively studied for adherence but not for the specific sub-group of newly-diagnosed patients — the problem statement narrows accordingly.
  • Design reshapes the question. If a cohort design is infeasible within a one-year master's timeline, the RQ may need to shift from "does X cause Y over 5 years" to a cross-sectional "what is the association between X and Y at one point in time" — with the causal language removed.
  • Piloting reshapes the instrument, and sometimes the objectives. A pilot may reveal that a key exposure variable is too sensitive to collect via self-administered questionnaire and needs face-to-face administration, or that a proposed scale has floor/ceiling effects in the local population.
  • Ethics review can reshape sampling. An IRB/JEPeM committee may require an opt-in rather than opt-out consent procedure, which changes the expected response rate and therefore the sample-size calculation.

Gordis (Epidemiology) frames this as the constant interplay between "what we want to know" and "what we can feasibly and ethically measure" — a mismatch anywhere in the cycle should send you back a step, not forward with a flawed plan.

Exam tip: When asked to "describe the research process," draw or narrate it as a loop with explicit feedback arrows (literature ⇄ problem, design ⇄ question, pilot ⇄ instrument) rather than a numbered list only — this earns application marks beyond simple recall.
❓ From Problem to Research Question

A good RQ is focused, feasible, and researchable. It should arise from a clear problem statement and literature gap.

  • Quantitative RQ often asks about relationship / difference / frequency.
  • Qualitative RQ asks how / why / what is the experience.
  • Avoid vague or unanswerable questions.
A weak RQ dooms the project — spend time here before designing.
FINER and SMART criteria for a research question ▼

Two complementary checklists are commonly used to critique a draft RQ before committing to it:

FINER (Hulley et al.)What it checks
FeasibleAdequate subjects, technical expertise, time, money, manageable scope
InterestingIntellectually engaging to the investigator (sustains motivation through a long project)
NovelConfirms, refutes, or extends prior findings — not simple duplication
EthicalAmenable to a study that an ethics committee will approve
RelevantTo scientific knowledge, clinical/public-health practice, or future research/policy

Once FINER is satisfied, the RQ itself is often refined against SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound. In practice, "specific" and "measurable" are where most weak RQs fail — e.g. "Is smoking bad for health?" is neither specific (which health outcome? which population?) nor measurable as written.

Worked example of RQ refinement

Vague draft

"Does poor diet affect diabetes in Malaysia?"

Refined, FINER-checked RQ

"Among adults aged 30–65 years with type 2 diabetes attending a primary care clinic, what is the association between high dietary glycaemic index intake (assessed by validated FFQ) and poor glycaemic control (HbA1c ≥ 7%) within the past 6 months?"

Notice the refined version specifies population, exposure with a measurement method, outcome with a threshold, and a time frame — each addition closes off a source of ambiguity that would otherwise cause disagreement between researcher, supervisor and analyst about what is actually being tested.

PICO variants, and translating PICO into objectives ▼

PICO was originally developed for clinical questions about interventions; for observational aetiological or descriptive research the "I" is more naturally read as Exposure, giving the variant PECO (Population, Exposure, Comparison, Outcome). For prevalence/burden studies without a comparison group, some authors use PO only (Population, Outcome) plus a Time/Setting element, sometimes written PICOT or PEO.

Question typeFrameExample
Therapy/interventionPICODoes a text-message reminder (I) vs no reminder (C) improve medication adherence (O) in diabetic patients (P)?
Aetiology / risk factorPECOIs night-shift work (E) vs day work (C) associated with higher risk of hypertension (O) among nurses (P)?
Prevalence / descriptivePO(+T)What is the prevalence (O) of low back pain (O) among factory workers (P) in the past 12 months (T)?
Diagnostic accuracyPIROIn patients with suspected TB (P), how does GeneXpert (I=index test) compare with sputum culture (R=reference standard) for detecting infection (O)?

Turning PICO into SMART objectives

Each PICO element usually maps onto one clause of the objective statement. Using the diabetes-adherence example from PYQ 1:

Primary objective

"To determine the prevalence of, and factors associated with, non-adherence to oral hypoglycaemic medication among adults with type 2 diabetes attending [clinic] between [month] and [month] [year]."

Secondary objectives (examples)
  • To describe the socio-demographic profile of non-adherent patients.
  • To determine the association between health literacy and medication adherence.
  • To identify the most commonly reported reasons for non-adherence using the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8).

Note each objective is measurable (has a named variable/instrument), time-bound, and traceable directly back to a PICO element — an examiner marking an objectives section is essentially checking that every PICO component reappears somewhere in the objectives, and nothing appears in the objectives that wasn't in the PICO/RQ.

2. Literature Review, Protocol & Proposal
Source: GKT 517 Course Notes — Literature, Review, Protocol, Proposal
🔎 Literature Search & Management

A literature search is "a systematic, explicit, and reproducible method for identifying, evaluating and interpreting the existing body of recorded works" on a topic (Fink, 1998) — not simply a list of what you have read, but an analysis of what has been done.

  • Databases: PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, MyJurnal, Google Scholar (supplementary), plus grey literature (theses, conference proceedings) and organisation sites (WHO, MOH, CDC).
  • Build a search with MeSH / keywords, Boolean (AND/OR/NOT), truncation and wildcards, limits.
  • Manage references with EndNote / Mendeley / Zotero; use a PRISMA flow if systematic.
Document the search (databases, dates, terms, number of hits) for reproducibility — an examiner should be able to repeat your search and get the same papers.
Six-step literature-search workflow, purposes & search techniques ▼

Purposes of a literature search

Beyond "reading around the topic," a good search: provides an academic basis for the study; feeds the problem statement; supports choice of tools/methods/data-collection approach; identifies gaps and potential pitfalls; and demonstrates the researcher's skill at finding and critically appraising relevant work.

The six steps

  1. Choose a topic / define the RQ. Check the question is neither too broad ("What is the prevalence of hypertension in patients with diabetes?") nor too narrow ("What are the attitudes of the general population in [town] to healthy meal preparation during sahur for glycaemic control in T2DM patients?") — aim for a workable middle ground, and write down candidate keywords as you go.
  2. Identify scope & focus. Decide how many years to cover (5–10 years is a common rule of thumb, extended further back for foundational theory or validated tools) and which concepts from the RQ will structure the search (e.g. for "KAP and effect of Ramadan fasting on glycaemic control in T2DM," the concepts are Ramadan fasting, KAP, DM, glycaemic control).
  3. Select databases. Bibliographic (PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library for medicine; Scopus/Web of Science for multidisciplinary coverage; CINAHL for nursing/allied health), organisational websites, and grey literature (dissertations, conference proceedings) each have different strengths — no single database is complete.
  4. Conduct & track the search. Review abstracts carefully before retrieving full text; log the exact search string used per database; follow up reference lists of key papers ("citation chasing"); use EndNote/Mendeley to store results as you go.
  5. Critically review what you found. For each paper ask: is it relevant, is the study significant, what are its strengths/weaknesses, what methods/theories were used, could bias (emotional, funding, publication) affect the conclusions, who is the intended reader?
  6. Record & synthesise. Export to a reference manager, remove duplicates, then write the review as an argument — not a string of "X reported... Y reported..." descriptive sentences with no linkage. Plot a logical structure (sections/sub-headings) before drafting; write first, edit later.

Theoretical vs conceptual framework

A theoretical framework is an existing theory the researcher adopts to guide the study (e.g. the Theory of Planned Behaviour). A conceptual framework is something the researcher constructs — synthesising theoretical and empirical literature into a diagram of the variables/concepts specific to this study and how they relate. It is sometimes called "the soul of the research project": without one, a study lacks direction for the literature review, and lacks a basis for interpreting findings afterward. A typical conceptual framework diagram groups independent variables (e.g. socio-demographic factors, occupational factors, behavioural factors) feeding into the outcome of interest, with arrows showing hypothesised relationships — this becomes the map for both the literature review headings and later the analysis (which variables to adjust for).

Choosing which literature is "good" evidence ▼

Not all literature carries equal weight. Guideline articles from a known author in the field or from a relevant organisation/committee (e.g. WHO, NHLBI, a national ministry of health) usually carry stronger authority than a single small primary study. Journal articles are the most common and up-to-date source and typically undergo peer review (a quality guarantee); books are less current but useful for orienting overviews; conference proceedings may reveal work not yet published and who the leading practitioners are; government/international-agency reports can be valuable original sources but are often harder to obtain.

Whatever the source, apply critical appraisal: check study design appropriateness, sample and comparison group selection, potential confounders considered, and coherence of the reported findings with other literature — this is the same skill examined in the GET502/GET503 critical-appraisal (CASP) module, applied here to build your own literature review rather than to grade someone else's paper.

📝 Protocol & Research Proposal

The protocol is the detailed plan of the study (the 'rulebook'). The proposal justifies and seeks approval for it, and serves three purposes: it convinces others of the value of the research, it functions as a contract between student and supervisor/committee, and it is a planning tool that forces the student to think through feasibility before fieldwork begins.

  • Proposal sections: title, background/introduction, rationale, objectives, literature review, conceptual framework, methodology, flow chart, expected (dummy) results, Gantt chart & milestones, references.
  • A strong proposal shows feasibility, rigour and relevance.
Write the protocol before data collection — it is your quality guarantee and ethics-submission backbone.
Proposal sections in detail, methodology checklist, good vs poor proposals ▼

Title

Should attract attention in the fewest possible words while adequately describing the study. Avoid opening with "The study of...", avoid a question mark, and generally avoid naming the study design in the title (an RCT is a common exception).

Introduction vs Rationale vs Literature Review

These three are often confused but serve different purposes and lengths:

SectionLengthPurpose
IntroductionBrief, 3–6 pagesStates what question the project answers and why; overview of the problem and its burden; not in-depth — points made here may be repeated in the literature review but in more depth later.
RationaleShort paragraph(s)States the study in context, in the researcher's own words: what is known, why the RQ matters, what answers the study will provide.
Literature ReviewIn-depthCritically comments on and discusses findings for the selected project: depth on the disease/problem, its burden, gaps in knowledge, relevant methodological aspects (design, tools, statistical tests used elsewhere), and ends in a conceptual framework.

Methodology checklist (what examiners look for)

A complete methodology section addresses, in order: study design; reference/source/study population; sampling frame; inclusion & exclusion criteria; sample-size determination (with formula, values used, and reference for each parameter); sampling method; study area, time & duration; research tools/materials (with validity/reliability/pre-testing plan); operational definitions of key variables; data-collection procedure; intended statistical tests; ethical considerations.

Inclusion vs exclusion criteria: inclusion criteria are not simply "the opposite" of exclusion criteria. Inclusion defines who is eligible (demographic/clinical characteristics, accessible population); exclusion is applied parsimoniously to remove specific subsets likely to be lost to follow-up, unable to provide good data, or barred by ethics (e.g. a 5-year vitamin-D trial might exclude those planning to move away, those with language barriers preventing consent, or those with a history of kidney stones).

Expected results (dummy tables)

A proposal should include blank "dummy tables" showing exactly how results will be presented — e.g. Table 1: socio-demographic characteristics with columns for n(%) and Mean(SD); Table 2: factors associated with the outcome, showing columns for crude OR (95% CI), adjusted OR (95% CI) and p-value. Building these in advance forces the student to think through the analysis plan before data collection, and is one of the clearest signals to a supervisor that the design is internally consistent.

Good vs poor proposal

Good proposal
  • Clearly defined, FINER-checked research question
  • Objectives clearly specified and traceable to the RQ
  • Literature review that provides real background, not just a list
  • Conceptual framework & theoretical assumptions clearly stated
  • Appropriate design & methodology; preliminary/pilot data shown
  • Necessary resources realistically available
Poor proposal
  • Repeats an existing project with no novelty
  • Weak structure, poor language, no clear argument
  • Research too ambitious for the timeline/resources
  • Inadequate or copied literature review, no theoretical foundation
  • Methods unclear or inappropriate to the RQ
  • Missing or inconsistent references

The typical process after writing: protocol presentation → departmental approval → conduct study → write report → supervisor review & correction → submission → examined by (usually two) internal examiners → correction → award of degree. A realistic Gantt chart with milestones (e.g. fieldwork preparation, data collection, analysis, report writing/presentation) should accompany the proposal.

3. Study Design, Sampling & Sample Size
Source: GKT 517 Course Notes — Study Design, Sampling, Determination
🌳 Choosing a Design

Design follows the RQ (see GKT 502 module for the taxonomy). For a master's project, feasibility is decisive.

  • Aetiologic 'what causes?' → cohort / case-control.
  • Prevalence / burden → cross-sectional.
  • Intervention → RCT / quasi-experimental (if ethical/feasible).
  • Experience/meaning → qualitative (phenomenology, grounded theory).
Mixed-methods combine quantitative + qualitative to answer complementary questions.

Population hierarchy, and each sampling method worked through step-by-step ▼

The population hierarchy (define before you sample anything)

Every sampling plan should explicitly name five nested layers, narrowing from broadest to the actual data-collection list:

LayerMeaningWorked example
Reference / target populationTo whom the study results will be generalised/inferredAdults in Kota Bharu with diabetes mellitus
Source populationFrom where the sample will actually be drawnAdults with DM who received treatment at any health clinic in Kota Bharu
Study populationWho will actually be studied (feasibility-limited subset)Adults with DM treated at Klinik Kesihatan Bandar KB
Sampling frameThe concrete list of eligible people from the study populationRegister of patients fulfilling the study's inclusion/exclusion criteria at that clinic
SampleThe persons actually included and measuredThe n patients selected from the frame

Probability sampling — a known, non-zero chance of selection for every unit

1. Simple random sampling (SRS)

Every unit in the frame has an equal probability of selection. Best suited to a relatively small, homogeneous population with a sampling frame already available. Procedure: (1) identify the population layers & frame, (2) determine sample size, (3) assign a consecutive number to every unit in the frame, (4) generate the required number of unique random numbers (lottery, random-number table, or software), (5) select those units. Worked example: frame of 30 people, need 10 — number them 1–30, draw 10 unique random numbers (e.g. 5, 12, 13, 14, 17, 19, 20, 21, 26, 27), and those 10 numbered individuals become the sample. Advantage: minimal sampling bias if applied correctly. Disadvantage: requires the full frame in advance — infeasible for large or geographically spread populations.

2. Systematic sampling

Used when a complete frame cannot be listed in advance (e.g. patients who will attend an out-patient clinic over the next few weeks) — an anticipated frame size is used instead. Sampling interval k = population size ÷ sample size. Procedure: anticipate the frame size, determine sample size, calculate k, pick ONE random start number between 1 and k, then take every k-th unit onward. Worked example: anticipated frame = 30, sample needed = 10 → k = 30/10 = 3. Random start = patient #2 → select patients #2, #5, #8, #11, #14, #17, #20, #23, #26, #29. Advantage: cheap, fast, spreads the sample evenly, approximates SRS when done correctly. Disadvantage: biased if there is a periodic pattern in the list that coincides with the interval (e.g. every 7th patient always attends on a quiet Monday clinic).

3. Stratified sampling

The frame is divided into mutually-exclusive strata (e.g. ethnicity, sex, disease severity) and each stratum is sampled independently, guaranteeing that every sub-group is represented. Compare with plain SRS on a heterogeneous population (e.g. 15 blue-marked, 15 orange-marked people): by chance, SRS of 10 might draw 7 blue and only 3 orange — a sample that does not reflect the true 50:50 population split. Stratifying and sampling proportionately within each colour would guarantee representation. Two types: proportionate (sample size per stratum proportional to that stratum's share of the population) or disproportionate (deliberately over- or under-sampling a stratum, e.g. to ensure enough of a rare sub-group for stable estimates). Advantage: superior representativeness. Disadvantage: a separate frame is needed per stratum, more effort, potentially larger sample size and cost.

4. Cluster sampling

Naturally occurring groups ("clusters" — villages, schools, clinics) are identified, a sample of clusters is randomly selected, and everyone within a selected cluster is included (one-stage cluster sampling). Advantage: avoids the need for an individual-level sampling frame, cuts travel/administrative cost, easy to obtain a large sample. Disadvantage: higher sampling error than SRS (people within a cluster tend to be similar — the "design effect"), requires group-level information, and may under-represent diversity present in the wider frame.

5. Multistage sampling

Combines two or more of the above methods across sequential stages. Worked example: Stage 1 — randomly select 5 of 10 districts in a state using SRS; Stage 2 — randomly select 10 secondary schools within each selected district using SRS; Stage 3 — randomly select 2 classes within each selected school using cluster sampling. This is the standard approach for large national surveys where a full individual-level frame is never available.

Non-probability sampling — element inclusion probability cannot be calculated

MethodDescriptionTypical use
Convenience (grab / accidental / haphazard)Whoever is readily available is sampledPilot studies, quick exploratory work
Purposive (judgemental)Researcher deliberately selects participants believed to be information-richQualitative studies, key-informant interviews
QuotaLike stratified sampling but selection within each sub-group is non-random and based on convenience/judgement up to a target quotaMarket-research style surveys
SnowballExisting participants refer further participantsHidden/hard-to-reach or stigmatised populations (e.g. people who inject drugs)

Snowball sampling has three variants: linear (each participant gives exactly one referral, continued in a chain), exponential non-discriminative (each participant gives multiple referrals and all are recruited, so the sample grows exponentially), and exponential discriminative (each participant gives multiple referrals but only one is chosen to continue the chain). Non-probability methods are quick, inexpensive and convenient, but representativeness is unknown and bias risk is higher — appropriate for qualitative or exploratory work, not for prevalence estimates intended to generalise to a population.

📐 Sample Size & Power
  • Determined by: expected effect size, α, power (1−β, ≥0.80), variability, design.
  • Use formulas or software (OpenEpi, G*Power, PASS).
  • For proportions/means, precision (margin of error) may drive size when no hypothesis test.
Under-powered studies risk type II error; over-sized waste resources. Justify every assumption.
Why size matters, the 8 driving factors, and Type I/II error ▼

Sample size must be calculated for every objective, using values quoted from a cited literature source, and several plausible scenarios should be calculated before settling on the largest logical one. If too few subjects are recruited: findings cannot be generalised, real (clinically important) effects may fail to reach statistical significance (type II error), and the study becomes arguably unethical (participants exposed to research burden with no realistic chance of a useful answer). If too many: unnecessary participants are put at risk/burden, resources are wasted, and trivial, clinically unimportant differences may become "statistically significant" — also an ethical problem.

H0 True (no real effect)H0 False (real effect exists)
Reject H0 (+)Type I error, α (false positive)Power, 1−β (correct detection)
Do not reject H0 (−)Confidence level, 1−α (correct)Type II error, β (false negative)

Eight factors that generally increase required sample size: smaller α; higher desired power; smaller expected effect size (harder-to-detect differences need more subjects); population proportion closer to 50% (variance is maximal there); larger population standard deviation; smaller (tighter) precision/margin of error; higher anticipated dropout; and certain complex designs (e.g. cluster designs need a "design effect" multiplier).

Worked formulas: single mean, single proportion, two means, two proportions ▼

1. Estimating a single mean

Formula: n = (Z₁₋α/2 · σ / Δ)², where Z₁₋α/2 is the reliability coefficient (1.96 for 95% confidence, 2.58 for 99%), σ is the population SD from a previous study, and Δ is the desired precision (margin of error, one-sided).

Worked example

A researcher wants to estimate the population's systolic blood pressure with precision ±5 mmHg. A previous study reported SD = 15 mmHg. At 95% confidence: n = (1.96 × 15 / 5)² = (5.88)² ≈ 34.6 → 35 participants.

2. Estimating a single proportion

Formula: n = (Z₁₋α/2 / Δ)² · P(1−P), where P is the expected population proportion/prevalence from previous literature (use P = 0.5 if truly unknown, since this maximises P(1−P) and gives the most conservative/largest n).

Worked example

Estimating prevalence of hypertension, expected P = 30%, desired precision ±5%, 95% confidence: n = (1.96/0.05)² × 0.3 × 0.7 = 1536.64 × 0.21 ≈ 322.7 → 323 participants. If a 10% anticipated dropout is added: adjusted n = 323 / (1 − 0.10) ≈ 359 participants.

3. Comparing two independent means

Formula (equal or m:1 allocation ratio): n = [(m+1)/m] × (σ/Δ)² × (Z₁₋α/2 + Z₁₋β)², where m is the allocation ratio between groups, σ the pooled/previous SD, Δ the minimum detectable difference between groups, and Z₁₋β the power term (0.84 for 80% power, 1.28 for 90% power).

Worked example

Comparing resting heart rate between obese and non-obese adults (1:1 ratio), previous SD = 30 bpm, desired detectable difference = 10 bpm, α = 5% (two-tailed, Z=1.96), power = 80% (Z=0.84): n per group = [(1+1)/1] × (30/10)² × (1.96+0.84)² = 2 × 9 × 7.84 ≈ 141 → ≈141 per group (282 total), before dropout adjustment.

4. Comparing two independent proportions

Formula: n = [(m+1)/2m] × [P₁(1−P₁) + P₀(1−P₀)] / (P₁−P₀)² × (Z₁₋α/2 + Z₁₋β)², where P₀ is the proportion among controls/unexposed and P₁ the proportion among cases/exposed (in a cohort: P is disease risk in exposed vs unexposed; in a case-control: P is exposure prevalence in cases vs controls).

Worked example

30% of the general adult population has "good fitness level" (P₀ = 0.30); it is anticipated that only 20% of obese adults do (P₁ = 0.20). With 1:1 allocation, α = 5%, power = 80%: numerator = 0.2(0.8) + 0.3(0.7) = 0.16 + 0.21 = 0.37; denominator = (0.20−0.30)² = 0.01; n per group = 1 × (0.37/0.01) × (1.96+0.84)² = 37 × 7.84 ≈ 290 → ≈290 per group. (Software such as OpenEpi/G*Power/PASS is normally used for the exact figure and to sanity-check hand calculations.)

Adjusting for dropout

Adjusted n = calculated n ÷ (1 − anticipated dropout rate). Always state the assumed dropout rate and cite it or justify it from clinic/attrition data.

Animal studies: where no prior effect-size/SD data exist, the crude "resource equation" method may be used: E = (total animals) − (total groups), with an acceptable range of E between 10 and 20. E < 10 under-powers the study; E > 20 wastes animals with no further gain in power — an ethical (3Rs) as well as statistical consideration.

⚠️ Bias, Confounding & Interaction

When analysing epidemiological data, an observed association could be explained by three "alternative explanations" — chance (random error), bias (systematic error), or confounding — the three classic threats to internal validity. Interaction (effect modification) is a separate, genuine phenomenon rather than a threat to validity, describing when an effect truly differs across sub-groups.

Random errorSystematic error (bias)
Chance divergence of a measurement from the true value; reduced by repeated measurementMeasurements differ from the truth in a consistent direction; NOT reduced by repeating measurements — only by better design/conduct
Confounding can be adjusted for at the analysis stage if measured; selection and information bias generally cannot be fixed after data collection — they must be prevented by design.
Selection bias & Information (measurement) bias — types and prevention ▼

1. Selection bias

Present when individuals have different probabilities of being included in the study according to characteristics related to exposure and outcome — usually because comparison groups do not arise from the same study base. It arises from procedures used to select subjects, factors influencing participation, and factors influencing attrition (loss to follow-up).

2. Information (measurement) bias

The method of gathering information yields systematic errors in classifying exposure or outcome status — leading to misclassification.

Misclassification typeDefinitionEffect on association
Non-differentialError occurs equally across comparison groups (e.g. same exposure-recall error rate in cases and controls)Typically biases the estimate toward the null (underestimates a real association)
DifferentialError occurs to a greater extent in one group than the otherDirection unpredictable — can exaggerate, underestimate, or even reverse the association
Worked numeric example — non-differential misclassification biases toward the null

True data (no error): Exposed cases 300, exposed controls 250, unexposed cases 100, unexposed controls 150 → OR = (300×150)/(250×100) = 1.80.
After 10% random (non-differential) misclassification of exposure in both cases and controls: exposed cases 280, exposed controls 240, unexposed cases 120, unexposed controls 160 → OR = (280×160)/(240×120) = 1.56. The true OR of 1.80 is diluted toward 1.0 — this is the classic signature of non-differential misclassification.

Specific named biases

BiasMechanismPrevention
Recall biasCases (with the outcome) recall past exposure differently/more thoroughly than controlsVerify against records/objective markers; use cohort or nested case-control design where exposure was recorded before outcome occurred
Interviewer biasInterviewer fails to follow standard probing/skip rules, often unconsciously probing cases moreStructured questionnaires, quality assurance, mask interviewer to case/control status

Respondent biasParticipant's own report of outcome during follow-up is unreliableConfirm outcome objectively (e.g. hospital records)

General prevention of misclassification

  • Use objective (e.g. biological) markers or diagnostic techniques instead of self-report where feasible.
  • Use clear, validated operational definitions for exposure/outcome and any co-factors.
  • Quality-control instruments (calibration, structured questionnaires).
  • Formally assess accuracy and precision of the measuring instrument/device.
Confounding: criteria, detection, worked stratified-analysis examples & control ▼

Confounding occurs when a non-causal association between an exposure and outcome is observed because of the influence of a third variable. It is more likely in observational than experimental studies.

Three criteria for a confounder

  1. Associated with the exposure in the source population being studied (causally or non-causally).
  2. An independent (causal) risk factor for the outcome — not merely correlated with it.
  3. Not an intermediate step on the causal pathway between exposure and outcome (if it is, adjusting for it would wrongly remove part of the true effect — "over-adjustment").

Worked example 1 — alcohol, smoking and lung cancer

Crude case-control data suggest alcohol drinkers have higher odds of lung cancer: OR = 3.0. But smokers may also drink more, and smoking is a strong cause of lung cancer — smoking is a candidate confounder. Stratifying by smoking status:

StratumOR for alcohol–lung cancer
Among smokers only1.0
Among non-smokers only1.0
Crude (unstratified)3.0

Once smoking is held constant within each stratum, the alcohol–lung cancer association disappears (stratum-specific OR = 1.0 in both strata). The crude OR of 3.0 was therefore entirely confounded by smoking — the true (adjusted) association between alcohol and lung cancer is null.

Worked example 2 — energy intake, physical activity and heart disease

Crude OR for high vs low energy intake and heart disease = 0.9 (appears slightly protective). Stratified by physical-activity level, the stratum-specific OR is 1.5 in both the high-activity and low-activity strata. Here confounding by physical activity masked/underestimated a true 50% excess risk — the crude estimate (0.9) understated the true association (adjusted OR ≈ 1.5).

Rule of thumb & types of confounding effect

If the crude and adjusted estimates differ by ≥10%, the crude estimate is considered confounded and the adjusted value should be reported. Confounding can be classified as positive (crude estimate pulled away from the null, i.e. exaggerated — as in example 1), negative (crude estimate pulled toward the null, i.e. masked — as in example 2), or qualitative (confounding is severe enough to reverse the direction of the association across 1.0).

Residual confounding can persist even after adjustment — from unmeasured confounders, confounders measured with error (misclassification), or confounder categories that are too crude/improperly defined.
Interaction (effect modification): definition and how it differs from confounding ▼

Interaction (also called effect modification) is present when ≥2 risk factors modify each other's effect on the outcome — i.e. the effect of exposure A on outcome Y is not homogeneous across strata of a third variable Z (the "effect modifier"). This can also be framed as: the observed joint effect of A and Z differs from what would be expected if their individual effects simply combined (additively or multiplicatively) — a deviation termed "statistical interaction."

ConfoundingInteraction
NatureA nuisance/bias to be removed to get the "true" single associationA real phenomenon to be reported, not removed
What you reportA single adjusted estimate (confounder is "controlled away")Separate stratum-specific estimates (e.g. effect in men vs women), because a single pooled number would hide real biological/behavioural heterogeneity
DetectionCrude vs adjusted estimate differ by ≥10%Stratum-specific estimates differ meaningfully from each other (test for statistical interaction, e.g. an interaction term in a regression model)

Interaction can be assessed on an additive scale (effect-measure modification using risk difference — relevant to linear/additive models, and to public-health impact) or a multiplicative scale (effect-measure modification using risk/odds ratio — relevant to logistic regression). It is possible for two factors to show interaction on one scale but not the other, so the scale used should be stated explicitly when reporting effect modification.

Exam tip: if asked to distinguish confounding from interaction, lead with — "confounding is a bias to eliminate via adjustment; interaction is a genuine effect to describe via stratum-specific results" — then support with the homogeneity-of-effects definition and note both are assessed via stratified analysis, but interpreted oppositely.

Plan who collects, how, when, and quality control (training, piloting, double-entry, SOPs). Data-collection techniques let us systematically gather information about the objects of study and their settings — haphazard collection makes it impossible to answer the RQ conclusively.

Choice of method is driven by the study objective, the definition/operationalisation of each variable, prior literature, and the best available reliable and valid tool. Variables must be clearly operationalised (e.g. "obesity" needs a stated cut-off — WHO vs Asian BMI classification — because different cut-offs give different prevalence).

Response rate & selection bias in data collection

Non-response is itself a form of selection bias if non-responders differ systematically from responders (e.g. sicker or busier people less likely to reply). Response-rate maximisation strategies include an introductory letter, advance notice, reminder/follow-up postcards or calls, and small incentives.

Case Report Form (CRF)

A structured data-collection tool (common in clinical trials) designed to capture all protocol-required information, including adverse events. A well-designed CRF simplifies database design, data validation and later statistical analysis — the layout should be logical, unambiguous and pre-coded wherever possible to speed data entry.

Ethical issues specific to data collection

Potential harms include violating privacy through sensitive questions, accessing records containing personal data, observing behaviour without awareness/consent, and failing to respect cultural values or taboos. Mitigations: obtain informed consent, avoid unnecessary probing into sensitive areas, ensure confidentiality of data obtained, understand the culture of informants beforehand, and use anonymous or coded responses for sensitive items.

Classical 3Cs, modern unitary validity (5 sources of evidence), and reliability ▼

Measurement is the process of observing and recording; validity is accuracy (are we measuring the right thing) while reliability is precision/consistency/reproducibility (would we get the same answer if repeated). A tool can be reliable without being valid — e.g. a weighing scale that consistently reads 2 kg too heavy is highly reliable (consistent) but not valid (inaccurate).

Classical "3Cs" view (Fletcher, Fletcher & Wagner; Streiner & Norman)

TypeSub-typeMeaning
Content validity—How well the tool covers the concept — judged on relevance, coverage, and representativeness of items
Criterion validityConcurrentCorrelates with a gold-standard/criterion measured at the SAME time
Criterion validityPredictiveCorrelates with a gold-standard/criterion measured LATER
Construct validityConvergentCorrelates with another tool measuring the SAME concept
Construct validityDiscriminantDoes NOT correlate strongly with a tool measuring a DIFFERENT concept

Modern unified view — validity as one construct with 5 sources of evidence (AERA/APA/NCME Standards)

Contemporary psychometrics treats validity as a single unitary concept (sometimes just called "construct validity") — the degree of evidence supporting the intended interpretation/use of a tool, drawn from five possible sources:

  1. Content — relevance, coverage and representativeness of items relative to the concept.
  2. Internal structure — correlatedness and dimensionality among items, examined via factor analysis (exploratory/confirmatory) and reliability statistics.
  3. Relations to other variables — convergent/discriminant evidence (vs other tools) and test-criterion relationships (concurrent/predictive, as in the classical view).
  4. Response process — evidence about how respondents actually interpret and answer the items, gathered via cognitive debriefing (asking respondents to explain how they arrived at their answer).
  5. Consequences — evidence about the intended and unintended consequences of using the tool's results (e.g. a person scored as "depressed" may be referred for treatment — intended — or may lose their job if the result is disclosed — unintended).

Reliability

Reliability is commonly quantified via internal consistency (e.g. Cronbach's alpha), test-retest reliability, and inter-rater reliability (e.g. kappa). Improve reliability through standardised administration, clear operational definitions, rater training, and (for translated tools) rigorous cross-cultural adaptation.

Questionnaire translation & cross-cultural adaptation (7 stages) ▼

When adopting an instrument developed in another language/culture, five aspects of equivalence must be established between the original and translated versions (Streiner & Norman, 2008): conceptual (do both populations understand the underlying concept similarly — e.g. "spanking" may be understood as discipline in one culture and as abuse in another), item (are the specific items relevant/acceptable locally — e.g. "using a furnace" is irrelevant in a tropical country and should be dropped or replaced), semantic (does the translated wording carry the same meaning — e.g. "I get butterflies in my stomach" translated too literally becomes meaningless, so it should be adapted to a locally natural phrase for anxiety), operational (same format/mode of administration — direct vs indirect question style, self- vs interviewer-administered), and measurement (equivalent psychometric properties — factor structure, reliability — in the new population).

The 7-stage translation process (adapted from Beaton et al. 2000; Wild et al. 2005)

  1. Forward translation — original language → target language, by two independent translators (one medical, one lay/non-medical), both native speakers of the target language.
  2. Reconciliation — a translation panel (study team + both forward translators + a linguist) combines the two forward translations into one version.
  3. Backward translation — target language → original language, again by two independent translators, both competent in the original language.
  4. Harmonization — compare the back-translated version(s) against the true original; discrepancies flag difficult words/concepts; the panel revises the forward translation accordingly, producing a preliminary version.
  5. Pre-survey evaluation — the same steps as for a newly developed questionnaire (expert review, FGD, cognitive debriefing) applied to the translated version, catching mistakes introduced during translation.
  6. Finalization — panel reviews pre-survey comments and fine-tunes the final version.
  7. Full validation survey — administered to a large sample (a few hundred), generating internal-structure and relations-to-other-variables evidence for the translated tool — this is often reported as a stand-alone validation paper before the translated tool is used in the main study.

Five design objectives for quality responses (McDonald et al., 2003)

  1. Consistent meaning — the item should mean the same thing to the researcher and every respondent: use simple, unambiguous words; avoid "OR" combining two concepts (double-barrelled items); avoid vague frequency terms ("kadang-kadang"/"sometimes" vs "jarang-jarang"/"rarely") — specify exact frequency or timeframe instead, and avoid overlapping categories ("within 1 year" vs "within 2 years").
  2. Expected response — be specific about the form of answer wanted; an open "When?" could mean age, date, or duration — restate as a specific, closed item if precision matters.
  3. Ability to respond — only ask what the respondent can realistically recall/know, e.g. "how many cigarettes have you smoked in the past 10 years?" is beyond most people's recall ability and should be reframed (e.g. pack-years via simpler sub-questions).
  4. Willingness to respond — avoid items inviting socially desirable (dishonest) answers, e.g. "Have you ever robbed anyone?" — use validated, less confrontational item wording, anonymity, or indirect questioning techniques for sensitive topics.
  5. Same type, mode & method of administration for all respondents — standardise how interviewers ask and how respondents answer (all self-administered vs all interviewer-administered; consistent format/layout) so responses are comparable.

Pre-survey evaluation (before full fieldwork)

  1. Expert/researcher review — covers the bulk of content-validity evidence.
  2. Focus group discussion (FGD) — representative sample of the target population gives general feedback on comprehension of key/problematic items.
  3. Cognitive debriefing — individual interviews probing how respondents understand and arrive at answers; more intensive than an FGD.
  4. Field pretest / pilot study — tests the complete instrument on a representative sample, evaluating the form (wording, formatting, logical flow), the administration process (length, timing, interviewer training/reliability, logistics), and data-entry preparation (coding scheme, item non-response rate).
Demographics are typically placed first or last, sensitive items placed later once rapport is established, and item order may be randomised where response-order effects are a concern.

Writing the statistical-analysis section of a proposal

Cover, in order: (1) data entry — software name, purpose, version; data validation (check, clean, explore); how missing data will be managed; listing of independent/dependent variables; data-coding scheme (e.g. 0/1); (2) descriptive statistics — how the data/variables will be described, how normality will be checked, and the reporting convention (categorical: n (%); numerical: mean (SD) if normal, median (IQR) if skewed); (3) inferential statistics — name the specific test, name independent & dependent variables with their type (numerical/categorical, parametric/non-parametric), briefly explain the analytical steps, and state how results will be interpreted (significance level, e.g. p<0.05) and reported (percentages to 1 decimal place, p-values to 3 decimal places, means/SD/95% CI to 2 decimal places).

Dummy tables (three-line format: title above the table, footnotes below) should be prepared in advance for both descriptive statistics (e.g. socio-demography) and inferential results (e.g. crude OR, adjusted OR, 95% CI, p-value columns) — this operationalises the whole analysis plan before a single data point is collected.

Referencing (Harvard style) — full citation rules and worked examples ▼

A reference is an acknowledgement that ideas, data or wording were drawn from another (usually published) source. Cite whenever you: state a theory/argument/viewpoint that is not your own; cite statistics, examples or case studies; describe a method directly based on another author's work; closely paraphrase a passage; or use a direct quotation (sparingly).

TermMeaning
ReferenceThe full bibliographic detail of a cited source, listed at the end of the document
In-text citationThe short marker in the body text pointing to a reference, e.g. (Zahir, 2022) or superscript/numbered (1)

Two generic referencing families

SystemDescription
Harvard (author–date)Parenthetical author-date citations in text; reference list ordered alphabetically by author surname. The style used for research proposals/theses here is the Harvard referencing style, based on APA format.
Vancouver (numbered)Numbered citations (in parentheses or superscript) in text; reference list ordered by citation number — common in health-science journals.

In-text citation rules

  • Two authors: if part of the sentence, use "and" — "Zahiruddin and Naing (2002) described..."; if not part of the sentence, use "&" in brackets — "(Zahiruddin & Naing, 2002)".
  • More than two authors (APA format): name only the first author followed by et al. (italicised) — "(Wilson et al., 1997)".
  • More than 20 authors (APA 7th): list the first 19 authors, insert an ellipsis (…), then the last author's name. (Note: EndNote cannot automate this — edit manually.)
  • Multiple references cited together: order chronologically, separated by semicolons — "(Smith, 1999; Jones et al., 2001; Turner & William, 2006)".
  • Same author, same year, multiple works: distinguish with lower-case letters — "Johnson (1994a)" is listed before "Johnson (1994b)".
  • No individual author: attribute to the organisation, e.g. "WHO (2013)"; never use "Anonymous". If truly no author exists, use the first few words of the title in quotes, e.g. ("New Child Vaccine," 2001).
  • Personal communications (letters, emails, interviews, lectures): cite in text only, never in the reference list — "(J. D. Black, personal communication, May 30, 2013)".
  • Indirect/secondary citation (you read author B citing author A, but never read A directly): cite both — "Miller (1953, as cited in Agrios, 2005) found…" — and list only the source you actually read (Agrios) in the reference list.
  • Placement: cite at the point the idea appears in the text — before the full stop if not grammatically part of the sentence, e.g. "…higher risk of infection (Zahiruddin & Naing, 2002)."

EndNote basics: building a library, Cite While You Write, and common workflows ▼

Reference-management software (EndNote, Mendeley, Zotero) removes the manual burden of tracking, formatting, and reformatting hundreds/thousands of citations to match each journal or thesis style — formatting is otherwise "time-consuming" and error-prone as the reference list grows. EndNote in particular ships with 4,000+ predefined bibliographic output styles.

Getting references into an EndNote library

  • Search & import from PubMed — search with keywords/Boolean logic, tick the desired records, use "Citation manager" to send them to an open EndNote library (or save as a .nbib file and import).
  • Export from subscribed databases — Scopus, Web of Science, ProQuest, Ovid etc. usually offer an "Export to EndNote/RIS" option.
  • Export directly from Google Scholar — set Scholar Preferences to show "Import into EndNote" links (imported references often need manual formatting clean-up).
  • Import PDF files — EndNote can extract reference metadata automatically via the PDF's DOI; without a DOI, the reference still imports but needs manual field editing.
  • Online search directly from EndNote — connect to a source (e.g. PubMed/NLM) and search without leaving the program.
  • Manual entry — required for sources with no online record (unpublished theses, internal reports); choose the correct reference type first (journal, book, report, etc.) so the output style formats it correctly.

Cite While You Write (CWYW)

CWYW is EndNote's Word plug-in: position the cursor exactly where the citation belongs (before the full stop if not part of the sentence), select the reference(s) in EndNote, and insert — the in-text citation and the full end-of-document bibliography update automatically. Switching the "Output style" (e.g. APA 6th vs the Harvard university version) instantly reformats every citation and the whole reference list. Use "Edit Citation" to convert a default "(Author, Year)" citation into a sentence-integrated "Author (Year)" form, or to add page numbers/suppress author names for a paraphrase-in-narrative style.

📝 Sample Questions (with model answers)
Practice questions in the style of GKT 517 past-year papers
Q1 Question & Design 15 marks
A master's student wants to study factors associated with non-adherence to diabetes medication in a clinic. (a) Frame a PICO question. (b) Suggest a suitable design and justify briefly.
Marks guide: 15 marks: PICO 8 · design 7
PICO

P: adults with type 2 diabetes attending the clinic. I: non-adherent (vs adherent) to oral hypoglycaemics. C: adherent patients (referent). O: prevalence/odds of non-adherence and associated factors (e.g., forgetfulness, cost, knowledge).

Design

A cross-sectional study is suitable to estimate the prevalence of non-adherence and its correlates efficiently; if testing specific causal predictors with temporality, a case-control (non-adherent vs adherent) could explore multiple factors. Cohort is less feasible for a master's timeline.

Q2 Sampling & Size 15 marks
Explain the difference between probability and non-probability sampling, and why sample-size justification matters for a research project.
Marks guide: 15 marks: difference 8 · justification 7
Difference

Probability sampling (simple random, stratified, cluster) gives every unit a known chance of selection → generalisable inference. Non-probability (convenience, purposive, snowball) is pragmatic and used in qualitative/pilot work but limits generalisability.

Sample-size justification

Size is driven by the desired power (1−β), α, expected effect size and variability. Justifying it prevents under-powering (type II error, wasted effort) and over-sizing (waste); it is required by ethics committees and shows feasibility.

Q3 Ethics 15 marks
List the three core principles of research ethics and describe three concrete protections you would build into a study interviewing patients about a sensitive condition.
Marks guide: 15 marks: principles 6 · protections 9
Principles

Belmont principles: respect for persons (informed consent, autonomy), beneficence (maximise benefit, minimise harm), justice (fair subject selection & burden-sharing).

Protections
  • Voluntary informed consent in the participant's language, with right to withdraw.
  • Confidentiality: anonymise recordings/transcripts; secure, access-restricted storage.
  • Minimise harm: private interview setting, trained interviewer, referral to support services if distress; submit to IRB before starting.
