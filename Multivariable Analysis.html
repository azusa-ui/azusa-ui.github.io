1. GLM · Multiway (Factorial) ANOVA

The General Linear Model extends the humble one-way ANOVA to two or more categorical factors at once, letting you test main effects and whether the factors interact in how they influence a numerical outcome.

Concept

Why not just run separate one-way ANOVAs?

A one-way ANOVA lets you compare group means for a single grouping variable (a "factor") with two or more levels — e.g., drug dose. But most real-world public health questions involve more than one grouping variable acting on an outcome simultaneously (Course lecture slides, Multiway Factorial ANOVA). Running separate one-way ANOVAs for each factor throws away important information:

  • The factors may share variance, so separate tests waste information and inflate the overall Type I error rate (Course lecture slides, Multiway Factorial ANOVA).
  • Most importantly, separate one-way tests cannot detect interaction (moderation) effects — whether the effect of one IV depends on the level of another IV (Course lecture slides, Multiway Factorial ANOVA).
  • Factorial ANOVA also reduces error variance by partitioning it among the additional factors, which is part of why it is considered one of the more elegant and powerful classical designs (Factorial ANOVA notes, IDST6122 Assignment #2).
Definition — Factor, level, factorial design. A factor is a categorical independent variable; a level is one of its categories (Factorial ANOVA notes, IDST6122 Assignment #2). Designs are named by "A × B" notation: the number of numbers = number of factors, and each number = the number of levels of that factor. A 2 × 3 design has 2 factors, one with 2 levels and one with 3 levels, giving 2 × 3 = 6 cells/groups (Course lecture slides; Factorial ANOVA notes).

Main effects vs. interaction effects

A main effect is the direct/independent effect of one factor on the DV, averaged (marginalised) across the levels of the other factor(s) (Course lecture slides, Multiway Factorial ANOVA; Main Effects and Interactions, supplementary reading). An interaction effect is the joint effect of two or more factors — it tells you whether the effect of one IV on the DV changes depending on the level of another IV (Course lecture slides). Regression models need an explicit product term to model interaction; ANOVA/GLM tests it "on a built-in basis" as soon as you request the interaction term (Course lecture slides).

Because the interaction is significant, the simple main effect of expectation is 15 IQ points among 7-year-olds but 0 points among 15-year-olds — the effect of one IV genuinely depends on the level of the other, which is exactly what "interaction" means.

Types of factors and designs

  • Independent-samples (between-subjects) design — each factor's levels represent different subjects, randomly allocated (Factorial ANOVA notes; Course lecture slides).
  • Repeated-measures (within-subjects) design — the same subjects are measured under every level of a factor (e.g., baseline, 1-month, 3-month diet weight) (Factorial ANOVA notes).
  • Mixed design — one factor is between-subjects, another is within-subjects (Factorial ANOVA notes; Course lecture slides). This topic (Topic 1) covers the fully independent-samples factorial ANOVA; repeated-measures and mixed models are covered separately (Course lecture slides, Multiway Factorial ANOVA).
Fixed vs. random factors. A factor is fixed if it includes all levels of interest (e.g., sex: male/female). A factor is random if its levels are an arbitrary sample from a larger population of possible levels (e.g., hospital size categories sampled from all hospitals) — random factors let you generalise beyond the specific levels studied but add extra variance to the model, and mistakenly treating a random factor as fixed inflates the Type I error rate (Factorial ANOVA notes, IDST6122 Assignment #2). Most ANOVA designs taught in this course, and used here, are fixed-effects models.

N-way ANOVA and the proliferation of interactions

Two IVs give one first-order interaction (A×B). Three IVs give three first-order interactions (AB, AC, BC) plus one second-order interaction (ABC) — four terms in all. Four IVs give six first-order, three second-order, and one third-order interaction — ten terms in all (Course lecture slides, Multiway Factorial ANOVA). As the number of factors grows, interpretation quickly becomes unwieldy, which is why higher-order (3-way+) factorial ANOVA is used sparingly in practice, and why the golden rule is: higher-order effects supersede lower-order ones — always interpret the highest-order significant interaction first (Course lecture slides).

Assumptions

Assumptions of factorial ANOVA (Course lecture slides, Multiway Factorial ANOVA):
  1. DV is numerical/continuous (interval or ratio level).
  2. DV is approximately normally distributed within each cell/group — ANOVA is fairly robust to violations of this if sample sizes are reasonably large (check via residual analysis).
  3. Homogeneity of variance — equal variance of the DV across all cells (check with Levene's test; if unequal, note it descriptively and consider adjusted post-hoc procedures).
  4. Cases represent random samples from the population(s) of interest.
  5. Independence of observations (for the independent/between-subjects model).

Hypotheses in a two-way ANOVA

Unlike one-way ANOVA's single hypothesis pair, a two-way ANOVA tests at least three sets of hypotheses (Factorial ANOVA notes, IDST6122 Assignment #2):

  • H0₁ / HA₁ — no main effect / a main effect of Factor A
  • H0₂ / HA₂ — no main effect / a main effect of Factor B
  • H0₃ / HA₃ — no interaction / an A × B interaction effect

General analytic workflow (as taught in the course)

Step 1 Data exploration / descriptive statistics
Step 2 Univariate screening tests (independent t-test / one-way ANOVA per factor)
Step 3 Fit the main-effects model
Step 4 Add and check the interaction term
Step 5 Check model assumptions (5a normality of residuals; 5b Levene's test for equal variance)
Step 6 Post-hoc / pairwise comparisons and estimated marginal means (EMM) — run stratified/simple main effects analysis if the interaction is significant
Step 7 Interpretation and data presentation (APA tables, plots, narrative)
(Course lecture slides, Multiway Factorial ANOVA)

Effect size

SPSS reports effect size for ANOVA terms as partial eta-squared (η²ₚ), requested under Options → Estimates of effect size. It is interpreted like R² and reported after the p-value (Course lecture slides, Multiway Factorial ANOVA):

η²ₚ = SSeffect / (SSeffect + SSerror)

Cohen's rule-of-thumb benchmarks for η² (Course lecture slides, citing Cohen; Miles & Shevlin, 2001): 0.01 = small effect, 0.06 = medium effect, 0.14 = large effect.

For the Corrected Model as a whole, R² (and adjusted R²) are reported and interpreted the same way as an overall effect size; adjusted R² is preferred when comparing model fitness across models with different numbers of terms (Course lecture slides).

R walkthrough

Illustrated using the course's Alcohol × Sleep demo variables (Score, Alcohol, Sleep) from Model1.sav.

1
Import the data and set factors. Read the SPSS file and make sure the two IVs are coded as factors (not numeric). 
library(haven)     # read .sav
library(car)       # Anova(), leveneTest()
library(emmeans)   # estimated marginal means & simple effects
library(ggplot2)   # profile plots

dat <- read_sav("Model1.sav")
dat$Alcohol <- as_factor(dat$Alcohol)   # "Yes"/"No"
dat$Sleep   <- as_factor(dat$Sleep)     # "With"/"Without"
str(dat)
2
Descriptive statistics and a profile (interaction) plot. 
aggregate(Score ~ Alcohol + Sleep, data = dat,
          FUN = function(x) c(M = mean(x), SD = sd(x), n = length(x)))

ggplot(dat, aes(x = Sleep, y = Score, color = Alcohol, group = Alcohol)) +
  stat_summary(fun = mean, geom = "point", size = 3) +
  stat_summary(fun = mean, geom = "line") +
  labs(title = "Profile plot: Alcohol x Sleep on performance score")
3
Fit the factorial ANOVA (GLM) with the interaction term. Use Type III sums of squares (matches SPSS default) via car::Anova(), after setting orthogonal contrasts. 
options(contrasts = c("contr.sum", "contr.poly"))  # required for correct Type III SS

model <- aov(Score ~ Alcohol * Sleep, data = dat)
Anova(model, type = 3)   # main effects + interaction, Type III SS

summary.lm(model)$r.squared        # R^2
summary.lm(model)$adj.r.squared    # adjusted R^2
4
Check assumptions. Levene's test for homogeneity of variance and Shapiro-Wilk on the model residuals for normality. 
leveneTest(Score ~ Alcohol * Sleep, data = dat)   # H0: equal variances
shapiro.test(residuals(model))                    # H0: residuals normal
hist(residuals(model))                            # visual check, per course guidance
If Levene's test is non-significant (p > .05), the equal-variance assumption is reasonably supported; the course notes that factorial ANOVA is fairly robust to moderate violations, but a variance ratio (largest/smallest) > 3 in balanced designs warrants transformation or a robust alternative (Factorial ANOVA notes, IDST6122 Assignment #2).
5
Effect sizes (partial η²). 
library(effectsize)
eta_squared(model, partial = TRUE)
6
Post-hoc / estimated marginal means, and — critically — simple main effects if the interaction is significant. Because the Alcohol × Sleep interaction is significant here, do not stop at the main-effect means; stratify one factor by the other. 
# Overall EMMs and main-effect pairwise comparisons (reported, but "with less interest" once interaction is sig.)
emmeans(model, pairwise ~ Alcohol, adjust = "bonferroni")
emmeans(model, pairwise ~ Sleep,   adjust = "bonferroni")

# Simple main effects: effect of Alcohol WITHIN each level of Sleep
emm_int <- emmeans(model, ~ Alcohol | Sleep)
emm_int
pairs(emm_int, adjust = "bonferroni")     # simple main effect tests + Bonferroni CIs

# equivalently, the joint (omnibus) simple-effect F-tests:
joint_tests(model, by = "Sleep")
This mirrors the two SPSS methods taught in the course: (1) splitting the file by one factor and re-running a one-way ANOVA per stratum, or (2) modifying the GLM /EMMEANS syntax to request the simple main effects directly (Course lecture slides, Multiway Factorial ANOVA).

Request options. Click Options…: move Alcohol, Sleep, and Alcohol*Sleep into Display Means for; check Compare main effects and set the Confidence interval adjustment drop-down to Bonferroni; under Display, check Descriptive statistics, Estimates of effect size, Observed power, and Homogeneity tests (this last option runs Levene's test); click Continue, then OK (Course lecture slides; Factorial ANOVA notes).

Read the output in order. Between-Subjects Factors table (N per level) → Descriptive Statistics per cell → Levene's Test of Equality of Error Variances (want p > .05) → "Tests of Between-Subjects Effects" (the ANOVA source table: Alcohol, Sleep, Alcohol*Sleep, Error, each with SS, df, MS, F, Sig., partial η², observed power) → Estimated Marginal Means with pairwise comparisons for each main effect → an Alcohol*Sleep interaction (cell) means table → the profile plot(s) (Course lecture slides; Factorial ANOVA notes). Always interpret the interaction row first.

If the interaction is significant, run simple main effects. Two supported methods (Course lecture slides, Multiway Factorial ANOVA):

Method 1 — Split file: Data → Split File → Compare groups, select Sleep as the grouping variable → OK. Re-run Analyze → General Linear Model → Univariate with Score as DV and only Alcohol as Fixed Factor (i.e., a one-way ANOVA run separately within each Sleep stratum), applying Bonferroni post-hoc if Alcohol has 3+ levels. Remember to turn Split File back off afterwards. This is simple but reduces the per-analysis sample size and error df, costing statistical power (Course lecture slides).

Method 2 — Modify the GLM syntax (preferred; preserves full sample size and power): Re-open Analyze → General Linear Model → Univariate, click Options…, move Alcohol into Display Means for, check Compare main effects, select Bonferroni, then click Paste instead of OK. This opens a Syntax window containing: 
UNIANOVA

Then Run → All. Interpreting a significant interaction (stratified)

Golden rule. When the interaction effect is statistically significant, the main effects are not interpreted in isolation — "one should never interpret the main effects as having an independent impact on the dependent or outcome variable" when a significant interaction is present (Factorial ANOVA notes, IDST6122 Assignment #2; consistent guidance in Course lecture slides). Instead, the effect of one factor is interpreted stratified by (conditional on) the level of the other factor — i.e., via simple main effects analysis.

The course's own decision rule (Course lecture slides, Multiway Factorial ANOVA):

  • Neither main effect nor the interaction is significant → no simple-effects or post-hoc testing at all.
  • Both main effects and the interaction are significant → run simple-effects analysis and post-hoc testing on the significant main effects (with less interest) and especially on the interaction (which better explains the pattern).
  • A main effect is significant but the interaction is not → post-hoc testing on the significant main effect(s) only.

Worked stratified interpretation — Alcohol effect stratified by Sleep status

Using the course's own Alcohol × Sleep demonstration dataset (Model1.sav), the interaction term was statistically significant, F(1, 28) = 9.29, p = .005, partial η² = .25 (Table ANOVA 2way with interactions doc; Course lecture slides). Following the mandatory next step, the simple main effect of Alcohol was tested separately within each level of Sleep (pooled error term from the full model, MSE = 0.084, error df = 28):

Narrative interpretation (not interpreting the main effects in isolation): Among participants who had a normal night's sleep, alcohol consumption was associated with a significantly lower performance score than no alcohol, F(1, 28) = 7.61, p = .010 (mean difference = −0.40). However, among participants who had missed a night's sleep, the deleterious effect of alcohol on performance was substantially larger, F(1, 28) = 49.97, p < .001 (mean difference = −1.03) — more than double the effect size seen under normal sleep. In other words, alcohol impaired performance in both sleep conditions, but sleep deprivation appears to amplify alcohol's negative effect on performance, which is precisely the pattern that produced the significant Alcohol × Sleep interaction. APA-style sentence template for a significant interaction (Course lecture slides, Multiway Factorial ANOVA): "The main effect of [IV] on [DV] was significant, F(df, df) = x.xx, p < .xxx, but this was qualified by an interaction between [IV1] and [IV2], F(df, df) = x.xx, p < .xxx. When [IV1] was [level a], [IV2]a (M = x.xx, SD = x.xx) was higher/lower than [IV2]b (M = x.xx, SD = x.xx). However, when [IV1] was [level b], [IV2]a was [pattern] than [IV2]b."

A second, independently-sourced illustration of the same principle

The "Main Effects and Interactions" supplementary reading provides an independent worked example using teacher expectations (High/Normal) × student age (7 vs 15 years) on IQ score (Main Effects and Interactions, supplementary reading). The interaction was significant, F(1, 36) = 10.125, p = .003. Stratified (simple main effect) tests showed: for seven-year-olds, high teacher expectations led to significantly higher IQ scores than normal expectations, F(1, 36) = 20.25, p < .001 (mean difference = 15.0 points); for fifteen-year-olds, teacher expectations had no significant effect, F(1, 36) = 0.00, p = 1.000 (mean difference ≈ 0). This is a textbook demonstration of why the main effect of "expectation" cannot be interpreted as a single, constant effect once the interaction is significant — its effect is present only at one level of age and absent at the other.

What if the interaction is not significant?

If the interaction term is not statistically significant, the course guidance is to re-run the model as a main-effects-only (additive) model and interpret each significant main effect independently, using its overall marginal/estimated marginal means and standard post-hoc pairwise comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni) — no stratification is required (Course lecture slides, Multiway Factorial ANOVA). The APA 7 "Two-way ANOVA without Interaction" example illustrates this reporting style: with a non-significant Instruction × Sex interaction, results are reported simply as "Males demonstrated greater achievement (M = 85.17, SD = 5.42) than females (M = 80.33, SD = 7.09). Each pairwise comparison among instructional types is statistically significant" (APA 7 Two way ANOVA without Interaction), i.e., both main effects are described on their own without any need for stratified sub-analysis.

2. GLM · Multiway ANCOVA

Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) extends the (M)ANOVA framework of Topic 1 by statistically "adjusting" group comparisons for one or more continuous covariates, and — because it fuses ANOVA with regression — it carries its own signature assumption, homogeneity of regression slopes, that must be checked before any adjusted-mean comparison is trusted.

Concept

What is ANCOVA? Analysis of covariance is a General Linear Model (GLM) that combines factorial ANOVA with linear regression: group means on a continuous, interval/ratio dependent variable (DV) are compared after "removing" the linear effect of one or more continuous covariates (CV) from the DV. Formally, Y = b0 + bx·X + bz·Z + e, where X is the covariate and Z is the (dummy-coded) grouping factor (Course lecture slides, Multiway ANCOVA; SPSS Notes on GLM ANCOVA).

Why use ANCOVA

  • Increased precision/power — reduces within-group (error) variance by partialling out variance explained by the covariate, sharpening the "signal-to-noise ratio" (Course lecture slides, Multiway ANCOVA).
  • Adjustment for imbalance/confounding — "levels the playing field" between non-randomised or unbalanced groups by statistically equating them on the covariate before comparing outcome means (Course lecture slides, Multiway ANCOVA).
  • Particularly valuable in pre-test/post-test designs, where the pre-test score is entered as the covariate for the post-test DV (SPSS Notes on GLM ANCOVA).

Minimum model structure

ComponentRequirement
Dependent variable (DV)Continuous/interval-ratio; should be approximately normal and homoscedastic across groups
Factor(s) / IV(s)≥1 categorical grouping variable; a design with 2 factors + 1 covariate is termed a two-way ANCOVA (minimum of 4 variables needed for a factorial ANCOVA)
Covariate(s) (CV)Continuous, measured (ideally) before the intervention/exposure; decided a priori from theory/conceptual framework, not chosen post-hoc by "best fit"
 (Course lecture slides, Multiway ANCOVA / Factorial ANCOVA)
Rule of thumb for number of covariates: [C + (J − 1)] / N < 0.10, where C = number of covariates, J = number of groups, N = sample size — used to avoid over-fitting/overparameterising a small sample (Course lecture slides, Multiway ANCOVA).

Assumptions of the covariate and the ANCOVA model

  • Covariate is reliably/precisely measured, and ideally measured before group assignment or intervention (measurement error in X biases adjustment).
  • Covariate is at least moderately correlated with the DV (otherwise little is gained in precision).
  • Multiple covariates should not be highly correlated with each other (avoid multicollinearity).
  • Linear relationship between covariate and DV.
  • Independence between covariate and factor(s) — ideally satisfied by design when the covariate is measured before randomisation/treatment.
  • Homogeneity of regression slopes (a.k.a. parallelism) — the regression of the DV on the covariate is the same (parallel slope) in every group.
  • Usual GLM/ANOVA assumptions also apply: independent random sampling, normality of residuals, homogeneity of variance (HOV/Levene's test) (Course lecture slides, Multiway ANCOVA; SPSS Notes on GLM ANCOVA).
Homogeneity of regression slopes — the assumption unique to ANCOVA. This is tested by adding a covariate × factor interaction term to the model. If that interaction is statistically significant, the slope of the DV-on-covariate regression line differs across groups — i.e. the effect of the covariate on the DV depends on group membership. This is called violation of homogeneity of regression (HOR), and it means the "adjustment" performed by standard ANCOVA (which assumes one common slope) is no longer valid, because a single adjusted mean cannot meaningfully summarise group differences that themselves vary with the covariate (Course lecture slides, Multiway ANCOVA; SPSS Notes on GLM ANCOVA).
Two different "interactions" in a factorial ANCOVA — do not confuse them:
  1. Factor × Factor interaction (only relevant when there are ≥2 factors, i.e. a genuine two-way/factorial ANCOVA) — interpreted exactly as in factorial ANOVA: if significant, interpret simple main effects (stratified by the other factor), not the main effects in isolation.
  2. Covariate × Factor interaction — this is the homogeneity-of-regression-slopes check described above. It is always tested regardless of how many factors there are, and a significant result is a problem (assumption violation) requiring remedial action, not a substantive finding to report as-is.
(Course lecture slides, Multiway ANCOVA)

Step 4 in the lecture's workflow — check the covariate × factor interaction first (homogeneity-of-regression-slopes test), before fitting/interpreting the "clean" ANCOVA model. Use Type III sums of squares (set contrasts appropriately) to match SPSS's default GLM output.

hor_model <- lm(score ~ methods * motivation, data = dat)
car::Anova(hor_model, type = 3)

On the course dataset this returns a significant methods:motivation interaction, F(2,12) = 10.77, p = .002 — i.e. the regression slope of score-on-motivation differs across the three teaching methods, so the homogeneity of regression assumption is violated (matches the course lecture slide's "P<0.05: Significant interaction between factor and covariate → Violation of homogeneity of regression (HOR) slope").

For contrast, fit the standard (no-interaction) ANCOVA model — this is the model one would report only if the HOR assumption had held.

ancova_model <- lm(score ~ methods + motivation, data = dat)

5

Remedy (a) — pairwise comparisons at selected points along the covariate. When HOR is violated, adjusted means are re-computed ("simple slopes"/floodlight analysis) at representative values of the covariate (e.g. Mean, Mean − 1SD, Mean + 1SD), rather than at a single overall mean. Using emmeans::emmeans() with the at = argument reproduces the SPSS syntax approach shown in the course notes.

# Motivation Mean = 5.28, SD = 2.14 (from the course dataset)
low  <- 5.28 - 2.14   # 3.14
mid  <- 5.28           # 5.28 (SPSS default = grand mean)
high <- 5.28 + 2.14    # 7.42

emmeans(hor_model, ~ methods | motivation,
        at = list(motivation = c(low, mid, high)))

pairs(emmeans(hor_model, ~ methods | motivation,
              at = list(motivation = c(low, mid, high))),
      adjust = "bonferroni")

This produces three sets of adjusted means and Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons — one each for Low, Mid, and High motivation — mirroring the three re-run SPSS UNIANOVA ... EMMEANS ... WITH(motivation = 3.14 / MEAN / 7.42) syntaxes given in the lecture (Course lecture slides, Multiway ANCOVA).

Interpreting a significant interaction (stratified)

General principle (same logic as factorial ANOVA): whenever an interaction term is statistically significant — whether it is a factor×factor interaction in a genuine factorial ANCOVA, or the covariate×factor interaction that signals HOR violation — the corresponding main effect(s) must not be interpreted in isolation. Instead, effects should be interpreted stratified: by level of the other factor (simple main effects) for a factor×factor interaction, or by selected value of the covariate (simple slopes/pairwise comparisons at fixed covariate values) for a covariate×factor interaction.

Worked illustration 1 — covariate × factor interaction (HOR violation), course data

In ANCOVA_model1.sav, the methods × motivation interaction was significant, F(2,12) = 10.77, p = .002. This means the effect of teaching method on performance score is not constant across levels of motivation — the three regression lines (one per method) are not parallel. Consequently:

  • A single "adjusted mean at the grand-mean motivation" is misleading if the group ranking or magnitude of difference changes across the motivation range.
  • Following remedy (a): pairwise comparisons of the three methods should be reported separately at Low (3.14), Mid (5.28), and High (7.42) motivation, allowing the reader to see, for instance, whether Lecture out-performs PBL only among highly-motivated students, or across the whole range.
  • This is analogous to reporting simple main effects in a significant factorial ANOVA interaction — one does not simply report "Method has an overall effect," but rather "Method's effect depends on motivation level, as follows…".

Worked illustration 2 — factor × factor interaction, published re-analysis (Samuel et al. 2010)

Where the source material includes a genuine multi-factor design, the same stratified logic applies to the factor×factor interaction. Shiflett & Schwartz's ANCOVA re-analysis of a 2×2 factorial trial of acupuncture and amitriptyline for HIV-related neuropathic pain (originally reported by Shlay et al., 1998) is instructive (Samuel et al 2010):

  • Using repeated-measures ANCOVA with baseline pain intensity as covariate, the acupuncture × amitriptyline interaction on pain intensity over time was significant, F(1,86) = 5.91, p = .017.
  • Because this factor×factor interaction was significant, the authors correctly did not report "acupuncture reduces pain" or "amitriptyline reduces pain" as blanket main-effect conclusions. Instead they interpreted the effect of each treatment stratified by the presence/absence of the other: acupuncture alone (without amitriptyline) produced a steady decline in pain through week 14; amitriptyline alone was effective early (week 6) but its benefit reversed by week 14; and the combination of acupuncture + amitriptyline showed a pain trajectory nearly identical to full placebo — i.e., combining the two treatments cancelled out the benefit each showed alone.
  • The same stratified pattern recurred for global pain relief (interaction F = 4.32, p = .04): acupuncture alone was associated with high pain relief, but when combined with amitriptyline, relief was among the lowest of the four groups — a post-hoc contrast of "acupuncture alone vs acupuncture+amitriptyline" was significant (p = .05), confirming this adverse interaction.
  • For mortality, effects "involving acupuncture tended to be magnified in patients in poor [baseline] health," with the combination of acupuncture and amitriptyline associated with a 53% two-year death rate versus 11% for acupuncture alone in the poor-health stratum — again reported stratified by baseline health status rather than as an unconditional main effect (Samuel et al 2010).
Take-home for exam answers: when asked to interpret a significant interaction in ANCOVA (of either kind), always (i) state that the interaction is significant and name the F, df, p; (ii) explicitly say that main effects cannot be interpreted in isolation; and (iii) present stratified/simple-effect estimates (adjusted means or contrasts within levels of the moderating factor/covariate value), as illustrated above.

Worked illustration 3 — no interaction, straightforward adjusted-mean comparison

By contrast, in the Avoidance dataset (Avoidance_Model 2.sav), the Group×Pre_Avoid interaction was not significant, F(2,27) = 0.67, p = .52, so HOR was tenable and the additive ANCOVA could be interpreted directly without stratification: Group had a significant adjusted effect on post-treatment Avoidance, F(2,29) = 8.60, p = .001, with adjusted means (at Pre_Avoid = 106.67) of BH ≈ 119.3, Control ≈ 108.3, and BH+CR ≈ 127.4, i.e., the combination behavioural-rehearsal-plus-cognitive-restructuring condition had the highest adjusted avoidance score after controlling for baseline avoidance (illustrative interpretation, computed from the course dataset; direction and clinical meaning of "higher avoidance = worse outcome" should be confirmed against the original scale definition in Avoidance data_Model 2).

3. Repeated Measures ANOVA

Repeated Measures ANOVA (RM ANOVA) extends the paired t-test to >2 related (within-subject) measurements, and — when combined with a between-subject grouping factor — becomes a mixed-design (split-plot) ANOVA capable of testing time, group, and time×group interaction effects.

Concept

3.1 What counts as a "repeated measures" design?

RM ANOVA is used when the same subjects are measured on the same continuous outcome under multiple conditions or time points — i.e. a within-subjects design (Course lecture slides, RM ANOVA Part 1). It is indicated whenever:

  • a measure is repeated over time (e.g. baseline, post-intervention, follow-up);
  • a measure is repeated across more than one condition (e.g. treatment A vs treatment B given to the same participants); or
  • data are matched/paired in some other structured way.
Why bother with a within-subject design? Because each participant acts as their own control, between-subject variability is removed from the error term, which increases statistical power to detect a real change — at the cost of possible carry-over, learning, or latency effects that must be designed around (e.g. wash-out periods, randomised task order) (Course lecture slides, RM ANOVA Part 1).
AdvantageDisadvantage
Fewer participants needed (economical, more ethical for scarce/vulnerable samples)Listwise deletion in SPSS — a participant missing even one time point is dropped entirely from the analysis
Removes between-subject variability → more powerful test of within-subject changeLearning, carry-over and latency (order) effects can confound the "true" repeated-measures effect
Natural fit for longitudinal/prospective study questionsSphericity assumption is easily violated and must be actively checked/corrected

3.2 Hypotheses (one-way RM ANOVA)

For a single within-subject factor with k related levels:

H0: μ1 = μ2 = μ3 = ... = μk (all related population means are equal)
HA: at least two of the related means differ (Course lecture slides, RM ANOVA Part 1).

For the mixed (within + between) design, two further assumptions are checked: homogeneity of variance (Levene's test) at each time point, and homogeneity of covariance matrices between groups (Box's test) (Course lecture slides, RM ANOVA Part 2).

3.4 Sphericity and Mauchly's test

Sphericity is the within-subject analogue of homogeneity of variance: it requires that the variances of the pairwise differences between all combinations of the repeated levels are equal (H0: σ2diff(1,2) = σ2diff(1,3) = σ2diff(2,3) ...). Violating it inflates the F-statistic and Type I error, so the ordinary "sphericity assumed" F-test can no longer be trusted (Course lecture slides, RM ANOVA Part 1).

Mauchly's Test of Sphericity (SPSS output) only applies with ≥3 repeated levels and can be under-powered in small samples.

  • If Mauchly's p > 0.05 → sphericity is met → read the "Sphericity Assumed" row of the within-subjects effects table.
  • If Mauchly's p ≤ 0.05 → sphericity is violated → apply an epsilon (ε) correction to the degrees of freedom, or switch to the multivariate approach.
Epsilon (ε) valueCorrection to use
ε < 0.75Greenhouse–Geisser (G-G)
ε > 0.75Huynh–Feldt (H-F)
Lower-boundIgnore — overly conservative

Alternatively, the multivariate approach (which does not assume sphericity at all) can be read directly, usually via Wilks' Lambda — the other multivariate statistics (Pillai's Trace, Hotelling's Trace, Roy's Largest Root) typically agree in conclusion (Course lecture slides, RM ANOVA Part 1).

3

get_anova_table(res.aov, correction = "auto")   # auto-picks GG or HF by epsilon

All three pairwise comparisons (Pre-Post, Pre-Follow-up, Post-Follow-up) were significant after Bonferroni adjustment, consistent with the slide's reported result (Course lecture slides, RM ANOVA Part 1).

Interpreting a significant interaction (stratified)

Rule: whenever the time×group (or factor A × factor B) interaction is statistically significant, the corresponding main effects must not be interpreted in isolation — the effect of one factor genuinely differs across levels of the other, so interpretation must be stratified (simple main effects), and any single "average" main-effect statement would be misleading (Course lecture slides, RM ANOVA Part 2, "If significant interaction").

Worked example: alcohol × sleep-deprivation on test score (Model 3, real data)

Recomputed from Model 3_two-way RM ANOVA.sav (n = 8, cognitive test score as DV): the alcohol×sleep interaction was significant, F(1,7) = 7.43, p = .030. Descriptive means (SD):

No AlcoholAlcohol
Normal Sleep19.9 (3.9)18.4 (3.4)
Sleep Deprived17.4 (3.0)10.5 (5.7)

Because the interaction was significant, the alcohol main effect (F(1,7)=5.14, p=.058) and sleep main effect (F(1,7)=8.71, p=.021) were not interpreted as averaged, overall effects; instead, simple main effects were examined by stratifying one factor across levels of the other:

  • Effect of alcohol at Normal Sleep: 19.9 vs 18.4, paired-t p = .460 — not significant; alcohol alone barely affected test scores when sleep was adequate.
  • Effect of alcohol at Sleep Deprivation: 17.4 vs 10.5, paired-t p = .019 — significant; alcohol substantially worsened test performance specifically when sleep-deprived.
  • Effect of sleep deprivation with No Alcohol: 19.9 vs 17.4, paired-t p = .081 — borderline/not significant.
  • Effect of sleep deprivation with Alcohol: 18.4 vs 10.5, paired-t p = .018 — significant; sleep deprivation compounded the impairing effect of alcohol.

Interpretation: alcohol and sleep deprivation act synergistically rather than independently on cognitive test performance — the detrimental effect of alcohol is confined to (and amplified in) the sleep-deprived condition, and vice versa. A statement such as "alcohol reduces test scores" or "sleep deprivation reduces test scores" on its own, ignoring the interaction, would misrepresent the data; the correct, stratified conclusion is that the combination of the two exposures — not either exposure alone — drives the impairment (author's calculation from the classroom-exercise dataset accompanying Course lecture slides, RM ANOVA Part 2).

Contrast: non-significant interaction (Model 2, gender × time)

In the mixed-design example, gender×time interaction was not significant (Huynh-Feldt F(1.5,42.7) = 0.051, p = .911), and the profile plot showed roughly parallel lines for males and females. In this situation the main effects can be interpreted directly and independently: there was a significant overall within-subject (time) effect (confidence rose steadily across the three occasions for both sexes combined), while the between-subject (gender) effect was not significant — males and females did not differ overall in mean confidence, and this conclusion applies uniformly across time points because there was no interaction to qualify it (Course lecture slides, RM ANOVA Part 2).

Reporting checklist for a significant interaction: (1) report the omnibus interaction F, df (with sphericity correction if applicable) and p; (2) report and interpret simple main effects, stratified appropriately; (3) apply a multiple-comparison correction (e.g. Bonferroni) within each stratified comparison; (4) present a profile plot to visualise the non-parallel pattern; (5) explicitly state that overall/marginal main effects are not being interpreted because of the interaction.

4. Statistical Computation

Reproducible research workflow, computational tools, and skills for transparent, verifiable statistical analysis in epidemiology and public health.

Reproducible research workflow

Two related but distinct concepts
  • Reproducibility — the ability to re-compute the same results from the same data using the same code/steps (Course lecture slides, Monash Reproducible Report).
  • Replicability — the chance that other, independent experimenters/researchers will obtain a consistent result when they repeat the study (Course lecture slides, Monash Reproducible Report; Workflow, Tools and Skills slides).

If a result is reproducible, the same output can be recreated by following a specific, documented set of steps applied to a consistent dataset (Course lecture slides, Monash Reproducible Report). Over recent decades, science generally — and, by extension, health and medical research — has faced what has been termed a "replication crisis," a recurring difficulty in reproducing or replicating previously published findings (Course lecture slides, Monash Reproducible Report, citing kdnuggets.com, 2019). The course materials note, illustratively, that of 49 highly-cited articles examined in one commonly cited analysis, roughly 16% were later contradicted and a further 16% showed a smaller effect size in subsequent studies (Course lecture slides, Workflow Tools Skills); this figure should be treated as an illustrative motivating statistic from the slide deck rather than a claim independently verified here.

Why this matters for a DrPH researcher

Many journals now require data availability as a condition of publication, and reviewers, collaborators, and future researchers (including your future self) may need to re-run an analysis exactly. Non-reproducible workflows raise doubts about the quality and trustworthiness of the underlying analysis (Course lecture slides, Workflow Tools Skills).

What "makes reproducibility work"

The lecture material frames reproducibility as something the analyst can control directly, whereas replicability partly depends on others. Practical guidance offered includes (Course lecture slides, Monash Reproducible Report):

  • The only thing you can truly guarantee is that your own work is reproducible.
  • Use a consistent, repeatable method for every step of the analysis.
  • Documentation of your process is critical — not optional.
  • Keep every version of everything: workflows and data alike.

Motivations for reproducibility ("why bother")

The Workflow, Tools and Skills slides list five practical benefits of adopting a reproducible workflow, which are useful to cite when justifying reproducible practice to supervisors or ethics committees:

  1. Reproducibility helps avoid disaster (e.g., losing track of how a reported result was actually generated).
  2. It makes it easier to write papers (the analysis and the manuscript can be assembled together).
  3. It helps reviewers "see it your way" — they can follow and check the analytic logic.
  4. It enables continuity of your own work over time (e.g., returning to a project months later, or a labmate/student continuing it).
  5. It helps build your professional reputation as a rigorous analyst.

(Course lecture slides, Workflow Tools Skills)

Literate programming and Markdown

The conceptual foundation for reproducible reporting is literate programming (LP), a term originating from Donald Knuth's work. LP tools generate two representations from a single literate source file: one representation is "tangled" — suitable for compilation/execution by the computer (the code) — and the other is "woven" — formatted, human-readable documentation (Course lecture slides, Monash Reproducible Report, citing Knuth, doi:10.1093/comjnl/27.2.97). In other words, code and narrative explanation live in the same document rather than being maintained separately.

Markdown is described as a lightweight, plain-text markup language that can be converted into HTML and many other output formats; besides analysis reports, it is commonly used for README files, online discussion-forum posts, and general rich-text authoring from a plain text editor (Course lecture slides, Monash Reproducible Report).

5. Linear Regression

Modelling a continuous, approximately Gaussian outcome as a function of one or more predictors — simple and multiple linear regression, assumption checking, and the interpretation of interaction terms (continuous×categorical and continuous×continuous).

Concept

Why linear regression?

Linear regression is used when the analyst wants to predict the mean of a continuous/numerical outcome as a function of one covariate (simple linear regression, SLR) or several covariates (multiple linear regression, MLR). The outcome is assumed to be normally distributed conditional on the covariate value(s) — it is the conditional distribution of Y given X that must be approximately Gaussian, not the marginal distribution of Y (course lecture slides, Linear_Reg_Lect_2023).

Simple vs. multiple linear regression
  • Simple linear regression: one outcome (numerical, assumed Gaussian given the covariate) + one predictor (numerical or categorical).
  • Multiple linear regression: one outcome + two or more predictors, which may be all numerical, all categorical, or a mix.

Independent variables are also called predictors, regressors, explanatory variables, or covariates; the outcome is also called the dependent variable or regressand (course lecture slides).

The model equation

The general (first-order) multiple linear regression model is written as

Yi = β0 + β1X1i + β2X2i + … + βkXki + εi,   εi ~ N(0, σ2), independent

β0 is the intercept (the mean response when all covariates are 0); each βk is a partial regression coefficient — the effect of a one-unit increase in Xk on the mean of Y, holding all other covariates in the model constant. Parameters are estimated by ordinary least squares (minimising the sum of squared residuals) or, less commonly in this course, maximum likelihood; both give unbiased, minimum-variance, consistent, sufficient estimators under the model assumptions (course lecture slides).

Categorical covariates enter the equation as dummy (indicator) variables. For example, with sex coded X2 = 1 (male) / 0 (female) in the model Yi = β0 + β1AGEi + β2SEXi + εi, the fitted line for females is Ŷ = β̂0 + β̂1AGE, and for males it is Ŷ = (β̂0 + β̂2) + β̂1AGE — i.e. a parallel shift in intercept, same slope (course lecture slides).

Model assumptions

Assumptions (apply to both SLR and MLR)
  1. Linearity — the mean of Y is a linear function of the X's.
  2. Independence — the Y values (and hence residuals) are independent of one another.
  3. Homoscedasticity (equal variance) — the variance of the sub-populations of Y (equivalently, of the residuals) is constant across the range of fitted values.
  4. Normality of residuals — for each combination of X values, Y (and hence the residual) is normally distributed. This is required to construct confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.
  5. The X's are treated as fixed (non-random); inference applies only to the range of X observed.

Where predictors are numerous and inter-correlated, multicollinearity is an additional practical concern (not a distributional assumption, but a modelling problem) — see below (course lecture slides).

Multicollinearity

Multicollinearity exists when predictor variables are highly correlated with one another. When this happens, "the regression coefficient of any one variable depends on which other predictor variables are included in the model and which ones are left out" — i.e. a coefficient no longer reflects an isolated effect of that predictor, only its partial/marginal effect given the other correlated predictors retained in the model (course lecture slides). The lecture material illustrates this with systolic and diastolic blood pressure, whose pairwise Pearson correlation was r ≈ 0.685 — high enough to complicate interpretation of either coefficient once both SBP and DBP are entered together (course lecture slides, Linear_Reg_Lect_2023).

The course practical notes use a full pairwise correlation matrix (via cor() and corrplot()) across the numerical covariates and flag any |r| > 0.7 as a candidate multicollinearity problem; in the worked dataset, weight, waist and hip were mutually correlated (r up to 0.87 with derived BMI) and therefore risky to enter together (course practical notes, Linear_Regression_Practicals2023). A conservative rule of thumb given in the lecture is to drop or combine variables correlated above 0.7 (course lecture slides).

Variance Inflation Factor (VIF): the correlation-matrix approach only catches pairwise collinearity; when three or more predictors are jointly collinear, the standard complementary diagnostic is the VIF for each predictor (VIF = 1/(1−R2k), where R2k is from regressing Xk on all other predictors), obtained in R with car::vif(). VIF > 5–10 (equivalently tolerance < 0.1–0.2) is conventionally taken to indicate problematic collinearity. This tool was not demonstrated explicitly in the reviewed course files, which instead relied on the pairwise correlation matrix; it is included here as the standard extension students should also be able to apply, and its use is illustrated in the R walkthrough below.

Model comparison, R², and the overall F test

Total variation in Y decomposes as SST = SSR + SSE (total = explained + unexplained sums of squares), giving the coefficient of determination R2 = SSR/SST = 1 − SSE/SST, bounded 0 ≤ R2 ≤ 1. Because R2 never decreases when a predictor is added — even a useless one — the adjusted R² (which penalises for degrees of freedom used) should be used to compare models with different numbers of predictors (course lecture slides).

Nested models can be compared formally with anova() in R (H0: the simpler and more complex model fit equally well; a significant p-value, conventionally < 0.05, favours the more complex model). The overall F-test for a fitted model tests H0: β1 = β2 = … = βp−1 = 0 using F* = MSR/MSE (course lecture slides).

Confounders, mediators, and moderators

Confounders and mediators are both covariates whose inclusion changes another covariate's coefficient, but they differ conceptually: a confounder is a common cause of (or associated with) the exposure and the outcome; a mediator lies on the causal pathway between exposure and outcome (a consequence of the exposure). Whether a variable is treated as a confounder or a mediator is a subject-matter judgement, not something statistics alone can decide (course lecture slides). A moderator (effect modifier) is different again: it is a variable whose level changes the strength or direction of another predictor's effect on the outcome — this is captured statistically by an interaction term, discussed below.

The lecture illustrates confounding/mediation logic using systolic BP (SBP) and diastolic BP (DBP) as covariates for hba1c: fitted alone, SBP's coefficient was 0.00853 and DBP's was 0.01562; fitted together, SBP's coefficient fell to 0.00556 and DBP's fell to 0.00827 — both attenuated once the other, correlated BP measure was added, consistent with the multicollinearity noted above (course lecture slides).

Interaction between covariates

When the effect of one predictor on Y depends on the level of another predictor, the model is non-additive; the usual "one-unit-increase-in-X increases Y by β units" interpretation breaks down for the predictors involved in the interaction. This is modelled by adding a product term:

Yi = β0 + β1X1i + β2X2i + β3(X1iX2i) + εi

The course lecture works through a numeric illustration: for E{Y} = 10 + 2X1 + 5X2 + 0.5X1X2, the slope on X1 is 2.5 when X2 = 1, but rises to 3.5 when X2 = 3 — the slope of X1 is itself a function of X2 once the interaction term is non-zero (course lecture slides). Interactions can involve two numerical covariates, one numerical and one categorical covariate, or two categorical covariates; illustrative fitted coefficients for each type appear in the lecture (e.g. fbs×mogtt2h numerical×numerical interaction, coefficient 0.0117, p<0.001; fbs×receptor-status numerical×categorical interaction, coefficient −0.202, p<0.001) (course lecture slides).

SPSS walkthrough

The reviewed course files did not include an SPSS-specific demonstration for this topic; the steps below follow the standard SPSS Statistics menu workflow for linear regression and interaction terms and are provided to satisfy the dual-software requirement of this compilation, using the same variables/concepts as the R materials above.

Step 1 — Simple/multiple linear regression.

  1. Analyze > Regression > Linear…
  2. Move the continuous outcome (e.g. hba1c) into Dependent.
  3. Move the predictor(s) (e.g. ldl, and gender recoded 0/1) into Independent(s). For a simple linear regression, enter one predictor only; for multiple regression, enter all predictors at once (Method = Enter) unless a stepwise procedure is specifically intended.
  4. Click Statistics… and tick Estimates, Confidence intervals, Model fit, R squared change, and — for multicollinearity — Collinearity diagnostics (this produces Tolerance and VIF for each predictor).
  5. Click Plots… and request *ZRESID (standardised residuals) vs. *ZPRED (standardised predicted values) to assess linearity/homoscedasticity, and tick Histogram and Normal probability plot for the residuals to assess normality.
  6. Click Save… and tick Standardized residuals, Cook's distance, and Leverage values to append diagnostic columns to the dataset for outlier/influence screening.
  7. Click OK. Read the coefficients from the Coefficients table (B, SE, t, Sig., 95% CI), overall fit from Model Summary (R, R², Adjusted R²) and the ANOVA table (overall F-test).

Step 2 — Building an interaction term (continuous×categorical or continuous×continuous).

Option A — Compute Variable (works in all SPSS versions):

  1. (Recommended) First mean-centre each continuous predictor to reduce non-essential (structural) collinearity between the main-effect and product terms: Transform > Compute Variable…, Target variable e.g. stress_c, Numeric expression stress - MEAN(stress) (or subtract a value obtained beforehand from Analyze > Descriptive Statistics).
  2. Transform > Compute Variable… again. Target variable e.g. stress_sex (or stress_loneliness). Numeric expression: stress_c * sex_male (categorical variable must first be dummy-coded 0/1) or stress_c * loneliness_c for a continuous×continuous product term.
  3. Return to Analyze > Regression > Linear… and enter the main-effect terms and the computed product term together as Independents (e.g. stress_c, sex_male, stress_sex).

Option B — built-in Interactions panel (SPSS 27+):

  1. In the Linear Regression dialog, select the two (or more) variables intended to interact in the source list.
  2. Click the >a*b> "Build Interaction" button (or use the Factors and Covariates/Interactions panel present in recent SPSS builds) to add the product term automatically to the Independent(s) list, alongside the two main-effect terms.

Either way, always keep the corresponding lower-order main-effect terms in the model together with the product term (do not enter an interaction term without its constituent main effects).

Step 3 — Reading and testing the interaction. In the Coefficients table, examine the Sig. value for the product term. If p < 0.05, the interaction is statistically significant and the model should not be interpreted using the main-effect coefficients alone (see Interpretation section). If not significant, drop the product term and re-fit the additive model, reporting a single overall slope for the continuous predictor, as illustrated in the R ldl×gender example above (p = 0.888, NS).

Step 4 — Diagnostics in SPSS.

  • Homoscedasticity/linearity: inspect the *ZRESID vs *ZPRED scatterplot requested in Step 1 for funnelling or curvature.
  • Normality of residuals: inspect the saved residual histogram/P-P plot; formally test with Analyze > Descriptive Statistics > Explore on the saved unstandardized residual (Shapiro–Wilk / Kolmogorov–Smirnov).
  • Multicollinearity: read Tolerance/VIF from the Collinearity Diagnostics output requested in Step 1 (Tolerance < 0.1–0.2, equivalently VIF > 5–10, flags a problem).
  • Influence: sort/filter cases on the saved Cook's Distance and Leverage columns (Step 1) to identify influential observations for further scrutiny.

Interpreting a significant interaction (stratified)

General rule: once an interaction term is statistically significant, the corresponding main-effect coefficient(s) no longer represent a meaningful "overall" slope and must not be interpreted in isolation. The predictor's effect must instead be reported conditional on the level (categorical case) or value (continuous case) of the moderating variable.

6. Logistic Regression

Modelling a binary (dichotomous) outcome via the logit link — odds ratios, model building, Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit, classification accuracy, and regression diagnostics (leverage, Cook's distance, DFBETA) for logistic models.

Concept

Why logistic regression?

Logistic regression is used when the outcome is dichotomous (e.g. diabetes yes/no) rather than continuous. Unlike linear regression, a logistic model differs in its outcome variable, its choice of parametric distribution (Bernoulli/Binomial rather than Gaussian), and its assumptions (course lecture slides). With a binary outcome, the conditional mean E(Y=1|x) must stay between 0 and 1, so a plain linear predictor cannot be used directly — the logistic (sigmoid) function keeps predicted probabilities bounded (course lecture slides).

The logistic model and its logit transformation

π(x) = E(Y=1|x) = exp(β0+β1x1+…+βkxk) / [1 + exp(β0+β1x1+…+βkxk)]

Transforming π(x) with the logit (log-odds) link linearises the model in its parameters:

g(x) = ln[π(x) / (1−π(x))] = β0 + β1x1 + … + βkxk

The logit is linear in its parameters, may be continuous, and can range from −∞ to +∞, unlike the bounded probability scale (course lecture slides).

Assumptions

  1. Independence of errors/observations
  2. Correctly specified model (right covariates included)
  3. Correct functional form (linearity in the logit for numerical covariates)
  4. Absence of multicollinearity
  5. Covariates measured with negligible error (fixed, not random)

(course lecture slides)

Estimation: maximum likelihood, not least squares

Parameters β0, β1, …, βk are estimated by maximum likelihood (ML), not ordinary least squares — the ML estimate is the parameter value that maximises the likelihood of the observed data; in practice the log-likelihood is maximised (course lecture slides).

Worked example: simple logistic regression (age only)

Three ways to test coefficient significance

  • Likelihood ratio (LR) test — compares −2×log-likelihood between a reduced (M0) and full (M1) model: G = D(M0) − D(M1). Worked example: log-likelihood with constant only = −68.331; with constant + age = −53.677; G = −2[−68.331−(−53.677)] = 29.31 (course lecture slides).
  • Wald test — W = β̂p/SE(β̂p), approximately standard normal under H0: βp=0; less preferred than the LR test (course lecture slides).
  • Score test — a third, less commonly reported alternative (course lecture slides).

Model building: confounder/mediator screening and multicollinearity

In the practicals, univariable models were fitted for each numerical covariate (age, msbpr, mdbpr, fbs, mogtt2h, hdl) against a diabetes outcome derived from the diabetes.dta dataset, then compared with a full multivariable model containing all six. Because systolic and diastolic blood pressure (msbpr, mdbpr) are highly correlated (Pearson r = 0.706 in the correlation matrix), including both destabilised their coefficients: age and msbpr both lost significance in the full six-covariate model, whereas a reduced model with msbpr, fbs and hdl retained clean, significant coefficients for all three — illustrating why a full pairwise/`corrplot()` collinearity screen should precede final model selection (course practical notes). The course materials stress that covariate selection should be systematic and theory-driven, not p-value-driven, and that confounders/mediators must remain in the model whenever theoretically justified, else the model is biased (course lecture slides).

Goodness of fit: Hosmer–Lemeshow, deviance, and Pearson χ²

Deviance and Pearson χ² tests are not recommended as overall goodness-of-fit tests for logistic regression with continuous covariates (too many small/sparse covariate patterns); the Hosmer–Lemeshow (HL) test is preferred (course lecture slides).

The HL test groups fitted probabilities into (conventionally) 10 deciles, then compares observed vs. expected frequencies in a g×2 table via a Pearson χ² statistic; a p > 0.05 supports adequate fit (course lecture slides). Applied to the practicals' final model (diabetes ~ age + msbpr + hdl + crural, n = 3,820), generalhoslem::logitgof() gave X² = 18.701, df = 8, p = 0.0165 — below 0.05, indicating some evidence of lack of fit that would warrant further model refinement (course practical notes). Note that a second HL implementation reported in the same practicals, ResourceSelection::hoslem.test(), returned an evidently anomalous result (X² = 3,820, accompanied by an "Ops.factor" warning), most likely reflecting a factor-vs-numeric coding mismatch in the outcome argument rather than a genuine model problem; this is a useful cautionary example of checking that HL implementations receive the outcome in the expected numeric 0/1 form rather than trusting output uncritically (course practical notes).

Classification table and ROC/AUC

Two further overall-fit summaries are the correct classification table (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity at a chosen probability cut-off, conventionally 0.5) and the area under the ROC curve, which is described as a more robust measure of discrimination than the classification table alone (course lecture slides). Rule-of-thumb AUC benchmarks given in the lecture: AUC = 0.5 no discrimination; 0.7–0.8 acceptable; 0.8–0.9 excellent; >0.9 outstanding (rare) (course lecture slides).

Regression diagnostics: leverage, residuals, and influence

The dedicated diagnostics reading (Sarkar, Midi & Rana 2011) distinguishes three related but distinct concepts in logistic regression diagnostics:

  • Outlier — an observation with an unusual response value conditional on its covariate pattern (large residual).
  • Leverage — an observation with an extreme covariate profile (far from the mean in covariate space), quantified by the diagonal of the "hat" matrix, hi (the Pregibon leverage), which measures how much that covariate pattern drives the fit of the model.
  • Influence — conceptually the product of leverage and outlyingness; an observation is influential if removing it substantially changes the estimated coefficients.

Interpreting a significant interaction (stratified)

General rule: once a categorical×categorical (or continuous×categorical) interaction term is statistically significant, the constituent main-effect odds ratios cannot be interpreted alone as "the" effect of that predictor — the odds ratio must instead be reported separately within each level of the interacting covariate.

Reporting a single pooled "male vs. female OR" or a single pooled "rural vs. urban OR" from a model that omits the interaction term would therefore be actively misleading for this dataset (course practical notes). As a hedge appropriate to a DrPH audience: the point estimates above are derived directly from the fitted coefficients; a rigorous report would additionally compute stratum-specific 95% confidence intervals for each conditional OR via the delta method or a linear-combination/contrast procedure (e.g. emmeans in R, or the Contrast Coefficients matrix / re-parameterised model in SPSS) rather than relying on the marginal SEs of the individual terms, since the variance of a sum of coefficients also involves their covariance.

Contrast: a non-significant interaction from the same dataset

By contrast, the age×gender interaction fitted on the same dataset (dmdx ~ age + gender + age:gender, and again as diabetes ~ age + gender + age:gender) was not significant (p = 0.279 and p = 0.479 respectively across the two outcome codings) (course practical notes). 7. Survival Analysis

Time-to-event methods — censoring, the Kaplan–Meier estimator, the log-rank test, and the Cox proportional hazards model — illustrated throughout with the course's own stroke-outcome cohort (n = 262, 63 deaths).

Concept

Why a dedicated set of methods for time-to-event data?

Survival analysis (time-to-event analysis) models the time until a defined event occurs — classically death, but equally relapse, recurrence, discharge, or any other well-defined endpoint (course lecture slides). The outcome variable of interest is therefore time until an event, measured in any convenient unit (days, weeks, months); ordinary linear regression or ANOVA cannot be used directly because survival data are typically right-skewed, non-normal, and — critically — usually incomplete for at least some subjects (course lecture slides).

A valid survival dataset requires, at minimum: (1) the duration from a defined starting point (e.g. date of admission) to the point at which the event occurs or follow-up ends, and (2) an event indicator coding whether the event of interest was observed (course lecture slides). In the course's worked example, stroke_outcome.dta (n = 262 stroke patients), the time variable dur_days was derived from admission date (doa) and discharge/outcome date (dod), and the event variable event was coded "status at discharge: 1 = dead, 0 = censored" (course practical notes).

Censoring

An observation is censored when the exact survival time is not known — typically because the subject was still alive/event-free when the study ended, was lost to follow-up, or withdrew (course lecture slides). Three censoring assumptions underpin standard survival methods: censoring events occur (1) independently of one another, (2) at random, and (3) non-informatively, i.e. censoring itself carries no information about the subject's risk of the event (course lecture slides).

Of the four theoretical types — left truncation, right truncation, left censoring, and right censoring — the course focuses exclusively on right censoring, the most common and most easily interpreted form, where the only information available is that the true event time exceeds the last observed follow-up time (course lecture slides).

In the stroke cohort, 199 of 262 patients (76%) were censored (discharged alive) and 63 (24%) died during the observation window (course practical notes; stroke_outcome.dta).

Survivor function and hazard function

Two related functions describe survival data: the survivor function S(t) = P(T > t), the probability of surviving beyond time t; and the hazard function h(t) = f(t)/S(t), the instantaneous risk of the event at time t given survival up to that point (course lecture slides). Knowing one function allows the other to be derived. The hazard function is always non-negative and has no upper bound; because the hazard is unobserved directly, it is estimated from the data via models such as Cox regression (course lecture slides).

Kaplan–Meier estimation

The Kaplan–Meier (KM) or "product-limit" estimator is the most widely used non-parametric estimator of S(t); the Nelson–Aalen estimator is a related alternative (course lecture slides). Good practice is to inspect KM curves for every categorical predictor before fitting a multivariable model, to get a feel for the shape of survival in each group and whether the curves appear roughly parallel (an informal check of the proportional hazards assumption, discussed further below) (course lecture slides).

Fitting survfit(Surv(dur_days, event=='dead') ~ 1) to the full stroke cohort gives the overall KM survival table (selected rows) (course practical notes):

Comparing groups: the log-rank test

To compare survivor functions between groups of a categorical predictor, the course recommends: (1) plotting KM curves by group, (2) inspecting them for separation and parallelism, then (3) formally testing the difference with a non-parametric test built on a group × event contingency table at each observed failure time (course lecture slides). Several weighting schemes exist — log-rank (weight = 1), Wilcoxon (weight = number at risk), Tarone–Ware, Peto–Prentice, Flemington–Harrington — and the analyst should decide a priori which to use rather than choosing post hoc for a favourable p-value (course lecture slides). The log-rank test is the best-known and the default in most software; it is a chi-square test comparing observed vs. expected event counts across categories defined by ordered failure times (course lecture slides).

The Cox proportional hazards regression

The most widely used regression approach in medicine and epidemiology is the Cox proportional hazards (PH) model, a semi-parametric model because the baseline hazard h0(t) is left unspecified while the covariate effects are modelled parametrically (course lecture slides):

h(t, X) = h0(t) · exp(ΣβiXi)

The hazard ratio (HR) for a covariate is obtained by exponentiating its regression coefficient, HR̂ = exp(β̂); e.g. a coefficient of 1.29 gives HR̂ = exp(1.29) = 3.65 (course lecture slides). The 95% CI for the HR is exp(β̂ ± 1.96×SE(β̂)). Coefficient significance is typically assessed with the Wald (Z) statistic or the likelihood ratio test (course lecture slides).

Interpreting a significant interaction (stratified)

Clinically, this suggests the prognostic benefit of a higher admission GCS is attenuated in patients with pre-existing diabetes, though the wide confidence interval expected around the diabetic-stratum estimate (given only 101 diabetic patients and 63 total deaths) counsels caution. As an appropriate hedge for a DrPH audience: the 0.86 point estimate for the diabetic stratum is a linear combination of two fitted coefficients (gcs main effect + gcs:dm2 interaction); the printed coxph() summary reports the standard error and CI for each individual term but not for this specific linear combination, so a rigorous report would additionally extract the model's variance–covariance matrix (vcov(cox.gcs.dm.ia)) and apply the delta method (or refit with dm2 releveled so "yes" is the reference category, which directly reports the yes-stratum gcs coefficient and its CI) before quoting a precise interval for the diabetic-stratum hazard ratio (course practical notes; general Cox-regression methodology).

Equivalently, viewed from the diabetes side: because the interaction is significant, a single pooled "diabetes yes vs. no" hazard ratio (2.931 from the additive model cox.mv above) is not an adequate summary either — the diabetes-associated hazard itself depends on the patient's GCS level, and reporting only the pooled adjusted HR would obscure this effect-modification (course practical notes).

8. Statistical Modelling

How a "final model" is actually arrived at — purposeful variable selection, the pitfalls of automated stepwise procedures, and the rules of thumb for confounding and interaction — followed by the decades-long, still-unsettled debate over what a p-value can and cannot tell us.

Model building strategy

Why a "strategy" is needed at all

Once more than a handful of candidate predictors are in play, there is rarely a single obvious "correct" model; the analyst must have (i) a plan for selecting variables and (ii) a set of methods for checking the adequacy of whatever model results (Model building.pdf, citing Hosmer, Lemeshow & Sturdivant's Applied Logistic Regression). The methods discussed below are described in the source text as an aid to — never a substitute for — clear scientific thinking: "successful modeling of a complex data set is part science, part statistical methods, and part experience and common sense" (Model building.pdf).

Two competing philosophies

One school of thought favours the most parsimonious model that still fits the data adequately, on the reasoning that fewer variables give more numerically stable, more portable estimates. A second, more epidemiologically oriented school argues for entering all clinically or biologically relevant variables regardless of univariable "significance," because confounding that is negligible variable-by-variable can still be considerable in aggregate (Model building.pdf, citing Rothman et al. 2008; Maldonado & Greenland 1993; Greenland 1989; Miettinen 1976). The tension between these two positions — parsimony vs. completeness of confounder control — is not fully resolvable on statistical grounds alone and, in the source text's own framing, is a matter of judgement within the scientific context of the problem (Model building.pdf; course lecture slides).

Purposeful selection: a seven-step procedure

The course materials present "purposeful selection" (after Hosmer, Lemeshow & Sturdivant) as a structured, analyst-driven alternative to fully automated variable selection. In outline (Model building.pdf; course lecture slides):

  1. Univariable screening. Each candidate predictor is examined on its own (contingency-table chi-square/likelihood-ratio test for categorical variables; univariable regression or t-test for continuous variables). Any variable with a univariable p-value < 0.20–0.25, together with all variables considered clinically important regardless of their p-value, is carried forward — a deliberately lenient threshold, since a conventional 0.05 cut-off at this stage is documented to miss variables later shown to matter (Model building.pdf, citing Bendel & Afifi 1977; Mickey & Greenland 1989).
  2. Fit the full multivariable model containing everything retained at Step 1, then remove variables that are not significant by their Wald statistic, comparing the reduced model to the fuller one via a partial likelihood-ratio test.
  3. Check for confounding after removal (see the change-in-estimate rule below); any variable whose removal materially shifts the coefficients of the variables left in the model is added back.
  4. Re-offer excluded variables one at a time to the model resulting from Steps 2–3, since a variable that was not significant alone can still make an important contribution once other variables are controlled. The result is termed the preliminary main effects model.
  5. Check linearity in the logit for each continuous covariate (smoothed scatterplots, fractional polynomials, or spline functions), yielding the main effects model.
  6. Test biologically plausible interactions one at a time against the main effects model, retaining only those significant at conventional levels (5–10%), giving the preliminary final model.
  7. Assess overall model adequacy and fit (goodness-of-fit, discrimination, influence diagnostics) before the model is used for any inferential purpose.

A simulation study cited in the source text found that purposeful selection retained known confounders and truly significant covariates more reliably than mechanical stepwise selection (Model building.pdf, citing Bursac et al. 2008).

Stepwise, best-subsets, and other automated procedures

Automated alternatives — forward selection with a test for backward elimination, backward elimination with a test for forward re-entry, and best-subsets selection — select variables purely on statistical criteria, without the analyst reviewing the biological plausibility of the result at each step (Model building.pdf; course lecture slides). The course lecture slides are emphatic that stepwise selection is attractive mainly because it is convenient ("needs less subject matter knowledge," "allows us not to think about the problem") rather than because it is statistically sound (Statistical Modelling.pdf/Statistical Modelling2.pdf, course lecture slides).

Documented problems with stepwise selection

According to the course lecture slides, mechanical stepwise procedures (Statistical Modelling.pdf/Statistical Modelling2.pdf, course lecture slides):

  • yield R² values that are biased high;
  • give F and chi-square test statistics that do not follow their claimed reference distributions;
  • produce standard errors and confidence intervals that are too narrow, and predicted values that are overly precise;
  • yield p-values that are too small and regression coefficients that are biased high (away from the null);
  • in observational studies used for confounder adjustment, can leave residual confounding uncorrected;
  • are made effectively arbitrary in the presence of collinearity among predictors; and
  • can select "biologically implausible" or noise variables while narrowly missing others that are genuinely important (Model building.pdf, citing Greenland 1989; Flack & Chang 1987; Griffiths & Pope 1987).

The underlying concern in both sources is the same: mechanical selection removes the analyst's judgement from the process, so the resulting "best" model is never scrutinised for clinical sense before being reported (Model building.pdf; course lecture slides). A DrPH-level hedge is appropriate here: these are well-documented statistical objections, but stepwise methods remain very widely used in practice, and the size of the resulting bias depends on the specific dataset, sample size, and degree of collinearity — the criticisms describe a systematic tendency, not a fixed quantum of error in every application.

Assessing confounding: the change-in-estimate rule

Rather than relying on a "significant" p-value for a potential confounder, the course materials use a change-in-estimate criterion: after removing a covariate from the model, compare the coefficient(s) of the variables that remain against their values in the larger model. A shift conventionally set at >20% in the source materials (some other literature uses a stricter 10% threshold) is taken as evidence that the removed variable was providing meaningful confounding control, and it is added back (Model building.pdf; Statistical Modelling.pdf/Statistical Modelling2.pdf). This process of deleting, refitting, and comparing is repeated, removing only a few covariates at a time, until the remaining set appears both statistically defensible and clinically/scientifically coherent (Model building.pdf).

It is worth flagging, as a hedge for causal interpretation, that change-in-estimate rules are a practical heuristic for confounder identification within a given variable set, not a substitute for thinking through the underlying causal structure; a separate strand of methodological literature argues that whether a variable should be adjusted for at all depends on its role in the causal graph (e.g. common cause, mediator, or collider), since conditioning on mediators or colliders can move an estimate away from the true causal effect rather than towards it (Rohrer 2018, "Thinking Clearly About Correlations and Causation"). This is a complementary, not contradictory, perspective to the change-in-estimate approach described in the course materials.

Assessing interaction (effect modification) during model building

Once a main effects model is settled, plausible pairwise interactions (arithmetic products of the constituent main-effect terms) are added one at a time and tested for statistical significance, typically at the 5–10% level (Statistical Modelling.pdf/Statistical Modelling2.pdf; Model building.pdf). Two points of practice are stressed in the source materials:

  • Unlike main effects — where both statistical significance and confounding/adjustment are relevant — interaction terms are judged on statistical significance alone; a non-significant interaction typically inflates standard errors without materially changing the point estimates, and so is ordinarily dropped (Model building.pdf).
  • Any interaction retained in the model must also make sense from a clinical or biological perspective — statistical significance by itself is not treated as sufficient justification (Model building.pdf).

Once main effects are "locked" at this stage, only interaction terms are candidates for removal in the simplification cycle that follows (Model building.pdf).

Parsimony vs. completeness — and other modelling pitfalls

The course lecture slides list a broader set of "problems with statistical modelling" beyond variable selection per se: an unstable model-generation process; misspecification through omitted non-linearities, omitted interactions, or missing predictors; discontinuities introduced by artificially categorising continuous predictors; misspecified correlation structure among observations (leading to inefficient estimates and overconfident inference); overfitting (with unrealistically extreme or falsely significant associations); and predictions made outside the observed range of the predictors (Statistical Modelling.pdf/Statistical Modelling2.pdf). The slides explicitly caution against categorising naturally continuous variables, noting that categorical recoding is "subjective," discards statistical information, and does not, contrary to a common belief, reduce measurement error (Statistical Modelling.pdf/Statistical Modelling2.pdf).

In comparing two competing final models, the course lecture slides list calibration, discrimination, face validity, robustness to measurement error in the required predictors, appropriate use of continuous (rather than artificially categorised) predictors, simplicity, and any systematic lack of fit for particular subgroups as the relevant criteria — i.e., parsimony is one desirable property among several, not an overriding goal in itself (Statistical Modelling.pdf/Statistical Modelling2.pdf).

The p-value debate

Background: why the ASA felt a statement was necessary

In 2016 the American Statistical Association (ASA) took the unusual step of issuing a formal position statement on p-values — something it had previously done only for narrowly scoped policy matters (e.g. value-added models in education, election auditing), never for a core matter of everyday statistical practice (The ASA Statement on p Values Context Process and Purpose 2016.pdf). The impetus, recounted in the accompanying editorial, included a "reproducibility crisis" in which systematic replication attempts failed at markedly higher rates than expected, high-profile science-journalism pieces questioning the foundations of null-hypothesis significance testing, and at least one journal (Basic and Applied Social Psychology) banning p-values outright (The ASA Statement on p Values Context Process and Purpose 2016.pdf; The ASA p-value statement 10 years on 2026.pdf). The statement itself was developed over many months by a large group of statisticians convened by the ASA Board and was approved by the ASA Executive Committee on 29 January 2016 (The ASA Statement on p Values Context Process and Purpose 2016.pdf).

The statement was published as an editorial-plus-statement by Ronald L. Wasserstein (then ASA Executive Director) and Nicole A. Lazar in The American Statistician, accompanied by an online supplement containing commentaries from more than twenty other statisticians who did not necessarily agree with every point (The ASA Statement on p Values Context Process and Purpose 2016.pdf). As the authors themselves stress, "nothing in the ASA statement is new" — statisticians had been raising these concerns for decades; what was new was a formal, consensus statement from the world's largest professional association of statisticians (The ASA Statement on p Values Context Process and Purpose 2016.pdf).

What a p-value is

The statement defines a p-value, informally, as "the probability under a specified statistical model that a statistical summary of the data (e.g., the sample mean difference between two compared groups) would be equal to or more extreme than its observed value" (The ASA Statement on p Values Context Process and Purpose 2016.pdf). The smaller the p-value, the greater the incompatibility between the data and the null hypothesis, conditional on the underlying model assumptions being correct (The ASA Statement on p Values Context Process and Purpose 2016.pdf).

The ASA Statement's six principles (Wasserstein & Lazar, 2016)
  1. P-values can indicate how incompatible the data are with a specified statistical model.
  2. P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone.
  3. Scientific conclusions and business or policy decisions should not be based only on whether a p-value passes a specific threshold.
  4. Proper inference requires full reporting and transparency (of all analyses conducted, not only the ones that "worked").
  5. A p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result.
  6. By itself, a p-value does not provide a good measure of evidence regarding a model or hypothesis.

(The ASA Statement on p Values Context Process and Purpose 2016.pdf; summarised in course lecture slides, asa_pvalues.pdf.)

Common misinterpretations the statement is aimed at

Drawing directly on the six principles above, the statement (and the discussants published alongside it) target several persistent misreadings of the p-value (The ASA Statement on p Values Context Process and Purpose 2016.pdf):

  • Treating the p-value as "the probability that the null hypothesis is true" (Principle 2) — it is a statement about the data given the model, not about the model/hypothesis given the data.
  • Using a single bright-line threshold such as p < 0.05 as a mechanical, sufficient justification for a scientific claim (Principle 3); the statement warns this encourages "erroneous beliefs and poor decision making" and can distort the scientific process.
  • Reporting only the analyses, or only the p-values, that "worked," a practice the statement links to p-hacking, data dredging, and selective inference, which render the reported p-values "essentially uninterpretable" (Principle 4).
  • Equating statistical significance with the practical, clinical, or economic importance of an effect (Principle 5) — a tiny, unimportant effect can be "significant" with a large enough sample, and a large, important effect can fail to reach significance with a small or imprecise sample.
  • Treating a p-value near 0.05 as strong evidence against the null, or a large p-value as evidence for the null (Principle 6); the statement is explicit that "a p-value near 0.05 taken by itself offers only weak evidence against the null hypothesis."

An online discussant, Andrew Gelman, pushed this further in a widely cited commentary reproduced alongside the statement: he argued the deeper problem is not the p-value itself but the wider practice of null-hypothesis significance testing and the "garden of forking paths" — the many reasonable, theory-respecting analytic choices a researcher could have made had the data come out differently, which invalidate a p-value's interpretation even when the researcher engaged in no deliberate p-hacking (asa_pvalues.pdf, "The Problems With P-Values are not Just With P-Values," Andrew Gelman, online discussion supplement to the ASA statement, The American Statistician, 2016). This is a useful hedge for practice: full disclosure of the single analysis actually performed does not, by itself, guarantee a valid p-value if other defensible analytic choices existed and were not pre-registered.

The "ten years on" follow-up: what changed, what didn't

A decade-later retrospective published in Significance (the joint magazine of the Royal Statistical Society and the American Statistical Association) traces what happened after 2016 (The ASA p-value statement 10 years on 2026.pdf, Robert Matthews, "The ASA p-value statement 10 years on," Significance, 23(2), 2026). Key developments documented there include:

  • A 2017 international symposium in Bethesda, Maryland — nicknamed "the Woodstock of Inference" — which fed into a 2019 special issue of The American Statistician, "Statistical Inference in the 21st Century: A World Beyond p < 0.05," containing over 40 invited papers (The ASA p-value statement 10 years on 2026.pdf, citing Wasserstein, Schirm & Lazar 2019).
  • The accompanying 2019 editorial by Wasserstein, Schirm and Lazar went further than the 2016 statement, explicitly calling for researchers to "stop using the term 'statistically significant' entirely" and to move away from bright-line thresholds altogether (The ASA p-value statement 10 years on 2026.pdf).
  • A parallel proposal (Benjamin, Berger, Johannesson et al., 2018, Nature Human Behaviour) to simply tighten the conventional threshold to p < 0.005 for claims of a "new discovery," on the grounds that findings meeting this stricter criterion have since been shown to replicate roughly twice as often as those meeting only p < 0.05 (The ASA p-value statement 10 years on 2026.pdf). The article reports this remains, of all proposed reforms, the most widely cited and adopted in practice, even though critics regard the 0.005 threshold as just as arbitrary as 0.05 and warn it demands larger studies or accepts a higher risk of missing genuine effects.
  • A December 2019 US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine report on the replication crisis, which called for better training in the "proper use of statistical analysis" (The ASA p-value statement 10 years on 2026.pdf).
  • A further 2021 ASA Presidential Task Force statement, issued partly to clarify that the 2019 "abandon significance" editorial was not official ASA policy; the Task Force instead gave broader guidance on good statistical practice and reaffirmed that p-values and significance tests remain valuable "when properly applied and interpreted" (The ASA p-value statement 10 years on 2026.pdf).
Net assessment in the 2026 retrospective

The article's own framing is that change has been evolutionary rather than revolutionary: the ASA statement has been cited more than 8,000 times, journal editors and authors are described as "more mindful of good practice regarding p-values," and some medical journals have shifted toward reporting confidence intervals alongside or instead of bare p-values — but no consensus has emerged on abandoning significance testing altogether, and prominent statisticians quoted in the piece (e.g. Sir David Spiegelhalter) remain sceptical that eliminating significance tests is either feasible or, in their view, the most important issue (The ASA p-value statement 10 years on 2026.pdf).

Implications for practice (DrPH-level hedging)

  • A p-value, on its own, is not a measure of how "true," how important, or how replicable a finding is; it should always be reported and interpreted alongside effect estimates, confidence intervals, study design quality, and external evidence (The ASA Statement on p Values Context Process and Purpose 2016.pdf).
  • Neither the original 2016 statement nor the 2026 retrospective claims that p-values should never be used; the more defensible, moderate reading — consistent with the 2021 ASA Task Force position cited above — is that p-values remain a legitimate tool when their limitations are understood and other contextual evidence is brought to bear, not that they should be discarded wholesale (The ASA p-value statement 10 years on 2026.pdf).
  • Because the debate remains genuinely unsettled among statisticians a decade on, it is appropriate for a DrPH-level analysis to state findings with calibrated hedging (e.g. "the data are compatible with," "provide weak/moderate evidence against") rather than binary "significant/non-significant" language, and to disclose the full set of analyses conducted, in the spirit of Principle 4 above (The ASA Statement on p Values Context Process and Purpose 2016.pdf; asa_pvalues.pdf).
